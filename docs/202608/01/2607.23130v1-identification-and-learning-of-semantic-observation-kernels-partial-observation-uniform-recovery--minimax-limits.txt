# Identification and Learning of Semantic Observation Kernels: Partial Observation, Uniform Recovery, and Minimax Limits 

Matthew Dixon 

Artificial Intelligence Finance Institute (AIFI) 

matthew.dixon@aifi.edu 

Abstract 

Probabilistic text generators supply conditional distributions over tokens and complete verbal continuations, whereas scientific use often requires a posterior over a finite state. Large language models are the leading example: phrase probabilities depend on prompt wording, and model-printed percentages are generated text rather than state posteriors. More generally, we ask when an observable language law can support a reproducible posterior over declared states. A semantic map groups meaning-equivalent continuations; held-out cases with reference posteriors identify a semiparametric inverse from grouped language probabilities to state probabilities. The language law remains nonparametric and no hidden model quantity is used. This is principally a theory and methods paper and makes several contributions. On the theoretical side, we derive conditions for existence, identification, stable recovery, and sequential updating; concentration, asymptotic, and nonparametric rates; identified sets under truncated probabilities; and a minimax boundary for uniform stability. On the empirical side, theorem-directed simulations verify recovery rates, compatible-set coverage, and stability gates, while two frozen language-model studies illustrate held-out recovery and conformal coverage. The results specify when observable language probabilities can provide an auditable state measurement without being interpreted as internal belief. 

Keywords: inverse problems; partial identification; nonparametric regression; minimax theory; measurement error; semantic uncertainty; partially observed models.

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are probability models for language. Given a prompt, an autoregres-sive LLM assigns a conditional probability to the next token and, by multiplying conditional token probabilities, to a complete phrase that could follow. These probabilities are useful but answer a linguistic question: what text is likely after this prompt? A physician, engineer, scientist, or risk analyst usually needs a different quantity: what is the probability of each application-relevant state given the evidence? 

The distinction is easy to miss because an LLM can print a polished numerical answer. If it writes “the probability of urgent intervention is 70%,” the number 70% is part of generated text. It need not equal the probability that the model assigned to phrases expressing urgency, and neither quantity is automatically a posterior probability of the patient’s state. Moreover, information-equivalent prompts can redistribute probability among phrases such as “urgent review” and “immediate escalation” even when the evidence and intended state have not changed. 1arXiv:2607.23130v1 [stat.ME] 25 Jul 2026 1. Once frozen, that map converts new grouped language probabilities into estimated state probabilities. The paper studies when this conversion exists, is identified, and is stable. This paper asks whether a posterior over meaningful states can be recovered from the observable language probabilities rather than from a self-reported number. The proposed bridge has two steps. First, a prespecified semantic map groups complete phrases that express the same declared state. Second, a calibration map, fitted only on held-out pairs of language measurements and reference posteriors, converts the grouped probabilities into an estimated state posterior. Figure 1 gives the entire construction. This is a measurement problem before it is a prediction problem. Two states may induce the same observable phrase distribution; the available record may contain only the most probable alternatives; and an inverse that predicts well on average may still amplify small probability errors catastrophically. These are familiar statistical difficulties in an unfamiliar observation space. The relevant ideas come from comparison and approximation of statistical experiments (Blackwell, 1953; Le Cam, 1964), statistical inverse problems (Engl et al., 1996; Cavalier, 2008), errors in variables and generated regressors (Carroll et al., 2006; Mammen et al., 2012), hidden-state observability (van Handel, 2009a), and minimax nonparametrics (Tsybakov, 2009). The language setting adds two structural features. First, observation cells are semantic: several complete verbal responses may represent the same state. Second, only selected continuation probabilities may be exposed. Unreturned probability mass is therefore missing information, not evidence of zero probability.

To make the problem concrete, consider a triage application with three declared states: urgent intervention, routine follow-up, and insufficient information. The semantic map groups “urgent review” and “immediate escalation.” Although rewording moves probability between the two phrases, both prompts yield the same grouped vector (.55 , . 35 , . 10) . Held-out calibration must still determine whether grouped vectors of this kind reliably recover the reference posterior (.60 , . 30 , . 10) . If rewording instead changed the grouped vector to (.35 , . 55 , . 10) , prompt presentation would have altered the measurement and would need to enter its uncertainty. The example contains the paper’s complete inferential sequence: verbal probabilities −→ semantic probabilities −→ reference posterior .

The first arrow is a measurable grouping operation; the second is a statistical inverse learned on held-out pairs. The central questions are whether the second arrow exists, whether it is unique, how strongly it amplifies error, and whether repeated application remains stable. 

Scope beyond LLMs. The motivating and empirical setting is an autoregressive LLM whose conditional token probabilities permit probabilities of complete phrases to be calculated. The paper is not restricted to this special case: its mathematical results are not tied to a transformer architecture. They apply to any fitted probabilistic text generator that defines an observable conditional law over complete responses. They do not apply when only a winning response is observed and no defensible probability record is available. The problem differs from ordinary multiclass prediction. The desired quantity is a conditional distribution over a declared state, not merely its most probable value. It also differs from conventional calibration of a classifier: the native outcome space consists of complete verbal continuations, many continuations can carry the same meaning, and the available probability record may omit some candidates. The map from a reference posterior to this reported semantic distribution is consequently an observation operator, and recovering the posterior is an inverse problem. This formulation connects semantic measurement to partial-identification theory (Manski, 2003; Chernozhukov et al., 2007), while retaining the observable-law definition of identifiability used in nonlinear filtering. 

Contribution of the paper. This paper studies the statistical layer between semantic measure-ment and sequential decision. It takes the state definition and semantic grouping as declared inputs and asks four questions: 1. Does the observable semantic experiment identify the state? 2. What posterior set remains when it does not? 3. At what rate can an unknown semantic update operator be learned? 4. Can the stability margin of that operator be certified uniformly? 3The answers determine whether a calibrated semantic measurement can be transported beyond its original sample. Decision losses and organizational control are deliberately outside the scope. The purpose of this paper is to present four primary contributions. First, we give a single inverse modulus that separates existence of semantic probabilities, point identification, and conditioning of posterior recovery. This yields both a finite-replication bound and a local asymptotic distribution. Second, we treat unavailable continuation probabilities as partial identification and propagate the resulting random set through sequential updates. Third, we derive uniform nonparametric recovery rates under beta-mixing calibration data, including the additional error created when the semantic probabilities are themselves estimated from repeats. Fourth, we show that stable repeated use is governed by a derivative functional whose minimax rate is slower than the rate for predicting the update itself. The simulation experiments are paired one-for-one with these theoretical results. The empirical study uses a frozen 520-scenario financial market-regime data set spanning earnings, economic activity, funding conditions, credit spreads, and market volatility. A prespecified evidence-generating model supplies the exact reference posteriors, while two fitted language models supply the archived candidate probabilities used for held-out evaluation. 

Relation to existing statistical and language-model research 

Statistical experiments, identification, and inverse recovery. Figure 1 locates the contribu-tion between several established statistical literatures. Blackwell comparison asks what decision-relevant information about a parameter is retained by an observable experiment, while Le Cam’s approximation theory formalizes when two experiments are nearly equivalent (Blackwell, 1953; Le Cam, 1964). We use this perspective to distinguish the latent-state experiment from its coarsened verbal observation. Statistical inverse-problem theory then asks whether the resulting observation operator can be inverted and how observational error is amplified by an ill-conditioned inverse (Engl et al., 1996; Cavalier, 2008). These principles are standard. What is new here is the observation operator: it is induced by a declared semantic partition of a conditional law on complete verbal responses, may be only partly observed, and is allowed to vary with prompt presentation. 

Estimated measurements and semiparametric learning. Measurement-error theory studies the uncertainty created when a regressor is observed through a noisy surrogate (Carroll et al., 2006). Two-stage semiparametric theory further shows that estimating a covariate in a first stage can alter the expansion, convergence rate, and limiting law of a second-stage nonparametric estimator (Mammen et al., 2012). The grouped semantic probabilities in this paper play both roles: they are imperfect measurements of the target posterior and generated regressors constructed from repeated language-probability observations. Our rate result therefore separates calibration-sample error from repeated-measurement error instead of treating the grouped probabilities as fixed covariates. The specific decomposition for a partially observed verbal probability law is a contribution of this paper, not a claim inherited from classical measurement-error results. 

Partial identification. When the returned candidates omit probability mass, assigning zero to the unreturned responses would convert missing information into false certainty. The appropriate statistical object is then an identified set rather than a point. This follows the general logic of partial identification (Manski, 2003; Tamer, 2010) and set-valued inference (Chernozhukov et al., 2007; Beresteanu et al., 2011). Our contribution is to construct the sharp simplex-valued set implied by the reported continuations and to propagate that set through the semantic inverse and the sequential recursion. Thus classical partial-identification theory supplies the inferential discipline, while the particular missing-mass geometry and its sequential consequences are new. 4Compositions, calibration, and predictive coverage. Both the target and observed quantities are compositions. Log-ratio coordinates therefore connect the calibration problem to compositional data analysis (Aitchison, 1982; Egozcue et al., 2003). Proper scoring rules clarify how probability forecasts should be compared (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). Modern conformal inference supplies distribution-free marginal predictive coverage after a predictor has been fitted (Lei et al., 2018; Barber et al., 2021; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023), and recent theory makes explicit how nonexchangeability can invalidate the usual guarantee (Barber et al., 2023). These distinctions matter here: a conformal region can quantify held-out recovery error under its sampling assumptions, but it cannot establish that the semantic measurement identifies the intended state, nor can marginal coverage certify an unsupported prompt or evidence shift. Identification and support must be checked before coverage is interpreted. 

Language-model uncertainty. Modern language-model research has documented miscalibration at token, sequence, and answer levels (Braverman et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2021). Directly elicited confidence can differ from native language probabilities (Tian et al., 2023), and binary label-token probabilities can benefit from post-hoc calibration (Giovannotti and Gammerman, 2024). A separate line of work groups sampled answers by meaning and uses the induced semantic entropy to detect unreliable generation (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). Decision-theoretic formulations stress that semantic uncertainty is necessarily task-dependent (Wang and Holmes, 2025). These works establish that uncertainty over text cannot generally be read from one winning token or one printed percentage. The present question is different but complementary. Rather than construct a scalar score that ranks likely error, we seek a probability distribution over a prespecified finite-valued state and ask whether it is identified by the complete observable experiment. Meaning-equivalent phrases are grouped, but unexpressed mass remains visible; prompt variation is part of the observation design; and a reference posterior defines what recovery means. This turns semantic uncertainty from a diag-nostic proxy into a semiparametric inverse problem with explicit existence, uniqueness, conditioning, and minimax questions. Recent surveys organize a rapidly growing collection of language-model uncertainty estimators (Xia et al., 2025); our contribution is to state the statistical conditions that any such estimator must satisfy before it may be interpreted as a posterior measurement. 

Sequential filtering and stability. Finally, sequential use connects the problem to observability and stability of nonlinear filters. Classical results show that identifiability of an observation process and forgetting of initial conditions are related but distinct properties (Le Gland and Mevel, 2000; van Handel, 2009a,b). Here the recovered semantic coordinate is itself estimated, possibly from a truncated probability record. The derivative gate and compatible-set recursion quantify how those measurement errors accumulate. They do not assert that the fitted language system performs Bayesian filtering internally. The analysis is intentionally black-box. No weights, embeddings, hidden states, attention maps, or inaccessible logits enter an assumption or estimator. The stochastic inputs are the contexts supplied to the fitted system, its observable probabilities for complete candidate continuations, the reference posteriors used for calibration, and recorded presentation metadata. The resulting posterior is therefore an external statistical measurement. It is not a claim about a model’s literal internal beliefs. 5How the argument develops 

The paper proceeds from the weakest claim to the strongest. Section 2 first asks whether semantic probabilities exist and whether they distinguish the target state. It then asks whether the inverse is well conditioned in finite samples. Section 3 turns this population question into a learning problem. After establishing recovery of the inverse map, it asks the harder question needed for repeated use: can the derivative of the update be certified uniformly? Section 4 treats incomplete candidate probabilities honestly through set-valued inference and shows how that ambiguity propagates over time. Section 5 aligns simulations with individual theoretical claims and then returns to archived language measurements. Proofs are placed in the appendix so that the main text can emphasize statistical meaning and limitations.

# 3 Learning and certifying the inverse 

Identification describes the population experiment, but an analyst still has to estimate the inverse from finitely many paired examples. This introduces two sampling layers. Across calibration scenarios, the semantic probability summary varies with the evidence. Within a scenario, that summary may itself be measured with error because only finitely many repeated continuations or returned alternatives are available. The theory must account for both. Calibration data consist of pairs (Si, Θi), where Si is the observed semantic probability summary and Θi is a reference posterior coordinate generated independently of the language system. Define 

m0(s) = E(Θi | Si = s). This conditional mean is the identified predictive inverse; it equals a structural inverse only when Θi = m0(Si) almost surely. This distinction prevents calibration accuracy from being misreported as structural identification. 8Predictive versus structural inversion.

# 4 Partial identification and sequential propagation 

The point-recovery results above assume that the relevant semantic probability vector is available. Actual probability records may contain only selected alternatives. Treating every absent candidate 13 as probability zero would manufacture certainty. The statistically honest response is to retain all full probability vectors compatible with the returned coordinates and missing mass, then map that set back to the posterior-coordinate space. When continuation probabilities are unavailable, define J (y) ⊆ ∆J as the set of full semantic laws compatible with the retained record y, including its reported missing mass. The compatible posterior-coordinate set is 

C(y) = {θ ∈ D : gu(θ) ∈ J (y)}.

It is empty under model contradiction, a singleton under point identification, and otherwise non-singleton. In particular, an absent candidate is not assigned probability zero. 

Worked missing-mass example. Suppose four semantic states have reported probabilities 

.55 and .25 for the two returned states, leaving missing mass .20 . The full composition is not 

(.55 , . 25 , 0, 0) . It belongs to 

{(.55 , . 25 , a, . 20 − a) : 0 ≤ a ≤ .20 }.

The ℓ1 diameter of this compatible set is .40 : one endpoint puts all missing mass in state three and the other puts it in state four. A midpoint allocation (.55 , . 25 , . 10 , . 10) is a convenient summary, but it is an assumption rather than an identified probability vector. The set C(y) transfers this ambiguity through the inverse map. The next question is whether such a measurement can be used repeatedly as new evidence arrives. This is the filtering problem. In an ordinary Bayesian filter, the posterior at time t − 1 is first propagated through a state transition and then updated using the observation at time t. Repeating these two operations produces a posterior conditional on the accumulated evidence history. Here the same architecture is used externally to the language model: the semantic measurement supplies an observation channel for a declared state-space model. We do not assume that the language model itself performs Bayesian filtering. Partial identification changes the object being filtered. If a probability record leaves several posterior coordinates compatible with the observation, the recursion begins from a set Ct−1 rather than a single posterior. The state transition propagates the width of that inherited set, and the next incomplete semantic measurement can enlarge it again. A useful filtering theory must therefore answer whether old ambiguity is forgotten, whether new measurement error accumulates without bound, and when the sequential posterior set remains informative. # 5 Simulation and empirical illustration 

5.1 Design 

This section sets out to validate both the theory and the proposed statistical approach. It has three goals. First, we check that the finite-sample behavior of the estimators follows the rates, identification conditions, and stability bounds derived above. Second, we examine whether the proposed diagnostics correctly distinguish recoverable cases from weakly identified, incomplete, or dynamically unstable cases. Third, we ask whether the complete workflow remains informative when the observation law is supplied by fitted language models rather than by a known simulator. The experiments are therefore organized as a sequence of theory-driven tests, not as a model leaderboard. The practical use case is motivated by financial-market monitoring. An analyst may receive evidence about earnings, economic activity, funding conditions, credit spreads, and market volatility, yet require a probability distribution over a smaller set of decision-relevant market regimes. The financial benchmark used below declares three such states: a favorable regime, a weakening or mixed regime, and a severe-stress regime. This setting is useful because the same evidence can be expressed through many verbal continuations, while downstream statistical analysis requires comparable state probabilities. It provides a concrete application of the semantic observation kernel without treating a language model’s output as external market truth. The numerical study uses complementary sources of evidence. Controlled simulations provide known data-generating processes, making identification, missing mass, derivative stability, and minimax separation directly observable; Monte Carlo intervals quantify simulation uncertainty. The frozen financial study then uses archived candidate probabilities from two fitted language models to test held-out posterior recovery, coverage, and prompt-equivalent stability on a 520-scenario market-regime benchmark. The simulations test whether the mathematical conclusions are correct under their stated assumptions, whereas the financial study tests whether the resulting measurement procedure is empirically usable on its declared support. Every simulation corresponds to a preceding theorem and uses the fixed seed 20260724. No fitted-language-system calls are made. First, scalar and two-dimensional posterior coordinates are mapped injectively to, respectively, three- and four-cell semantic laws and estimated from repeated multinomial observations. Second, four-cell compositions are subjected to exact top-two reporting; the unreturned mass is allocated over all compatible omitted cells. Third, correctly specified cubic Gaussian conditional-mean updates with κ ∈ { 0.75 , 0.95 , 1.05 , 1.25 } are fitted and assessed using the exact simultaneous derivative band in Corollary 3.4. 5.2 Archived language measurements 

Controlled simulations can validate mathematics because the true observation operator is known. They cannot show that actual language probabilities carry recoverable state information. The archived illustration serves that second, more modest purpose. Its question is not whether a language system knows the market. It is whether, on a frozen controlled benchmark with a known reference posterior, observable candidate probabilities support held-out recovery and uncertainty coverage. The empirical illustration reuses two frozen controlled studies, each with 520 scenarios, five repeated measurements per scenario, and disjoint calibration, validation, conformal and 100-scenario test partitions. The reference posteriors come from the prespecified synthetic evidence-generating model; the fitted language systems supply observable candidate probabilities. The illustration therefore evaluates recovery from actual language probabilities while retaining a known posterior target. It is not evidence that the reference model describes an external market truth. Both calibrations had full sampled design rank and condition numbers near 20. Raw-response parser cross-checks passed and no missing candidate was silently replaced by zero. In a separate 30-path sequential illustration, calibrated prompt-equivalent comparisons had mean JS divergence 

0.00112 (maximum 0.01072 ), while simultaneous finite-horizon coverage was 0.933 for a nominal 

0.90 region. These results support stable recovery on the frozen experimental support. They do not establish global injectivity of the language kernel or transport to arbitrary prompts, models, domains, or evidence processes. 

What the empirical numbers mean. Coverage .940 on one 100-scenario test partition means that the nominal 90% conformal region contained the reference posterior in 94 scenarios. Coverage 

.900 means 90 of 100. These are finite test counts, not proof of universal validity. The small prompt-equivalent JS divergence says that the calibrated distributions changed little under the tested rewordings; it does not say that arbitrary prompts are equivalent. Condition numbers near 20 also warn that modest probability errors can be amplified, which is why the paper reports coverage 20 and parser completeness alongside mean divergence. 

Connection back to the running example. The empirical workflow has the same structure as triage. Evidence and a prespecified reference model generate a target posterior. Candidate phrases are assigned observable probabilities, grouped into semantic states, and calibrated on one partition. Only after every choice is frozen are posterior recovery, prompt equivalence, and uncertainty coverage evaluated on held-out scenarios. This order prevents the semantic map or prompt family from being chosen to flatter test outcomes. 

# 6 Discussion 

The central lesson is a hierarchy of statistical claims. Each level requires the preceding level, but none is implied by it. This hierarchy is useful both for interpreting the theorems and for designing an empirical study. The paper separates existence, identification, and learnability. A semantic pushforward exists once its cells and covered mass are declared. That does not imply that the resulting experiment distinguishes the latent target. Identification in turn does not imply stable finite-sample recovery. Finally, accurate one-step recovery does not imply that an estimated recursive operator is contractive. This hierarchy prevents several common overclaims. Repeatedly observing a precise language distribution cannot repair a wrong semantic partition. Returning only the most likely alternatives does not justify assigning zero mass to the rest. Low test error does not certify a derivative-based stability margin. A prompt-specific calibration cannot be transported to a new prompt family without a new support and equivalence argument. Return once more to the triage example. Adding .38 and .17 establishes the urgent semantic probability .55 only because the phrases were assigned to the same measurable cell. It does not show that .55 identifies the clinical posterior. A held-out inverse may map it to .60 , but that does not show that a nearby input maps stably. A narrow one-step error interval does not show that repeated updates contract. Finally, a contractive inverse cannot recover probability mass that the observation record never revealed. The paper’s results correspond to these successive questions rather than to one omnibus notion of “trustworthiness.” 

What can be certified. On a declared state space, evidence class, prompt family, fitted system, and supported calibration domain, the procedure can assess local identification, finite-replication error, missing-mass ambiguity, held-out recovery, information-equivalent prompt stability, and a derivative-based contraction gate. The word local is important: certification is conditional on the experiment actually tested. 

What cannot be certified from the same data. The procedure cannot establish that the chosen state ontology is scientifically correct, that the reference probability model describes an external world, that unseen prompts preserve information, or that the fitted system possesses an internal Bayesian belief. Nor can conformal coverage repair nonidentification: prediction sets quantify error around an estimand, while identification determines whether that estimand is recoverable from the observation law at all. 

Why prompt variation is a statistical design factor. Prompt wording is neither mere nuisance text nor part of the target posterior. It indexes the observation kernel. Equivalent prompts are useful replicates only after their equivalence class has been declared and tested. A prompt that 21 changes the target, horizon, evidence, or requested semantics defines a different experiment. The unadjusted-offset row in Table 4 illustrates the danger: presentation bias can look like posterior movement unless it is estimated on calibration data and frozen before testing. The statistical theory complements, but does not replace, the measurement, mathematical-structure, and decision layers. Semantic measurement declares the estimand; lexical calculus asks whether transformations close on the representation; the present paper establishes what can be identified and learned; and a separate decision analysis translates the remaining posterior error into regret. 

# 7 Conclusion 

The motivating problem sounds simple: turn probabilities over language into probabilities over meaningful states. The analysis shows why the apparent simplicity is deceptive. Words must first be grouped into declared measurable events; the induced observation law must distinguish the target; the inverse must be well conditioned; omitted probability mass must remain visible; and repeated use requires derivative rather than level control. Probabilities over language become scientifically useful only through a declared observation experiment. Once that experiment is explicit, standard statistical questions regain their force: which states are separated, what information is missing, how rapidly can the inverse be learned, and can its stability be certified uniformly? The results show why no single accuracy or calibration number answers these questions. Identifi-cation is governed by the observation modulus; incomplete probability mass produces a set rather than a point; recursive stability depends on derivative estimation; and the contraction boundary has a slower minimax rate than function prediction. These distinctions provide the statistical conditions needed before a semantic measurement is repeatedly filtered or used in consequential decisions. The constructive conclusion is equally important. These obstacles are not a reason to discard language probabilities. They show how to study them with familiar statistical tools. A declared semantic map supplies the measurable coarsening, a reference experiment supplies the estimand, an inverse modulus quantifies identifiability, partial-identification sets preserve unknown mass, and simultaneous derivative bands govern recursive stability. Together they turn an informal appeal to linguistic confidence into a sequence of falsifiable statistical claims. The next empirical priority is broader replication across state spaces, domains, and genuinely independent evidence processes. The next theoretical priority is inference for adaptive semantic partitions without sacrificing post-selection validity. Both extensions should preserve the governing principle of this paper: probabilities over language become posterior measurements only to the extent that the observable experiment identifies, calibrates, and stably supports that interpretation.
