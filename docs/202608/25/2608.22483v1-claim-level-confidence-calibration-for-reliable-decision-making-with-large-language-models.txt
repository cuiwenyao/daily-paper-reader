Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly support decision-making in high-stakes domains, but they often hallucinate and express con-fidence that is misaligned with factual correctness. Response-level confidence is a coarse signal: a single generation can mix correct and incorrect statements, so a single number is not actionable for users that must accept, reject, or verify individual pieces of information. We study claim-level confidence calibration as a decision-relevant uncertainty signal: each response is decomposed into atomic, ver-ifiable claims, and each claim is assigned a calibrated confidence using inference-time signals from consistency across samples and self-verification. Our framework operates in closed-box settings (no logits, no fine-tuning) and applies post-hoc calibration directly at the claim level, enabling selective intervention such as evidence retrieval or human review for low-confidence claims. Across Triv-iaQA and TruthfulQA we evaluate seven baselines on six recent models (Llama-3.1, Mistral, Qwen2.5, DeepSeek-R1, GPT-4, GPT-4o), and show that claim-level decomposition combined with post-hoc calibration reduces expected calibration error on factual questions while exposing failure modes on adversarial false-premise questions where decision-makers most need reliable uncertainty estimates.

1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are being deployed as components in decision-support pipelines, where a downstream user must de-cide whether to accept, reject, or further verify the model’s output. In this setting, miscalibrated confidence is not merely a statisti-cal nuisance: a confidently stated but incorrect claim can directly drive a wrong decision [6, 14, 48]. Three broad directions have emerged to mitigate this risk: (1) uncertainty quantification and cal-ibration , which align reported confidence with empirical accuracy [12, 32]; (2) inference-time consistency checks , which sample multi-ple generations and treat agreement as a reliability signal [40]; and (3) self-verification and cross-examination , which probe a candidate response for contradictions and unsupported content [5, 30]. We focus on (2) and (3) because they are model-agnostic, inference-time controls applicable to closed-box deployments where access to logits or fine-tuning is impossible. However, prior work applies these signals at the response level. This is too coarse for decision-making: a single response can contain four claims, three of which are correct, and a response-level score conflates the reliable parts with the unreliable ones. A decision-maker who only knows that the response has 0 .7 confidence cannot tell which sentence to trust. Decomposing responses into atomic claims makes hallucinations measurable at the granularity at which decisions are actually made [2, 46]. 

Contributions. This paper studies claim-level confidence calibra-tion as a decision-relevant uncertainty signal for LLM deployments: (1) We present a closed-box, inference-time pipeline that decom-poses a response into atomic claims, attaches a per-claim confidence from self-verification or log-probability signals, arXiv:2608.22483v1 [cs.CL] 23 Aug 2026 UDM-KDD ’26, August 9, 2026, Jeju, Korea Abbasli et al. 

and applies post-hoc calibration directly at the claim level (§2). (2) We compare against seven representative baselines span-ning sequence likelihood, verbalized confidence, parametric calibration, and prior claim-level methods (HalluMeasure, LMvsLM) on TriviaQA and TruthfulQA across six modern models including reasoning models (§3). (3) We diagnose when claim-level calibration helps decisions (mixed-correctness, closed-box deployments) and when it fails (adversarial false premises, where models confidently reason toward wrong conclusions). The full appendix sup-plies background, additional metrics, reliability diagrams, and reviewer-driven ablations. 

Relation to prior claim-level work. Most closely related is Yuan et al. [46], which proposes fact-level calibration and correction for long-form generation. Our objective is different: we calibrate per-claim confidence to align with empirical correctness (ECE, smECE, Brier) without modifying responses, apply post-hoc TS/PS at the claim level, and conduct a systematic multi-model, multi-baseline evaluation including adversarial false-premise questions. We also differ from HalluMeasure [2] (no calibration step, ternary NLI labels rather than continuous confidence) and from LMvsLM [5] (response level, no claim aggregation). A detailed comparison and an extended related work appear in Appendix B. 1

2 Claim-Level Confidence at Inference Time 

Problem. Given a prompt 𝑥 and an LLM response 𝑟 , we seek (i) a decomposition into atomic, verifiable claims E ( 𝑟 ) = {𝑐 1, . . . , 𝑐 𝑛 }

and (ii) a confidence score conf (𝑐 𝑖 ) ∈ [ 0, 1] for each claim such that confidence aligns with the empirical probability that 𝑐 𝑖 is supported by evidence. Aggregated appropriately, claim-level confidence sup-ports concrete decision actions: accept the response, reject it, re-trieve evidence for low-confidence claims, or escalate to a human. Background on uncertainty types, calibration fundamentals (TS, PS, smECE, etc.), and full definitions are deferred to Appendix A. 

Pipeline (Fig. 1). Our inference-time pipeline has five stages: (1) In-context reasoning —greedy decoding for the primary response and optional 𝑁 -sample stochastic decoding; (2) Claim extraction —an LLM-based extractor (GPT-4o-mini) emits self-contained, atomic claims of ≤ 15 words with pronouns resolved; (3) Claim verifica-tion —each claim is scored by an auxiliary verifier (LLM, RAG, or web search); (4) Per-claim confidence —we convert verifier outputs into a numeric score in [0, 1]; (5) Calibration and aggregation —we apply post-hoc temperature or Platt scaling at the claim level , and aggregate to a response-level score via mean (or min) when needed.

Claim-level post-hoc calibration. We apply temperature scaling (TS) and Platt scaling (PS) to the per-claim scores rather than to response-level likelihoods. This is what makes the signal usable for selective intervention: a calibrated 0 .85 on a single claim con-cretely means accept this claim with ∼15% expected error , whereas a response-level 0 .85 averages over heterogeneous sentences. Cal-ibration parameters are fit on a held-out subset of the training split. 

Why claim-level is closed-box-friendly. Unlike methods requir-ing fine-tuning or token-level probabilities (e.g., [9]), the verbalized variant only requires text outputs. This makes it directly applicable to API-only models (GPT-4, GPT-4o) which are common decision-support deployments. 

Baselines. We compare against (i) length-normalized sequence likelihood with and without CoT [23]; (ii) verbalized confidence [35]; (iii) post-hoc TS/PS on sequence likelihoods [12, 34]; (iv) Hal-luMeasure [2], a claim-level NLI-based detector; and (v) LMvsLM [5], an iterative cross-examination protocol. Implementation details are in Appendix C. 

3 Experiments 

Setup. We evaluate on TriviaQA [20] (factual trivia, an easy-difficulty regime where most claims are well-formed) and TruthfulQA [27] (an adversarial regime built around common misconceptions and false premises). We use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [8], Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [17], Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [44], DeepSeek-R1 [13], GPT-4 [1], and GPT-4o [16]. We report ECE, smECE, Brier score, AUROC, and BLEU; dataset sizes are in Appendix C.1. 

What works for decision making (Tab. 1). On TriviaQA, para-metric calibration (PS/TS) and our claim-level method yield sub-stantially lower ECE than uncalibrated few-shot baselines for every model. For Llama and Mistral, PS reduces ECE to ≤ 0.013. For Qwen and Mistral on TruthfulQA, TS cuts ECE by 3–4 × over the few-shot baseline. On Mistral/TriviaQA, our method attains an ECE of 0 .123 at 𝛼 =0.3, outperforming LMvsLM (0 .270) and approaching Hal-luMeasure (0 .107); notably, this is the only setting where the best mixture weights self-verification more heavily than cross-sample consistency ( 𝛼 <0.5), indicating that the relative value of the two signals is model-dependent. These reductions translate directly into a more usable accept/reject signal for downstream decision-makers: a calibrated 0 .9 now reliably means ∼10% expected error. 

Selective prediction vs. calibration (AURC). For Llama-3.1, AURC reveals a complementary picture to ECE. Because PS and TS only rescale probabilities, they preserve the few-shot ranking and thus share its AURC (0 .523 on TriviaQA; 0 .001 on TruthfulQA)— calibration does not improve selective prediction by itself. Con-versely, HalluMeasure attains the lowest TriviaQA AURC (0 .400) despite its high ECE (0 .242), and our method sits mid-pack (0 .466). Where claim-level decomposition pays off. HalluMeasure achieves the lowest ECE on DeepSeek-R1 / TriviaQA (0 .001) and our method outperforms the temperature-scaling baseline on GPT-4o / Trivi-aQA by 15x. The benefit of claim-level scoring is largest in mixed-correctness regimes (long, multi-claim factual responses) where a response-level number averages over heterogeneous content. This is exactly the regime where a decision-maker needs to know which 

sentence to trust, not just an overall score. 

Where it fails: adversarial false premises. On TruthfulQA, claim-level methods consistently underperform on models that confidently state false-premise-induced claims. For GPT-4o, our method’s ECE rises to 0 .412 versus 0 .118 for TS—a model-level 

consensus failure : GPT-4o produces fluent, high-probability claims that are systematically wrong, and our pipeline faithfully reflects the model’s own (mistaken) confidence rather than correcting it. LMvsLM mitigates this on GPT-4o (0 .112) and DeepSeek-R1 (0 .066) because cross-examination actively probes for contradictions. The implication for decision-makers is that closed-box, confidence-only signals are not sufficient under adversarial inputs: an external probe or evidence source is required. 

Reasoning models. DeepSeek-R1 emits long CoT traces that, when claim-extracted in full, produce many intermediate reasoning claims with weakly calibrated per-step confidence. Mean aggrega-tion dilutes the final-answer signal; restricting extraction to the final answer segment is a practical fix that we leave as a deploy-ment recommendation (see Appendix C.6). Across both GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1, verbalized confidence is better calibrated than on the open-source models, suggesting reasoning-focused train-ing improves the model’s ability to express uncertainty in natural language.

Decision-relevant takeaways. (i) For mixed-correctness, easy-difficulty inputs in closed-box settings, claim-level decomposition with post-hoc calibration provides actionable per-claim confidence and matches or beats response-level baselines. (ii) For adversar-ial inputs (false premises, misconceptions), an active verification step (e.g., LMvsLM-style cross-examination or external retrieval) is necessary; confidence alone is unreliable. (iii) Reasoning models produce better verbalized confidence but require restricting claim extraction to the final answer for deployment. Detailed cost anal-ysis, 𝛼 sensitivity, and an ablation isolating decomposition from verifier strength are in Appendix C.3 and Appendix C.5. 

4 Conclusion 

We studied claim-level confidence calibration as a decision-relevant uncertainty signal for LLM deployments. Decomposing responses into atomic claims and applying post-hoc calibration at the claim level yields actionable per-claim confidence in closed-box settings, and reduces expected calibration error on factual questions. A Preliminaries and Calibration Background A.1 Key Definitions 

Claim. A claim is an atomic, verifiable statement extracted from an LLM response. Given a prompt 𝑥 and response 𝑟 , a claim ex-traction function E : 𝑟 → { 𝑐 1, . . . , 𝑐 𝑛 } produces 𝑛 atomic claims. Each claim 𝑐 𝑖 should be (i) atomic (a single factual assertion); (ii) 

verifiable (assessable as supported, contradicted, or unknown given evidence); and (iii) self-contained (interpretable without the full response context). 

Claim verification. Given 𝑐 𝑖 and optional evidence 𝑒 , a verifier 

V : (𝑐 𝑖 , 𝑒 ) → { verbalized confidence } produces a verification label or numeric score. The verifier may be an auxiliary LLM, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system, or an external knowledge source. 

Inference-time signals. Statistics derivable during generation that correlate with correctness without requiring access to parame-ters or training. We use two types: (i) consistency signals , derived by sampling 𝑁 responses and measuring agreement; and (ii) self-verification signals , obtained by prompting the model or an auxiliary verifier. 

Claim-level confidence. For each claim we estimate conf (𝑐 𝑖 ) ∈ [0, 1] that should be calibrated, i.e. conf (𝑐 𝑖 ) should align with the empirical probability that 𝑐 𝑖 is supported by evidence. 

A.2 Uncertainty: Epistemic vs. Aleatoric 

Uncertainty is commonly split into epistemic (model) uncertainty and aleatoric (data) uncertainty [4, 21, 39]. Epistemic uncertainty arises from limited knowledge and is reducible with more data or a better model. Aleatoric uncertainty arises from input noise and is irreducible [36]. Many estimation approaches use entropy-based statistics on softmax outputs [38, 41], with recent work on prob-abilistic contrastive learning to better represent data uncertainty [22]. 

A.3 Calibration Methods for LLMs 

Existing calibration methods for LLMs fall into three groups by access level. 

Open-box calibration fine-tunes the model or a small adapter using internal logits/representations. Calibrated fine-tuning penal-izes overconfident incorrect answers [19]; LitCab [29] adds a single linear layer on top of last-layer logits with a max-margin objective; Yang et al. [45] integrate Laplace approximation with LoRA; SLiC-HF [50] uses preference data to calibrate token ranking. Other work fine-tunes models to output “unsure” [43, 49]. Open-box methods are costly at LLM scale and often fail to generalize across model families [35]. 

Post-hoc calibration rescales final-layer outputs. Temperature scaling (TS) [12] introduces a temperature 𝑇 tuned on a held-out set,

B Extended Related Work 

Uncertainty estimation for LLMs spans open-box, post-hoc, and closed-box methods (cited above). Closely related inference-time approaches sample multiple generations: self-consistency [40], universal self-consistency [3], semantic uncertainty [23], and self-evaluation for long-form calibration [15]. Self-verification methods include SelfCheckGPT [30]; cross-examination methods include LMvsLM [5] and multi-agent debate [26]. Token-level uncertainty quantification for fact checking has been studied by Fadeeva et al. [9]; this requires per-token log-probabilities and is therefore inapplicable to closed-box deployments that are our primary target. 

Claim-level evaluation. HalluMeasure [2] decomposes responses into atomic claims and classifies each via NLI labels; entailment-based verification uses NLI or LLMs as verifiers [28]. 

Closest relation: Yuan et al. [46]. Their work proposes fact-level confidence calibration and correction for long-form generation. Distinctions: (i) Calibration vs. correction. Yuan et al. rewrite in-correct facts; we estimate whether expressed per-claim confidence Claim-Level Confidence Calibration for Reliable Decision Making with Large Language Models UDM-KDD ’26, August 9, 2026, Jeju, Korea 

aligns with empirical correctness probability (ECE, smECE) without modifying responses. (ii) Post-hoc calibration. Yuan et al. do not apply TS/PS at the claim level; we treat per-claim confidence as a quantity to be calibrated. (iii) Evaluation metrics. They use preci-sion/recall/correction accuracy; we use ECE/smECE/BS/AUROC— confidence-accuracy alignment, not rewriting quality. (iv) Breadth.

C Implementation Details C.1 Datasets 

TriviaQA [20] contains >650K question–answer–evidence triples, plus 95K author-written QA pairs. Following [37] we sample 12K examples for training and 1.5K for testing. We include 10 in-context exemplars [10, 23]. 

TruthfulQA [27] contains 817 questions across 38 categories de-signed to elicit common misconceptions. We use a zero-shot setting where each question has true and false target answers with sup-porting sources. The dataset is split evenly between training and testing; for the few-shot baselines we add 10 in-context exemplars.

C.6 Reasoning Model Diagnosis 

DeepSeek-R1 emits long chain-of-thought traces that, when claim-extracted in full, produce many intermediate reasoning steps with weakly calibrated per-step confidence. Mean aggregation dilutes the final-answer signal. Restricting extraction to the final answer segment is a practical deployment fix; we observe consistent im-provement when the extractor is gated to the final answer span. This is a configuration recommendation, not a fundamental limitation of the framework.

E Discussion: When the Method Helps Decision Making 

Best-case scenarios. (i) Mixed correctness : when responses con-tain both correct and incorrect claims, claim-level scores support targeted intervention. (ii) Closed-box deployment : verbalized confi-dence at the claim level avoids the logit-access constraint of TS/PS-on-likelihoods and Fadeeva et al. [9]. (iii) Moderate complexity :multi-claim responses where decomposition adds value beyond a single score. 

Failure modes. (i) Consensus failure : all sampled responses agree on an incorrect claim (TruthfulQA on GPT-4o). (ii) Verifier weakness :errors in claim extraction or verification propagate. (iii) Unverifiable claims : future predictions or creative content where no external check exists. 

Practical recommendations. Cost-benefit: apply claim-level decomposition selectively when fine-grained confidence matters; for simple queries response-level may suffice. Verifier selection: 

verifier quality dominates; consider hybrid LLM+retrieval veri-fiers. Selective application: use fast response-level screening to flag suspicious responses, then claim-level analysis only for those. 

Threshold calibration: safety-critical domains require conserva-tive thresholds; exploratory uses can tolerate lower confidence. 

Future directions. Adaptive sampling (early-stop 𝑁 when samples agree); hierarchical confidence over interdependent claims; domain-specific calibration (medical/legal/scientific); uncertainty-aware training that incorporates claim-level signals into objectives.
