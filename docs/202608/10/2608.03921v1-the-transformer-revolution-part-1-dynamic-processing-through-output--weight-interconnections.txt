# The Transformer Revolution, Part 1: Dynamic Processing through Output-Weight Interconnections 

Marco Giunti †, Fabrizia Giulia Garavaglia ‡

> † giunti@unica.it, ‡ fabriziag.garavaglia@unica.it † ALOPHIS – Applied Logic Philosophy and History of Science ‡ Dip. di Pedagogia, Psicologia, Filosofia † ‡ Università di Cagliari † ORCID: 0000-0003-4182-4850, ‡ ORCID: 0009-0003-4699-4709

ABSTRACT This paper o=ers a new interpretation of the Transformer during inference. Against the “stochastic parrot” view that large language models merely reproduce statistical regularities learned in training, we argue that Transformers construct and apply prompt-dependent transformations whose parameters are generated during inference. We call this form of computation SIDPP: Sequence-level Interactive Dynamic Parallel Processing. The Transformer is interpreted as a system that transforms concepts by means of concepts. Token vectors are the concepts to be transformed; parameterized transformations defined by matrices and vectors are the transforming concepts. These may be static, when fixed through training, or dynamic, when generated from the input sequence. Mechanically, they correspond to groups of simple neural networks. The Transformer’s architectural novelty lies in output– weight interconnections, through which the outputs of some networks determine the weights of others, alongside ordinary output–input interconnections. By means of these interconnections, the system constructs transformations from the prompt and uses them to modify token representations. The contribution of dynamic processing grows with prompt length and may equal or exceed that of static processing—a phenomenon we call strong prompt sensitivity. This account bears on interpretability, predictability, control, and the design of smaller, more sustainable systems. Finally, since the human neural system possesses the mechanisms required to implement SIDPP, we argue that a form of SIDPP may, in principle, be neurally realized in the cerebral cortex. We therefore conjecture that human language processing may itself be a form of SIDPP produced by a functional architecture relevantly similar to that of the Transformer. 

## 1 Introduction 

Less than four years after the launch of ChatGPT on 30 November 2022, we are witnessing a period of accelerated development and innovation in Artificial Intelligence (AI), largely driven by the emergence of a new paradigm based on the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al. 2017) and on its subsequent adaptations and applications across an extremely wide range of fields and tasks. Looking at the history of AI (Nilsson 2010), we can distinguish three main phases, characterized overall by four di=erent types of systems: rule-based symbolic systems in the first (1950–1980); traditional neural networks in the second (1980–1990); and, in the third (1990–2017), systems that exploit statistical regularities extracted from large amounts of data. The third phase initially comprised statistical machine-learning systems proper and, subsequently, deep neural networks. 1 These types of systems di=er profoundly from one another, both in their structure and in the way they process information. They nevertheless share one common feature: none of them was ever able to pass the Turing test (1950), or even to come close to passing it (Oppy & Dowe 2021). Turing proposed this test as an objective criterion for determining whether a machine is intelligent. Although this interpretation is no longer tenable (Block 1981; Bayne & Williams 2023; Biever 2023; Jones & Bergen 2026), the fact remains that, in order to pass the test, a machine must be capable of conversing with a human being on any topic, using natural language appropriately and drawing on a shared body of knowledge. Equipping a machine with these capacities represented an insurmountable problem for AI until, at the end of 2022, ChatGPT demonstrated to the general public that it possessed them. GPT-4.5 subsequently formally passed the Turing test, with statistically robust results, in a series of preregistered experiments (Jones & Bergen 2025, 2026; cf. Giunti 2026). To understand how this breakthrough became possible, it is necessary to investigate in depth the mechanisms underlying the operation of the Transformer architecture, which represent a radical change in the way AI processes information. We call this new type of processing SIDPP—Sequence-level Interactive Dynamic Parallel Processing (§ 3). We will see, in particular, that the Transformer is a system that transforms concepts by means of concepts (§§ 2.1–2.2). The concepts to be transformed are vector representations of tokens, while the transforming concepts are the neural networks that modify these representations (§ 2.3). Transforming concepts constitute the material structure of the Transformer because they are nothing over and above interconnected simple neural networks. What makes the Transformer innovative, however, is the non-standard form of interconnection among these networks, whereby the outputs of some determine the weights of others (§ 2.7). 

Dynamic transforming concepts—the networks whose weights are the outputs of other networks and are therefore updated dynamically during inference—make a highly significant contribution to the Transformer’s overall processing. Their contribution increases with prompt length and, even with relatively short prompts, may become comparable to, or even greater than, that of static transforming concepts—the networks whose weights are fixed through training. We call this phenomenon strong prompt sensitivity (§ 4). It was clearly manifested in GPT-4.5’s recent success in passing the Turing test (§ 5). An essential part of the Transformer’s processing is therefore dynamic: it depends on parameters that are not assigned fixed values through training and whose contribution remains substantial even with relatively short prompts. Understanding and exploiting this distinctive feature of the Transformer opens up new perspectives for the interpretability, predictability, and control of such systems (§ 6.1). It may also have important implications for the design and implementation of new, smaller systems with lower energy consumption and environmental costs (§ 6.2). The central thesis of this paper is that, during inference, the Transformer is not merely a set of static parameters mechanically applied to an input, but a system of parallel processes that

## 2 The Transformer as a System Composed of Groups of Simple Neural Networks Linked by Output–Input and Output–Weight Interconnections 

As is well known, the Transformer has two distinct modes of operation: (i) training, including pre-training and fine-tuning, and (ii) inference, when it is given a prompt and generates a response on that basis. A widely held view is that the key to its entire functioning lies in mode (i), and that inference therefore consists simply in reproducing the statistical regularities that the model learned during training (Floridi et al. 2025). This is the source of the well-known metaphor according to which LLMs are nothing more than “stochastic parrots” (Bender et al. 2021). In fact, careful consideration of the mathematical definition of the model and, in particular, of the meaning of the mathematical operations performed by attention heads (Vaswani et al. 2017, § 3.2; Lee & Trott 2023; Giunti 2025, § 2.1.1; Jurafsky & Martin 2026, chs. 8 and 9) immediately reveals that the model’s operation during inference goes far beyond the mere reproduction of statistical regularities learned during training. The system is capable of adapting its previously acquired knowledge on the basis of the prompt it receives, using these adaptations to modify the initial representations of each token, and, on this basis, selecting the token that constitutes the best continuation of the prompt itself.

2.2 Dynamic Transforming Concepts as Adaptations of Static Transforming Concepts Based on an Input Sequence 

An important point of this conceptual interpretation of the Transformer is that it clarifies why the operation in inference goes far beyond the pure and simple repetition or application of what has been learned in training. Training determines only some of the concepts that the system uses to transform the representations of each token. These are the static transforming concept s, defined by the weight matrices and also by the bias and scaling vectors, the elements of which are all trainable parameters of the system: 10 they act on the representations of the tokens by transforming them based on what has been previously learned. In addition to these transformations, however, the Transformer employs further transformations, defined by additional matrices and vectors whose elements are not trainable parameters, but are dynamically produced during the inference process itself. We have called these transformations dynamic transforming concepts , and they are at work in each attention head

This process—consisting of (i) the adaptation of previously acquired knowledge on the basis of an input sequence and (ii) the subsequent application of the adapted knowledge to modify the representations of the prompt—is much more complex and sophisticated than the mere direct application, to the vector representations of the prompt, of the transformations defined by matrices or vectors fixed by training. However, in order to fully understand what is novel about the Transformer architecture, it is also necessary to consider how the mathematical operations that define the model can be identified with a system composed of groups of simple neural networks that, as we shall see, are interconnected in non-standard ways .

2.3 Transforming Concepts as Three Types of Simple Neural Networks 

First of all, we note that, from a mechanical-structural point of view, all the transforming concepts—the parameterized transformations of the three types ℒ[𝑴] , +[𝒗 ] and ∘[𝒗 ]—can be identified with three di=erent types of simple neural networks. 1. The transforming concepts of the type ℒ[𝑴] , where 𝑴 ∈ ℝ $×& , are dense linear networks ,consisting of two fully connected levels of units, the first with 𝑞 input units and the second with 𝑝 output units. Each input unit has only output connections with all output units, each of which has only the corresponding input connections. The 𝑖 -th row of 𝑴 identifies the weights of all output connections of the 𝑖 -th input unit, while the 𝑗 -th column gives us the weights of all the incoming connections of the 𝑗 -th output unit. Each input unit performs no transformation on the incoming signal but simply routes it to all the output units through its outgoing connections. Each output unit produces the weighted sum of all signals from the input units, thus implementing the 𝑗 -th component of the linear transformation ℒ[𝑴] .2. The transforming concepts of the type +[𝒗 ], where 𝒗 ∈ ℝ $, are one-to-one additive networks . They have two layers, the first with 𝑞 input units and the second with 𝑞 output units. The 𝑖 -th input unit has only one output connection with the 𝑖 -th output unit and no input connections with other units. In addition, the 𝑖 -th output unit has only one input connection with the corresponding input unit and no output connection with other units. The 

𝑖 -th element 𝑣 ! of vector 𝒗 is the weight of the corresponding connection. Each input unit does not transform the input signal but only routes it to the corresponding output unit through its output connection. Each output unit sums the input signal with the weight of its connection, thus implementing the 𝑖 -th component of the transformation +[𝒗 ]. 3. The transforming concepts of the type ∘[𝒗 ], where 𝒗 ∈ ℝ&, are one-to-one multiplicative networks . 2.7 The Novelty of the Transformer: Output-Weight Interconnections Between Networks with Trainable Parameters and Networks with Dynamic Parameters 

We saw in the previous section that, with the sole exception of the formal transposition operation, 𝑇𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑠𝑝 , all the Transformer’s matrix operations can be identified either (i) with 

groups of 𝑛 ,-. simple neural networks , of which only the first 𝑛 ≤ 𝑛 ,-. are active, or (ii) with two types of interconnections between such groups of networks: operational output-input interconnections , if they modify the output before transferring it as input; routing output-input interconnections , if they transfer the output without modifying the signals, but regroup them in a di=erent way. However, we still need to understand the exact role of the 𝑇𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑠𝑝 operation within the system of interconnected groups of simple neural networks that realizes the entire functional structure of the Transformer. To this end, we must take a step back and recall what we said in § 2.4 about those modules in which two processes coexist and are ultimately reduced to a single process .In such cases, one of the two processes terminates , and its final operation therefore produces an output that is not further processed, but instead becomes the parameters of an operation of the other process, which we call the receiving operation . This means that the parameters of this operation are not fixed by training, but are instead dynamic parameters determined by the output of the final operation of the process that terminates (see Figures 4–6 and, in Figure 1, the two Residual connection modules). In the Transformer, the receiving operations are always parameterized operations of types 4 (𝑀𝑢𝑙𝑡[𝑴] ) or 3 ( 𝐴𝑑𝑑[𝑴] ), whereas the final operations are parameterized operations of types 4 (𝑀𝑢𝑙𝑡[𝑴] ) or 1 ( 𝐴𝑑𝑑[𝑴] ), or the formal operation 𝑇𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑠𝑝 applied to the output of a parameterized operation of type-4 ( 𝑀𝑢𝑙𝑡[𝑴] ). This implies that the reduction of two processes to one always occurs through a new type of interconnections between groups of 𝑛 ≤ 𝑛 ,-. 

simple networks: they link each output unit of the final group of networks of the process that terminates with the connections of those networks in the receiving group whose weights are identical to the output of that unit. We call interconnections of this new type output-weight interconnections (see Figures 8–8c and 10–12e). In the Transformer, the reduction of two processes to one occurs in four di=erent ways and, as we shall see below (§ 2.7.1), the 𝑇𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑠𝑝 operation plays an essential role in the second. It should also be kept in mind that the first two ways occur in each attention head (see Figures 4– 5), the third in each residual connection associated with a multi-head attention module (see Figures 1 and 3), and the fourth in each residual connection associated with a feed-forward module (see Figures 1 and 2). ## 3 Sequence-level Interactive Dynamic Parallel Processing in the Transformer 

The analysis conducted so far has clarified that a Transformer can be described in two di=erent ways, equivalent from a functional point of view, but which highlight di=erent aspects of this architecture: (i) as a system of successive processing modules, each consisting of one or more processes: each process is a succession of operations on matrices whose rows are vector representations of the prompt's tokens; (ii) as a system composed of groups of 𝑛 ,-. simple neural networks, where the 𝑖 -th network of each group performs the processing related to the 

𝑖 -th (1 ≤ 𝑖 ≤ 𝑛 ≤ 𝑛 ,-. ) prompt’s token. In such a system, each group of simple networks implements a parameterized operation on matrices of types 1, 2, 3, 4, or a structural operation of types 1 or 2. In addition, two groups of simple networks are connected by: (a) standard output-input interconnections, in which the output of a network in the first group is the input of the network corresponding to the same token in the second group; (b) two other types of output-input interconnections, operational and routing , which correspond to the structural operations on matrices of types 3, 4, 5 in the first case, and to the 𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑐𝑎𝑡 formal operation in the second; (c) output-weight interconnections of four di=erent types. The information processing carried out by the Transformer represents a radical change compared to that of previous AI systems. We call this new type of processing SIDPP – 

Sequence-level Interactive Dynamic Parallel Processing , where: (i) SPP— Sequence-level Parallel Processing —means that the input sequence is processed in parallel, producing a corresponding output sequence. The elements of these sequences are vector representations of the prompt's tokens that, from a mathematical point of view, are treated as rows of matrices; (ii) SIPP— Sequence-level Interactive Parallel Processing —is a SPP where at least one vector representation in the output sequence depends on an vector representation of a di=erent token in the input sequence; (iii) SDPP— Sequence-level Dynamic Parallel Processing —is a SPP such that the output sequence depends on parameterized transformations, whose parameters in turn depend on the input sequence. The parameters of these transformations are called 

dynamic. 

Sequence-level parallel processing (SPP) is the type of processing that takes place in all parameterized or structural operations of the Transformer, starting from the one that performs positional encoding and up to the one that produces the probability distributions by applying the function 𝑠𝑜𝑓𝑡𝑚𝑎𝑥 . Processing is also interactive (SIPP), but not dynamic, in normalization operations. In the addition operations of residual connection modules, processing is dynamic (SDPP), but not interactive. Finally, in each attention head, sequence-level parallel processing is dynamic and also interactive (SIDPP). Considering the Transformer as a system of interconnected groups of simple neural networks, we realize that the di=erent types of processing are related to di=erent morphological characteristics of such a system. First, sequence-level parallel processing (SPP) is produced (i) by the groups of simple neural networks that implement the parameterized operations of the Transformer or the structural operations of the first two types, and (ii) by the groups of functional units that implement the operational interconnections corresponding to the structural operations of types 3, 4, 5. Secondly, dynamic processing (SDPP) is made possible by the output-weight interconnections, which are present both in the residual connections (see Figures 8–8c) and in the attention heads (see Figures 10–12e). Finally, interactive processing (SIPP) is due to the fact that the two types of output-weight interconnections in the attention heads and the operational interconnections corresponding to Norm structural operations (see Figures 13–13c) are interwoven links between two groups of simple networks: they connect networks in one group to networks in the other group that do not correspond to the same token. In contrast, all other interconnections are not interwoven, because they connect the networks of one group with networks of the other group corresponding to the same token. Interwoven interconnections ensure inter-token interaction between two groups of networks, but it is important to note that this interaction occurs in two di=erent ways, depending on which of three forms the interwoven links take. 𝑁𝑜𝑟𝑚 operational interconnections and output-weight interconnections of the first type (§ 2.7.2) make the 𝑗 -th feature (1 ≤ 𝑗 ≤ 𝑞) of the 𝑖 -th token 

(1 ≤ 𝑖 ≤ 𝑛 ≤ 𝑛 ,-. ) depend on the 𝑗 -th features of all tokens; they are therefore feature ⇛

feature links (see Figures 10d, 11d, 13c). The output-weight interconnections of the second type (§ 2.7.2) make the 𝑗 -th feature (1 ≤ 𝑗 ≤ 𝑛 ≤ 𝑛 ,-. ) of the 𝑖 -th token (1 ≤ 𝑖 ≤ 𝑛 ≤ 𝑛 ,-. )depend on all features of the 𝑗 -th token; they are therefore token ⇛ feature links (see Figures 10e, 11e). Finally, we note that, in attention heads, it is precisely the succession of these two types of interaction, 14 implemented by the corresponding interwoven output-weight interconnections, that constitutes the causal mechanism by which a token attends to other tokens.

## 5 Strong Prompt Sensitivity in GPT-4.5’s Turing Test Success 

The phenomenon of strong prompt sensitivity was also clearly manifested in GPT-4.5's recent passing of the Turing test (Jones and Bergen 2025, 2026; cf. Giunti 2026). Table 1 presents the results of four tests conducted by Jones and Bergen, who selected the human participants from two di=erent populations: participants recruited through Prolific (Prolific 2026) or students recruited through the UCSD Psychology Undergraduate Subject Pool. All four tests used a three-player format, with one human interrogator and two respondents, one human and the other an AI. In all four tests, the AI respondent was GPT-4.5. The only di=erence was that, in the first two, it was given a short system prompt called PERSONA (only 187 words long), which describes the personality and style to adopt when responding to the interrogator; in the other two, this prompt was replaced by an even shorter one (21 words), which simply states that the model is to take part in a Turing test with the aim of convincing the interrogator that the respondent is human (see Table 2). As shown in Table 1, the success rates change markedly depending on whether the PERSONA prompt is present: from 75.5% to 42.1% in the tests with the Prolific population, and from 69.2% to 27.7% in those with the UCSD student population. These results are also statistically robust. In fact, GPT-4.5-PERSONA's success rates are significantly greater than 50% both in the test with the UCSD student population (right-tailed 𝑝 -value = 5 × 10 <L < 0.001 ), and in the test with the Prolific population (right-tailed 𝑝 -value = 2 × 10 <#M < 0.001 ), whereas GPT-4.5-NO-PERSONA's success rates are significantly less than 50% in the test with the UCSD student population (left-tailed 𝑝 -value = 2 × 10 <N < 0.001 ), but not significantly less than 50% in the test with the Prolific population (left-tailed 𝑝 -value = 0.1 > 0.001 ). 16 

In summary, we can therefore state that GPT-4.5 with the PERSONA prompt passed both Turing tests, but without that prompt it certainly failed one, while we cannot determine with certainty whether it failed or passed the other. In any case, these results demonstrate GPT-4.5's strong prompt sensitivity and provide clear confirmation of the decisive impact of dynamic processing on the behavior of Transformer systems. 

## 6 Looking to the Future: New Research Perspectives 

The di=erent levels of description according to which we have analyzed the Transformer open up a series of new perspectives that can form the basis for new lines of research in di=erent fields. The central thesis of this work is that a Transformer, during inference, is not just a set of static parameters mechanically applied to an input, but a system of parallel processes that builds and then uses new transformations starting from the prompt. This construction takes place thanks to non-standard interconnections between groups of simple networks: not only output-input, but also output-weight. It follows that an essential part of processing—what we have called SDPP here—depends crucially on dynamic parameters (i) whose values are not fixed by training and (ii) whose number grows with the length of the prompt, so that they can come to have as much impact on processing as static parameters do, or even more. Starting from this reading, we can indicate at least two research perspectives that appear particularly promising. 

6.1 A Better Understanding of the Processes Actually Carried Out by the Transformer: Explainability, Control, and Predictability 

A first line of research concerns a better understanding of what the Transformer really does in inference and, on this basis, a possible progress towards three closely related goals: explainability, control and predictability. Many contemporary approaches to interpretability focus predominantly on static elements: matrices of learned weights, neurons, features, or circuits identified a posteriori in the trained model (Montavon et al. 2018; Samek et al. 2019; Olah et al. 2020; Elhage et al. 2021). This is certainly important, but it risks neglecting a decisive part of the processing: the production and use of dynamic transformations (defined by matrices and vectors) that depend on the prompt and that, precisely for this reason, can change significantly from one input to another.

The perspective proposed here suggests instead to treat inference as a process of assembling 

a computational structure specific to that prompt: a “material” structure (in the mechanical-structural sense) that is formed during execution through output-weight interconnections, and that realizes transformations whose operational content and relative weight are not immediately readable by the set of static parameters alone. From this point of view, understanding the output of the model does not only mean reconstructing what static knowledge has been invoked, but also reconstructing: 

• what dynamic transformations have been generated; 

• where they have been grafted (which networks have received which dynamic weights); 

• how these weights modulated the transformation trajectories of token representations; 

• what role did the residual connections play as a channel of additive dynamic parameters. In other words, explainability becomes, at least in part, a science of structural dependencies between processes: not only which tokens influence which tokens (interaction), but also which tokens determine which parameters (dynamics) and, therefore, which parameters determine which transformations. In this perspective, attention is not simply a matrix of weights to be read 

a posteriori , but a causal process that is produced through the succession of two types of interaction between representations of different tokens, made possible by two types of interwoven interconnections, both of the output-weight type, therefore prompt-specific and dynamic (see § 3, last paragraph). Precisely for this reason, the approach avoids an immediate identification between high attention weights and causal reasons of the output: several studies have shown that attention maps, while often informative, do not constitute a causal explanation of the model's behavior in themselves (Jain & Wallace 2019; Wiegreffe & Pinter 2019). This approach also opens up concrete possibilities on the control side. If a relevant part of the behavior depends on transformations that arise in response to the prompt, then intervening on the prompt (or on portions of its content) is not only a way of providing information, but a way of shaping the computational structure that will be assembled in inference. This suggests new control techniques that are not reduced to simple textual rules, but that aim to govern the formation of dynamic parameters: for example, by controlling in a targeted way the distribution of attention, the stability of the generated transformations and the balance between dynamic and static contributions (Brown et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2023). Finally, predictability can also be rethought in a more realistic way. In the presence of strong prompt sensitivity, predictability cannot be understood as a global property of the model, but as a local property of the specific structure that has been formed in inference. In this perspective, a reliable prediction requires estimating (or constraining) the dynamic complexity of the computation actually triggered: how much space is given to dynamic parameters, how much they vary with small changes in the input, and how much the response depends on those variations. In summary: understanding the role of dynamic parameters is not a technical detail, but a necessary step to move from static and partial interpretations to a more faithful understanding of the real processes of the Transformer, with direct repercussions on interpretability, control and reliability. 6.2 Neuromorphic Implementation: Reduction of Energy Costs, Environmental Impact, Robotics and Edge Computing 

A second perspective concerns the physical implementation of AI systems. Today, large Transformers are, in fact, systems of neural networks simulated through numerical computation on vast parallel-hardware infrastructures. This mode of implementation entails very high energy and environmental costs and is intrinsically tied to a centralized model based on data centers, with constraints on latency, connectivity, and energy consumption that become critical when considering AI applications deployed on a large scale and extensively integrated into a wide range of devices and environments (Strubell et al. 2019; Patterson et al. 2021). The interpretation proposed here, however, suggests a different idea: the Transformer should not be seen only as a single monolithic model, but as a modular system of groups of simple networks whose power arises above all from the non-standard interconnection (in particular output-weight) and the consequent dynamics in inference. In more general terms, the idea that the output of one network can generate the weights of another has also been explored in the literature on hypernetworks (Ha et al., 2017); here we find it as the central organizing principle of the Transformer in inference, and therefore as a possible design constraint for a hardware that wants to reproduce its dynamics. On the neuromorphic side, the theme of computation as local signal processing and modulation is a historical thread that goes from the first neuromorphic engineering programs to the most recent manycore architectures (Mead 1990; Indiveri & Liu 2015; Davies et al. 2018). In such an implementation, at least in principle, the aim could be: 

• reduce dependence on huge amounts of high-precision numerical multiplication operations, spreading computation over simpler units; 

• use forms of local modulation and memory that make the generation and application of dynamic parameters more efficient; 

• Achieve smaller, more efficient and more autonomous systems, suitable for environments where energy and connectivity are limited resources. This scenario is particularly relevant for two areas. 

Robotics. A robotic agent that has to operate in real, dynamic and uncertain environments benefits from the ability to adapt quickly to the context. The idea of dynamic transforming concepts suggests a model in which adaptation is not delegated to retraining, but occurs through the construction, in real time, of transformations dependent on the input. If this mechanism can be implemented in hardware at low cost, it could pave the way for more autonomous agents that are less dependent on continuous access to remote systems. 

Edge computing. Bringing some of the intelligence to the edge (devices, sensors, local infrastructures) requires compact, low-power models. But it also requires a type of plasticity that, today, is often achieved with centralized systems. The idea of dynamic transformations generated in inference, on the other hand, suggests an operational plasticity, obtainable without updating weights via training, but through modulations produced by the input (Shi et al. 2016; Satyanarayanan 2017). This is exactly the kind of mechanism that good neuromorphic architecture might want to replicate. In conclusion, the mechanical-structural reading of the Transformer is not just a conceptual exercise. It offers a lexicon and a set of constraints to rethink the future design of AI systems: not just bigger models or more data, but architectures that enhance the dynamics in inference and that can be implemented in a more sustainable way. These perspectives naturally lead to the question that closes the first part of our investigation: if output-weight interconnections are the key to the computational plasticity of the Transformer, is there something analogous in the plasticity of our neural system? It is to this possibility—bold but not without clues—that the last paragraph of this first part is dedicated. 

## 7 A Bold Conjecture 

We have shown in the preceding sections (§ 2.7 and § 3) that, from a mechanical–structural point of view, the true novelty of the Transformer lies in output–weight interconnections, which make sequence-level dynamic parallel processing (SDPP) possible. An interconnection of this type connects an output unit of one network to a connection in another network whose weight is identical to the output of that unit (see Figure 16). Output–weight interconnections can therefore be conceived as links that modify or configure the connections of the receiving network. For the moment, however, this is only a metaphor, because what does it mean, operationally, to modify a connection? We can make this idea precise by asking what the functional units of the simple networks composing the Transformer are, what operations they perform, and where exactly they are located. According to what we said in § 2.3, the input units of any simple network do not modify the incoming signal in any way, but merely route it to the output units through their outgoing connections. We therefore identified the output units as the actual functional units of the network: in dense linear networks (see Figure 17), each output unit produces the weighted sum of all the signals coming from the input units; in one-to-one multiplicative (or additive) networks, it multiplies the single incoming signal by the weight of the connection (or adds the signal to this weight). However, in the case of dense linear networks, identifying the functional units with the output units is rather coarse-grained, because it does not take into account that the weighted sum is a complex operation that can be decomposed into two successive stages. In the first stage, all the signals coming from the input units are multiplied, in parallel, by the weights of the corresponding connections; in the second, all the weighted signals are summed. It is therefore natural to suppose that distinct functional units operate at each of these two stages. At the second stage, there is a single unit that sums the already weighted signals: each output unit of the network can be identified with a functional unit of this kind, which is purely integrative. At the first stage, by contrast, each connection has a distinct functional unit that multiplies the signal by the corresponding weight: there are therefore as many multiplicative functional units as there are connections (see Figure 18). This analysis has thus led us to reconsider how dense linear networks are usually conceived and also enables us to make explicit the mechanism by which their connections are modified or configured (see Figure 19). First, output units are still conceived as functional units, but they perform only the sum of the already weighted signals, not the multiplication of the signals by their respective weights. Second, for each connection from input unit 𝑖 to output unit 𝑗 , there is a corresponding functional unit with two inputs and one output. Its output is connected to output unit 𝑗 , and its first input is connected to input unit 𝑖 . Its second input, by contrast, is the one that determines the weight of connection 𝑖 → 𝑗 . Modifying, or configuring, connection 𝑖 → 𝑗 therefore now has a precise operational meaning: it means changing its weight by supplying a new value to the second input of the corresponding functional unit located on that connection. This is precisely what is achieved by implementing an output–weight interconnection that links an output unit of another network to the second input. This way of conceiving dense linear networks also applies, with only minor modifications, to one-to-one multiplicative or additive networks. In one-to-one multiplicative networks, the only difference is that the functional units consist solely of the multiplicative units associated with each connection. In this case, the output units perform no operation, because each receives a single signal that has already been weighted by the functional unit located on the corresponding connection. The same applies to one-to-one additive networks, except that the functional units located on the connections are additive rather than multiplicative. We now note that the interpretation of simple networks outlined here makes evident a series of correspondences, both morphological and functional, between these artificial networks and biological neural networks (see Figure 18). First, an output unit , with its function of integrating the different signals converging on it, corresponds to the soma of a biological neuron; second, an input unit , with its function of routing the signal to different output units, corresponds to the 

terminal arborization of the axon of a biological neuron; third, the connections between an input unit and different output units correspond to the links between the branches of the axon and the dendrites of different neurons; finally, the functional units located on those connections correspond to the synapses between the axon terminals and the dendritic terminals, which establish those links. We have repeatedly emphasized that dynamic processing is what makes the plasticity and adaptability of the Transformer possible and that, at the neural level, the mechanism underlying this processing is provided by output–weight interconnections. In this section, we have precisely defined this mechanism as a link between an output unit of one simple network and the second input of the functional unit located on a connection of another simple network. We have also noted the morphological and functional correspondence between the functional units located on the connections of a simple network and the synapses of a biological network. We now ask whether this correspondence can also be extended to output–weight interconnections—that is, whether biological networks contain mechanisms morphologically and functionally similar to a link between an output unit and the second input of the functional unit located on a connection (see Figure 19). Keeping in mind the correspondences established above, this means that we are looking for a mechanism morphologically similar to a link between a second neuron and a synapse connecting an axonal branch of a first neuron to a dendrite of a third. From a functional point of view, moreover, the link should ensure modulation of the signal coming from the first neuron, and this modulation should be caused by the signal sent by the second neuron through the hypothesized link. We know, in fact, that at least two mechanisms of this type are present in biological neural networks and, in particular, in the human cerebral cortex. In axo-axonic synapses (see Figure 20), an axon from a second neuron contacts the presynaptic bouton of the axon of a first neuron and modulates its neurotransmitter release. 17 A modulatory effect of one synapse on another may also occur between two neighboring axo-dendritic synapses on the same dendrite; here, however, the modulation is functional and postsynaptic (affecting the response), rather than  

> 17 For a review of axo-axonic synapses (especially axon-on-terminal contacts) and their role in the presynaptic modulation of neurotransmitter release and in the regulation of neural circuits, cf. Cover & Mathur (2020).

structural and presynaptic (affecting release). 18 It is also known that the plasticity and adaptability of the cerebral cortex depend to a large extent on synaptic mechanisms, that is, on the modulation of the efficacy of connections between neurons. 19 

Thus, mechanisms morphologically and functionally similar to the Transformer’s output– weight interconnections are known to exist in the human cerebral cortex, in the sense that a second influence (another axon, or a nearby synapse on the same dendrite) converges locally on a synapse and modulates its efficacy. Since output–weight interconnections constitute the material basis of sequence-level dynamic parallel processing (SDPP), it follows that, at least in principle, this type of processing could be implemented directly at the neural level in our nervous system. Moreover, in light of the further correspondences we have established between simple neural networks and biological networks, an analogous conclusion is also plausible for sequence-level parallel processing in general (SPP) and for sequence-level interactive parallel processing (SIPP). In summary, it is possible, at least in principle, that a form of SIDPP can be realized at the neural level in our cerebral cortex. Since SIDPP is the type of processing that characterizes the Transformer architecture, this in-principle possibility leads us to formulate a bold conjecture: 

Conjecture T. Is it possible that the morphology and functioning of our neural system realize a type of functional architecture similar to that of the Transformer? This hypothesis will be developed in detail in the second part of this work, highlighting its plausibility, particularly with regard to language processing in the brain areas responsible for it.

LEGEND By a simple neural network , we mean any neural network of one of the following three types: one-to-one additive, one-to-one multiplicative, or dense linear. In the following schemes, the output units of a simple neural network are represented by yellow dots, whereas the input units are represented by black, red, blue, or green dots of the same size. The connections between input and output units are represented by: (a) thick lines, when their weights are model constants fixed independently of training or input; (b) thin lines, when their weights are model parameters, either trainable or dynamic. Connections with trainable parameters are represented by colored thin lines. Connections with dynamic parameters are represented by thin lines onto which an arrow points (see Figures 8–8c and 10–12e): if both the line and the arrow are black, the arrow carries no signal and the connection therefore has no weight; if the line and the arrow have the same color—red, blue, or green—the weight of the connection is equal to the signal carried by the arrow.
