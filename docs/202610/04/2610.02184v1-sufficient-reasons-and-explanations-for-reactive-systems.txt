# Sufficient Reasons and Explanations for Reactive Systems ⋆

Hadar Frenkel and Nadav Rutman Moshe 

Department of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Bar-Ilan University, Israel 

hadar.frenkel@biu.ac.il 

Abstract. We address the problem of temporal causality and explain-ability for reactive systems, and, in this setting, study sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations. These two notions are well-known explain-ability measures in the context of neural networks. In this work, we unify these notions for reactive systems and formal specifications given in temporal logic, providing dedicated definitions for sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations. We then lift these definitions to temporal 

sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations, providing more general and symbolic representations of explainability. We analyze the complex-ity of both verifying and finding explanations of the different types, and we demonstrate our approach using a prototype implementation. 

# 1 Introduction 

In this work, we address different aspects of explainability and causality stud-ied in the literature, and study them in the context of reactive systems and temporal logic specifications. Reactive systems interact with their environment indefinitely, where, at each discrete time point, they can react to environmental inputs with some system outputs. They form the common formalism used in the verification community today, used as an abstraction for many different for-malisms, from hardware circuits [2], to software code [23]. The prominent logic to reason about the behavior of reactive systems over time is Linear Temporal Logic (LTL, [29]), widely used to formally prove that systems adhere to their specifications. But, even if we can prove the correctness or incorrectness of a system, it is not always clear why a system satisfies or falsifies its specification. In recent years, many works have studied explainability and causality in re-active systems [22,1,7]. In particular, a recent line of work has studied temporal actual causality , explaining effects given as LTL specifications: Based on the def-initions of Halpern and Pearl [19], [10] provides an algorithm for finding actual causes as finite sets of events on the trace; [11] defines causes as temporal prop-erties; [14] presents an algorithm for the synthesis of temporal actual causes as automata over infinite words; and [8] provides a complexity analysis.

This work was supported by the Israel Science Foundation (ISF grant No. 655/25). arXiv:2610.02184v1 [cs.FL] 1 Oct 2026 In this work, we study different formalisms for temporal explainability, namely, 

sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations [4], which are intuitive formalisms that are already used to explain the outputs of neural networks. Similar to Halpern and Pearl’s actual causality, sufficient reasons take into account the 

current input to the system, and aim at explaining the behavior of the sys-tem, when given this input. But, while Halpern and Pearls’ actual causes are the most refined reasons for a given effect, sufficient reasons provide the most general reason for the effect (though they might collide in some cases). For ex-ample, [19] describe a scenario in which Suzy and Billy throw a rock, but since Suzy is stronger, her rock hits a bottle first, which causes the bottle to break. In their actual causality analysis, they define an elaborated mechanism to derive that Suzy is the actual cause for the broken bottle. Sufficient reasons define the sufficient events, that, had they occurred, the result would stay the same. In this case, Suzy and Billy throw the rock is a sufficient reason, but minimal sufficient reasons would be Suzy throws the rock , or Billy throws the rock . While actual causality can be used to derive accountability (here, Suzy is to blame for the broken bottle), (minimal) sufficient reasons tell us what are the sufficient actions we need to take in order to achieve the same outcome. Contrastive explanations ,that we study at the end of this paper, describe what are the minimal changes we need to do to avoid the outcome. In the context of reactive systems, when a system violates its specification, a model-checker outputs a counterexample trace [6]. Applying our causal analysis on this trace using sufficient reasons, we can find the most general behavior of the system that would still violate the specification (analysis which one can then use to repair the system). In contrast, we can use contrastive explanations to find minimal changes that would help avoiding the violation. In the same way, we can find positive example traces that satisfy the specification, and use our proposed analysis to derive what actions would maintain the same positive behavior (using sufficient reasons), or what are minimal changes that would lead to a violation, to help us avoid it (using contrastive explanations). Motivated by the need for such intuitive and simple definitions of explain-ability, we provide definitions of (minimal) sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations for reactive systems, both as sets of events (not limited to finite sets), and as temporal reasons and explanations. We present an algorithmic ap-proach for verifying and finding (events/temporal) reasons and explanations, and demonstrate our approach with a prototype implementation, and we pro-vide complexity analysis for each problem: verification/synthesis of sufficient reasons/contrastive explanations in the temporal/non-temporal setting. Following these definitions, this work presents a new approach for temporal explainability for reactive systems, allowing the user to choose what definitions to use depending on the type of desired explanation. Moreover, we establish a common ground for a formal definition of explainability, already used across different fields. 

Contributions We provide definitions, complexity analysis, and algorithms for sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations for effects given in temporal logics. 2We show that for minimal sufficient reasons that are sets of events on the trace, there is always an ultimately periodic representation for this set of events, which we can find by solving a model-checking instance. Our use of logical formalism to express minimal sufficient reasons allows us to find infinite sets of events on a trace. For minimal temporal sufficient reasons, that is, the reasons themselves are temporal properties and not only sets of events, we show that there is always a unique, ω-regular minimal temporal sufficient reason. We show that the problems of verifying (minimal, temporal) sufficient reasons and contrastive explanations are all PSPACE complete, which stems from the underlying LTL model-checking problem. The main focus of our work is the analysis of sufficient reasons. In addi-tion, in Section 7, we briefly address contrastive explanations. While contrastive explanations were addressed in previous works [17,28] as counterfactual traces, here we provide a complexity analysis for the synthesis of contrastive explana-tions, and provide a characterization of temporal contrastive explanations, that were not studied before. Finally, we present a prototype implementation.

# 3 Running Example 

Consider the system of Figure 1, representing a biased arbiter: once user 1 issues a request ( r1), user 2 can never be granted access to some resource ( g2). On the other hand, every request of user 1 is answered by a grant ( g1), and requests of 

user 2 are answered by grants as long as user 1 did not issue any request. 

(Minimal) sufficient reasons: Consider the simple specification user 2 is eventually granted access , expressed by the LTL formula φ1 = g2, and the counterexample trace π = {r1, r 2, g 1}ω . A minimal sufficient reason for the vi-olation of φ1 on π would be a minimal set of events that appear on π and are sufficient to determine that π ⊨ ¬φ1. In this case, {⟨ 0, r 1⟩} , is such a minimal sufficient reason: it is sufficient to observe r1 on time 0, to never observe g2. A sufficient reason for ¬φ1 := ¬g2 would be any superset of {⟨ 0, r 1⟩} .

Temporal sufficient reasons: Now, assume we want user 1 to never be granted access, via the LTL specification ¬g1. It’s negation is g1, and the trace π above is a witness for the violation. Every occurrence of r1 on π con-stitutes a sufficient reason for g1. However, in this case, we might want to describe the reason in a more general way, as a temporal property. A temporal sufficient reason would be any property that π satisfies which is sufficient to ob-serve g1. Here, temporal sufficient reasons are: r1 – every trace that always has r1 (as π), would satisfy g1; r1; simply r1; and r1, which is also the minimal temporal sufficient reason (see Section 6): While the trace π satisfies 

r1, it is sufficient that it would have only satisfied r1 to observe g1.

Contrastive explanations: For g1 and π above, a contrastive explanation is a closest trace τ to π, on which g1 does not hold, for example {r2, g 2}ω :only avoiding all occurrences of r1 would avoid g1.

A sufficient reason provides a set of elements that is sufficient to fix the output of a function. In this section, we lift this definition to express sufficient reasons for LTL effects in reactive systems. We define reasons as (possibly infinite) sets of events that appear on the trace. In Section 6 we lift the definition once more, to refer to temporal sufficient reasons. In the following, we define reasons over the set IP of input propositions, while the effect φ can be over all of AP , to express relations between inputs and outputs. One can easily tweak the definition to take into account other propositions either in the reason or in the effect. 

Definition 4 (Sufficient Reasons for LTL Effects). Let T be a transition system, π ∈ TR (T ), φ an LTL formula such that π ⊨ φ, and R ⊆ N × IP .

– We say that R is a sufficient reason (SR) for π ⊨ φ in T , if for every trace 

τ ∈ TR (T ), if τ [t] = {a} π[t] for all ⟨t, a ⟩ ∈ R, then τ ⊨ φ. In that case, we say that R is a SR for ⟨φ, T, π ⟩.

– We say that R is a minimal sufficient reason (MSR) for ⟨φ, T, π ⟩ if R is a SR for ⟨φ, T, π ⟩ and there is no SR R′ such that R′ ⊊ R.

# 7 Contrastive Explanations 

Contrastive explanations are system traces that, in contrast to π, do not satisfy the effect. We will usually be interested in minimal contrastive explanations, i.e., the ones that are the closest to the trace π, to understand what are the minimal changes needed to be done in order to avoid the effect. To reason about such minimal explanations formally, we first define a distance between traces. We define a distance measure ∆ : (2 AP )ω × (2 AP )ω → 2N×IP to be the set of all inputs and timesteps on which the two traces disagree: 

∆(π1, π 2) = {(n, i ) | n ∈ N, i ∈ IP , i ∈ π1[n] \ π2[n] ∨ i ∈ π2[n] \ π1[n]}

Definition 6 (Contrastive Explanations). Let T be a transition system, let 

π, τ ce ∈ TR (T ), and let φ be an LTL formula such that π ⊨ φ.

– We say that τce is a contrastive explanation (CE) for ⟨φ, T, π ⟩ if τce ⊭ φ.

– We say that τce is a minimal contrastive explanation (MCE) for ⟨φ, T, π ⟩ if 

τce ⊭ φ, and, for every trace τ ′̸ = τce ∈ TR (T ) such that τ ′ ⊭ φ we have 

∆(π, τ ′)̸ ⊆ ∆(π, τ ce ). That is, there is no CE that is closer to π than τce .

7.3 Between Sufficient Reasons and Contrastive Explanations 

Previous works in the context of neural networks have established duality be-tween MSRs and MCEs [20]. In the context of explaining temporal effects in reactive systems, this does not hold; while the notions of MSRs and MCEs are intuitively dual, we cannot directly derive, using a duality relation, one from the other. It is not enough to negate the events in a MSR to avoid the effect, since MSRs are not unique. One might suggest to negate all MSRs, however, this is too strict: it is enough to negate one event from each MSR, to find a CE. We formalize this in Theorem 9. The proof is straightforward and appears in Appendix B for completeness. Therefore, MSRs and MCEs are clearly related, but are not dual. The reason that duality fails here, while holds for the neural networks domain, is the nature of our problem: In other domains, such as [20], the only component needed for evaluation is the function f which is explained. In our work, the function we evaluate is the satisfaction of φ, but we have a sec-ond component that influences the decision, which is the system T from which the traces are taken. 

Theorem 9. Given T , π and φ, let R = {Ri | i ∈ I} be the set of all MSRs for

# 10 Conclusions 

Motivated by the need to formalize different explainability notions and unify these notions across different fields, we study sufficient reasons and contrastive expla-nations for reactive systems given LTL effects. We provide formal definitions for the non-temporal and temporal variants of the problem, together with algorithms and complexity analysis for verification and synthesis of the different variants. 18 Sufficient reasons provide another view on explainability of reactive systems, and we believe that exploring more notions of explainability, and formally defin-ing them, would allow better understanding of systems and their behavior. In the future, we plan to develop our prototype into a fully automated tool that can be used for computing (minimal, temporal) sufficient reasons and con-trastive explanations automatically. We also plan to study the relations and derivations between the different causality and explainability notions.

22 A LTL and HyperLTL 

We provide here the full definition of LTL and its semantics, and then of Hyper-LTL and its semantics. 

LTL Linear Temporal Logic (LTL [29]) is defined over the set of atomic propo-sitions AP using the following grammar: 

ψ := p | ψ ∨ ψ | ¬ ψ | ψ | ψ U ψ

Where p ∈ AP , is the temporal operator next , and U is the temporal operator 

until . We use the usual derived Boolean operators ∧, →, ↔, and the derived temporal operators ψ = true U ψ (eventually ψ) and ψ = ¬ ¬ψ (globally 

ψ). We define when an infinite word α ∈ (2 AP )ω satisfies an LTL formula φ

inductively, as follows: 

α ⊨ p iff p ∈ α[0] 

α ⊨ ¬ψ iff α ⊭ ψα ⊨ ψ1 ∨ ψ2 iff α ⊨ ψ1 or α ⊨ ψ2

α ⊨ ψ iff α[1 , ∞) ⊨ ψα ⊨ ψ1 U ψ2 iff ∃t2 ∈ N s.t. α[t2, ∞) ⊨ ψ2

and ∀t1 < t 2 : α[t1, ∞) ⊨ ψ1

We say that a system T satisfies an LTL formula φ, denoted by T ⊨ φ iff 

∀α ∈ TR (T ) : α ⊨ φ.The language of an LTL formula φ is the set of all word that satisfy φ:

L(φ) = {α ∈ (2 AP )ω | α ⊨ φ}.It holds that every LTL formula φ can be translated to a non-deterministic Büchi automaton A such that L(φ) = L(A) [31]. We denote this automaton by 

Aφ and will use this notation throughout the paper. 

HyperLTL HyperLTL [9] extends LTL with quantification over traces, allowing it to express relational properties over infinite executions. The syntax of HyperLTL is defined using the following grammar, given a finite set V of trace variables. 

ψ := pπ | ψ ∨ ψ | ¬ ψ | ψ | ψ U ψφ := ∃π.φ | ∀ π.φ | ψ

Where π ∈ V is a trace variable. That is, HyperLTL formulas are LTL formulas where atomic propositions are augmented by traces variables, and, with a prefix of existential and universal trace quantifiers. The semantics of HyperLTL are given with respect to a set of traces T and a trace assignment Π : V → T . Given a trace variable π ∈ V and a trace τ ∈ T , we denote by Π[π 7 → τ ] the trace assignment that coincides with Π on all variables, 23 except for π which is mapped to τ . For the assignment Π, we denote by Π[t, ∞)

the assignment that maps every variable π ∈ V to the suffix of Π(π) starting from position t, that is: Π(π)[ t]Π(π)[ t + 1] Π(π)[ t + 2] · · · . The semantics of HyperLTL is defined inductively as follows. 

Π, T ⊨ pπ iff p ∈ Π(π)(0) 

Π, T ⊨ ¬ψ iff Π, T ⊭ ψΠ, T ⊨ ψ1 ∨ ψ2 iff Π, T ⊨ ψ1 or Π, T ⊨ ψ2

Π, T ⊨ ψ iff Π(π)[1 , ∞), T ⊨ ψΠ, T ⊨ ψ1 U ψ2 iff ∃t2 ∈ N s.t. Π[t2, ∞), T ⊨ ψ2

and ∀t1 < t 2 : Π[t1, ∞), T ⊨ ψ1

Π, T ⊨ ∃π.φ iff there exists τ ∈ T s.t. Π[π 7 → τ ], T ⊨ φΠ, T ⊨ ∀π.φ iff forall τ ∈ T : Π[π 7 → τ ], T ⊨ φ

We say that T satisfies ψ, denoted T ⊨ ψ, if Π∅, T ⊨ ψ where Π∅ : V → ∅ is the empty assignment, meaning that ψ contains no free trace variables that need to be assigned. We say that a transition system T satisfies a HyperLTL formula ψ

if TR (T ) satisfies ψ.
