Abstract 

We propose ReHoPER, an inference-only, zero-shot method that improves large language models’ reasoning by generating and answering intermediate questions along multiple paths before the final answer. It iteratively plans a horizon of candi-date intermediate questions, selects one to answer, and replans from the updated history. ReHoPER is task-agnostic, using the same generic instructions across datasets and models without labeled data or task-specific prompt design. Across multiple datasets, including iLLC, a new controlled benchmark for compositional reasoning, ReHoPER outperforms strong baselines, with the largest gains in the most compositional settings. Our implementation 1 and the iLLC generator 2 are publicly available to support future work.

1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) trained at scale can be prompted in natural language to perform a wide range of NLP tasks, achieving strong zero- and few-shot performance, often close to fine-tuned systems, without task-specific training [1, 2]. Instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback further improve their ability to follow instructions and generalize to new tasks, reinforcing LLMs as general-purpose, prompt-driven systems rather than narrowly trained models [3, 4]. Despite this progress, benchmarks designed to probe multi-step and compositional reasoning, such as MoreHopQA [5] and MuSR [6], show that even state-of-the-art models still struggle to integrate multiple pieces of evidence and handle long-range dependencies. Inference-time reasoning has improved with methods such as Tree-of-Thoughts [7], StrategyLLM [8], and Self-Ask [9], which guide models through multi-step procedures like search, strategy retrieval, or follow-up

However, these approaches often depend on labeled data (e.g., solved examples for learning or retrieving strategies) or a manually crafted reasoning structure tailored to each new task. In contrast, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) [10] and Plan-and-Solve (PS) [11] use simple, reusable instructions (e.g., “let’s think step by step” or “first outline a plan, then solve the problem”) to enable oracle-free reasoning across tasks, and Self-Discover (SD) [12] further reduces manual effort by letting the model generate its own reasoning structure at inference time. Still, these methods mostly follow linear reasoning patterns, and can potentially benefit from richer, more flexible planning mechanisms in the spirit of Tree-of-Thoughts and StrategyLLM, while remaining oracle-free and task-agnostic. In this work, we introduce Receding-Horizon Planning for Enhanced Reasoning (ReHoPER), which targets this design space from a different angle. Rather than prescribing a fixed sequence of explana-tions or relying on discovered task-specific templates, we guide the model to generate and answer intermediate questions about the current instance before committing to a final prediction. ReHoPER operates entirely at inference time and remains task-agnostic as the same generic instructions for proposing and answering intermediate questions can be used across tasks, models, and answer-format prefixes (Input-Output, CoT, and PS) without any task-specific labeled data or demonstrations. Figure 1 summarizes the planning loop and how predictions from multiple paths are combined. ReHoPER follows a receding-horizon strategy. At each step, it generates a list of intermediate questions and answers the first unresolved one given the current history, loosely analogous to classical receding-horizon control [13, 14]. This alternating loop of planning and execution combines look-ahead with step-by-step adaptivity. Each answer updates the reasoning state, triggering replanning that can revise, refine, or discard previously proposed questions, enabling a dynamically evolving reasoning process rather than a static question sequence. We evaluate ReHoPER against strong base-lines on MuSR, MoreHopQA, and iLLC, a new controlled benchmark for compositional execution, using a wide range of LLMs. Across these settings, ReHoPER improves over the compared methods under matched prompting conditions. Our contributions are as follows: (i) we introduce ReHoPER, a task-agnostic, inference-only method that uses adaptive planning to generate and answer intermediate questions across multiple reasoning paths and aggregates their predictions; (ii) we introduce iLLC, a controlled diagnostic benchmark for compositional execution; (iii) we provide a comprehensive empirical study that compares ReHoPER against strong baselines from prior work across diverse models, datasets, and reasoning styles; (iv) we offer a systematic analysis of ReHoPER design choices and behaviors, through ablations and trajectory analyses; and (v) we publish our implementation and iLLC data generator to facilitate further research. 

2 Related work 

Prompting strategies play a central role in eliciting reasoning behavior from LLMs. Early work predominantly evaluated LLMs using Input–Output (IO) prompting, where models directly predict a final answer without exposing intermediate reasoning. This paradigm changed with the introduction of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, which encourages models to generate explicit reasoning steps in natural language through either few-shot exemplars with rationales [10] or minimal zero-shot instructions such as “Let’s think step by step” [15]. However, while zero-shot CoT can elicit step-by-step reasoning, it does not require the model to commit to a complete reasoning outline, leading to missing steps and omission errors. Plan-and-Solve (PS) prompting [11] addresses this limitation by decoupling planning from execution: the model first generates an explicit plan and then executes it, effectively encouraging structured reasoning via a “plan, then solve” instruction. Several methods decompose compositional tasks into subquestions. Self-Ask [9] generates and answers follow-up questions before producing a final response, while Least-to-Most (LtM) prompting [16] first produces subproblems and then solves them sequentially. However, both rely on curated exemplars and predefined reasoning templates, in which the decomposition is determined upfront and executed in a single pass. Self-Discover (SD) [12] removes manual design by composing a reasoning structure in a zero-shot setting. Still, it commits to a fixed plan before solving, limiting its ability to adapt as new information emerges during reasoning. ReHoPER instead uses generic instructions to generate and answer intermediate questions for each instance, gathers predictions along multiple paths, and aggregates them. Within each path, it selects one question from a short plan 2before updating the plan from the new answer. This allows subsequent questions to reflect newly obtained information without committing to a fixed decomposition. This iterative plan–execute–replan paradigm connects ReHoPER to classical planning and control, particularly receding-horizon control (RHC), also known as model predictive control, where decisions are made by optimizing over a short horizon, executing one step, and replanning from the updated state [13, 14, 17]. However, unlike classical RHC, ReHoPER does not rely on an externally defined reward function; instead, it uses the model’s own predictions of the goal question, conditioned on the evolving reasoning state, as an implicit evaluation signal. Beyond explicit decomposition, several methods enhance LLM reasoning through iterative sampling, structured search, or self-reflection. Self-consistency [18] samples multiple independent CoT trajec-tories and aggregates their final answers, using diversity to reduce variance. Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) [7] extends this idea by explicitly exploring a tree of intermediate reasoning states using search and evaluation, enabling broader exploration at the cost of increased inference complexity. StrategyLLM [8] commits to a high-level plan prior to execution. However, these approaches typically rely on a fixed plan (with no ability to re-plan) or costly search over multiple branches. Self-Refine [19] and CoVe [20] iteratively improve outputs via self-feedback and verification, RISE [21] combines decomposition, retrieval, and self-critique through iterative self-exploration, and Think Twice [22] repeatedly re-answers the same question to correct errors. Different from the above approaches, Re-HoPER integrates lightweight planning and adaptive question selection within multiple independent forward reasoning trajectories, enabling targeted exploration without handcrafting reasoning paths, manually labeled data, or post-hoc revision. 

3 Methodology 

We propose ReHoPER ( Receding-Horizon Planning for Enhanced Reasoning ), an inference-only method that guides an instruction-tuned LLM through intermediate questions before answering the goal question Q∗. ReHoPER runs multiple stochastic reasoning paths and within each path repeatedly looks ahead by generating a horizon of candidate intermediate questions conditioned on the current path history. It then executes only one question, incorporates the resulting answer into the history, and replans from this updated state. This makes the process inherently dynamic: each newly obtained intermediate answer can change what information is missing, render some planned questions irrelevant, or suggest a better follow-up direction. Thus, ReHoPER adapts its reasoning trajectory dynamically, correcting earlier plans and incorporating newly revealed information on the fly without committing to a fixed plan. By combining diversity across paths with per-step look-ahead planning and replanning within each path, ReHoPER maintains an organized decomposition while enabling course correction, leading to more reliable final predictions. We formalize the task setting in Section 3.1, defining the inference-only objective and notation. In Section 3.2, we then present ReHoPER in detail, describing its receding-horizon planning, path rollout, and aggregation procedure. 

3.1 Problem statement 

Each task example consists of an optional context C and a goal question Q∗. Given an instruction-tuned LLM f , the objective is to produce a prediction ˆy that matches the ground-truth label y. The proposed method operates in a fully inference-only setting and does not rely on any task-specific instruction, labeled examples, or additional unlabeled data beyond the given task test example. 

3.2 ReHoPER

4 Experimental setup 

4.1 Datasets and tasks 

We evaluate ReHoPER on three reasoning benchmarks spanning long-context narrative inference, multi-hop evidence aggregation, and controlled compositional execution: MuSR [6], MoreHopQA [5], and our proposed i-th to Last Letter Concatenation (iLLC) benchmark. MuSR includes three long-context reasoning tasks, MM (Murder Mysteries), OP (Object Placement), and TA (Team Allocation), each requiring models to integrate scattered clues and constraints. MoreHopQA tests multi-hop question answering over contexts that require connecting multiple pieces of evidence. We follow the original setup by evaluating on the 150-example subset selected by the dataset authors. Finally, iLLC 

generalizes the established Last Letter Concatenation (LLC) [10] by requiring models to extract the 

i-th-to-last character from each word and concatenate the resulting letters; i = 1 recovers standard LLC. We denote each subset as L{ i}-{ w}, where i is the target character position from the end and 

w is the number of words in the sequence. Our main experiments use L1-4 , L1-6 , L2-4 , and L2-6 ,while additional results on the longer L1-8 and L2-8 settings are reported in Appendix J. 4We use iLLC to study compositional execution in a setting where the required operations are known. Solving an instance requires extracting one character from each word, preserving the order of the words, and combining the characters into the final answer. This structure fits our study of intermediate-question planning because the problem can be broken into well-defined steps. It also lets us vary difficulty in a controlled way: i changes the character-extraction operation, while w changes the number of operations that must be composed. Since the task does not depend on external knowledge, its results are less affected by differences in factual recall or retrieval. In addition, the generator can produce new labeled instances at different difficulty levels, making the benchmark easy to scale and extend. We use iLLC to test procedural compositional reasoning under controlled conditions, complementing the broader reasoning settings covered by the natural-language benchmarks. Further details on iLLC and the other datasets, including statistics, examples, and post-processing procedures, are provided in Appendix M. 

4.2 Language models 

We evaluate ReHoPER on a diverse set of instruction-tuned LLMs spanning multiple model families, parameter scales, and deployment regimes, including dense models from Llama, Gemma, Phi, and Mistral, as well as sparse Mixture-of-Experts models such as gpt-oss and Qwen3. To make larger models practical at inference time, we use quantized AWQ or GPTQ variants when needed. For Qwen3, we evaluate both its non-thinking mode and its thinking-enabled mode; unless a result is explicitly labeled as Qwen3 with thinking, Qwen3 denotes the non-thinking configuration. Full model and deployment details are provided in Appendix K, Table 16. 

4.3 Baselines and setup details 

We use three different assistant output prefixes: IO (input-output), CoT (chain-of-thought) [10], and PS (plan-and-solve) [11]. Concretely, IO leaves the assistant output unconstrained (empty prefix), while CoT prepends the assistant’s response with “Let’s think step by step.” and PS prepends it with “Let’s first understand the problem and devise a plan to solve the problem. Then, let’s carry out the plan to solve the problem step by step.”. Notably, adding CoT or PS explicitly forces a particular reasoning style and thus can override or mask the model’s native reasoning behavior that would otherwise emerge under IO. For each prompt variant, we pair it with Self-Consistency (SC) [18] as our first baseline by sampling 61 independent completions (same prompt with stochastic decoding), post-processing each completion into a candidate answer, and returning the majority-vote answer among the candidates (ties broken deterministically by choosing the alphabetically smallest answer). As another baseline, we include the Self-Discover (SD) [12], adapted to our instance-specific setup. For each test instance, we first prompt the model using only that single unlabeled example to propose a high-level reasoning strategy and decomposition tailored to that instance (e.g., what intermediate checks to perform, what constraints to track, how to verify the final answer). We then turn this into a one-off reasoning template and use it to answer the same instance. For completeness on iLLC, we also compare against Least-to-Most (LtM) prompting, its original CoT baseline [16], and Self-Refine (SR) [19]. For SR, we use instruction-only feedback and refinement prompts for five iterations, since no few-shot exemplars are available for our datasets. We treat these as analysis-only references rather than core baselines, as they rely on labeled exemplars, manually curated reasoning structures, or task-specific refinement prompts. ReHoPER is designed to be prefix-agnostic and compatible with all SC assistant output prefix variants. Concretely, ReHoPER can be combined with {IO, CoT, PS} by simply applying the chosen prefix dur-ing the evaluation stages, yielding composite configurations of the form ReHoPER × {IO, CoT, PS} (e.g., ReHoPER with CoT, etc.). This design allows us to flexibly adopt the prefix that best matches the requirements of a given task, while keeping the underlying reasoning and evaluation procedure unchanged. We use n = 6 reasoning paths, a maximum depth of mmax = 10 steps, and temperature 

0.5. This configuration produces up to 1 + n · mmax = 61 candidate answers per instance (one from the initial evaluation plus up to n · mmax intermediate evaluation outputs), enabling a majority vote analogous to the 61-sample SC baselines. We choose n = 6 and mmax = 10 as a pragmatic trade-off that balances breadth (path diversity) and depth (multi-step refinement). 5 Results and discussion 

5.1 ReHoPER results 

Table 1 compares ReHoPER with SC and SD across all datasets. Across all models, datasets, and prefixes, ReHoPER improves over the SC setup under the same answer-format prefix in a majority of cases, with stronger gains for more structured prefixes. Appendix N shows examples of how ReHoPER generates and uses intermediate questions in both correct and incorrect cases. On average (bottom-right block), ReHoPER improves accuracy by 5.8pp for IO, 8.4pp for CoT, and 6.4pp for PS. These gains are calculated before rounding the accuracies and show that our approach is beneficial even when models already produce explicit chains of thought. Appendix A shows that ReHoPER also improves over SC when each benchmark family is given equal weight, with gains of 3.9pp for IO, 6.6pp for CoT, and 4.8pp for PS. Appendix H shows that these gains are stable across repeated runs. ReHoPER also often recovers and surpasses IO-level performance even when raw CoT or PS lags behind, indicating that the full intermediate-question procedure can help beyond the surface prompt alone. Notably, ReHoPER tends to be strongest and most reliable when paired with PS, making it a suitable default for new tasks where it is unclear which prefix works best. Remarkably, the gains are most pronounced on the hardest, most compositional settings. On the iLLC L2-{w} variants, where the SC baseline performance is low, ReHoPER yields large im-provements, e.g., 14.3pp for IO, 25.4pp for CoT, and 20.9pp for PS on L2-6. 5.2 Effect of replanning and look-ahead 

Table 2 compares ReHoPER with ablations without replanning or look-ahead on MuSR MM, More-HopQA, and iLLC L2-4. These aggregate trends support the combined design, although individual configurations can favor an ablation. 5.4 Paths vs. steps 

Figure 3 compares how ReHoPER+PS and SC+PS improve as more candidate predictions are included in the vote. For ReHoPER, the dashed curves add reasoning steps across six paths, while the dotted curves add paths with a fixed maximum depth of ten steps; the solid curve adds independent SC samples. This comparison shows how the choice between deeper reasoning and more paths affects accuracy as the candidate budget grows. Appendix D gives the exact vote construction and results for other models and output prefixes. ReHoPER outperforms SC in most configurations, often 8from small iteration budgets onward. Across datasets and models, both ReHoPER and SC exhibit diminishing returns as gains per additional step or path become small beyond a certain point. For ReHoPER, one contributing factor is that some paths terminate early, so later steps contribute fewer new candidates. The figure also shows that the relative value of steps versus paths is task dependent. On iLLC, the dotted curves (more paths at fixed depth) usually rise faster than the dashed curves in the first few iterations, indicating that the earliest paths provide the largest marginal benefit; however, they plateau quickly while the dashed curves continue to improve, suggesting that deeper reasoning steps are ultimately more beneficial on this dataset. In contrast, for example, in MuSR with Phi-4, configurations that emphasize more paths can be advantageous, and the upward trend of the SC curve (figuratively, many step-0 paths) is consistent with this behavior.

Table 3 compares ReHoPER+PS with Least-to-Most (LtM) [16], its few-shot CoT baseline, and Self-Refine (SR) [19] on iLLC. For L1, we use the original CoT and LtM prompts in the LtM paper [16]; for L2, we construct analogous prompts following the same template to isolate differences in reasoning rather than prompt design. In SR, feedback and refinement are implemented as few-shot prompts in the original work, but no exemplars exist for our datasets and creating task-specific ones would defeat the purpose of our zero-shot setting. We therefore use instruction-only feedback and refinement prompts, perform five refinement iterations, and take the final refined answer. SR improves over LtM in several cases, but does not match ReHoPER. Our method performs best overall and is particularly stronger on the more compositional L2 variants, where LtM, CoT, and SR degrade sharply. This indicates that while post-hoc refinement helps, explicit planning with intermediate questions is more effective for compositional reasoning even in a task-agnostic, zero-shot setting. 5.6 Compute–accuracy tradeoffs 

Table 4 reports runtime and accuracy on iLLC, with accuracy averaged over IO/CoT/PS. Here, we denote the original SC setup used in the main experiments as SC 61 , and include SC 181 , which increases the sampling budget from 61 to 181 samples to better match ReHoPER’s runtime and number of forward passes. For efficiency, we batch the three prefix-specific conversations in a single inference request for all methods; in ReHoPER, planning and intermediate questions are shared across prefixes, and prefixes are applied only at final prediction. Increasing the sampling budget yields limited gains: consistent with Figure 3, SC 181 is only slightly better and sometimes worse than SC 61 , indicating that sampling more final answers is not the main limitation of Self-Consistency. In contrast, ReHoPER achieves larger improvements by allocating compute to planning and intermediate reasoning, with gains evident on L1 and substantially larger on L2. SC 181 also incurs higher runtime, often exceeding ReHoPER while rarely matching its accuracy. Overall, ReHoPER’s advantage comes from structured computation via receding-horizon planning, not simply more forward passes. 

6 Conclusion and future work 

In this work, we introduced ReHoPER, a task-agnostic, inference-only method that reasons through intermediate questions across multiple paths and aggregates their predictions. It improves over Self-Consistency and other strong baselines under the same answer-format prefix in most settings, 9with the largest gains on harder compositional tasks. Our controlled iLLC benchmark helps expose how performance changes with compositional difficulty, while planning ablations examine design choices within the method. An interesting direction of future work will be to make the compute budget more adaptive (e.g., dynamically increasing the number of paths when some terminate early). Our code and iLLC generator are publicly available on GitHub.

Tables 6–9 summarize the intermediate questions (IQs) produced by ReHoPER and its two ablations. The column #IQ reports the average total number of valid IQs generated per example across all paths, while #IQ/Path reports the corresponding average per path; its subscript is the average standard deviation of the number of IQs across paths for each example. Similarly, #uniqIQ counts the average number of distinct valid IQs across all paths, and #uniqIQ/Path reports the average number of distinct IQs within each path, again with the corresponding across-path standard deviation. Finally, %uniqIQ measures global diversity across paths, i.e., #uniqIQ divided by #IQ, while %uniqIQ/Path measures within-path diversity, i.e., #uniqIQ/Path divided by #IQ/Path. A useful sanity check is that %uniqIQ/Path is essentially 100% for ReHoPER across all datasets in Tables 6 and 7. This is expected because ReHoPER explicitly selects the first candidate IQ that has not already been asked in the current path, and terminates the path if all candidates repeat. The ablation without replanning shows the same pattern in Table 8: although it does not replan, it commits to a single ordered list of IQs at the beginning of each path, and repeated questions within that fixed list are rare. In contrast, the ablation without look-ahead generates only one next IQ at a time without a horizon or selection step, so it can repeatedly ask the same or near-identical questions. Importantly, this repetition happens even though the instruction for the ablation without look-ahead explicitly asks the model not to repeat previously asked questions (Figure 15 in Appendix L). This suggests that prompt-level discouragement alone is not sufficient to reliably enforce non-repetition; the explicit selection mechanism in ReHoPER is what keeps within-path uniqueness at 100% .On the three datasets used for the planning ablations, trajectory lengths and question diversity differ across configurations. The ablation without look-ahead almost always reaches the maximum path length, generating about 60 IQs in total, or 10 IQs per path, because its termination criterion is tied to the step cap rather than the availability of useful new questions. However, this larger number of generated IQs does not necessarily correspond to more distinct questions. On MoreHopQA, the ablation without look-ahead generates 60 .00 IQs on average but only 20 .51 unique IQs, while ReHoPER generates fewer total IQs ( 43 .07 ) but more unique IQs ( 24 .25 ). 100% for ReHoPER, any reduction in %uniqIQ reflects overlap across different paths rather than repetition within a path. To further examine whether ReHoPER simply follows its initial plan or actively adapts its reasoning trajectory, we analyze how often replanning changes the next intermediate question. At each step i,the current plan contains a predicted next question and a “second-next” question. After answering the current question, ReHoPER replans from the updated history. We compare the second-next question from the previous plan with the actual next question selected after replanning. If they differ, we count the transition as replanned. Tables 10 and 11 report the resulting trajectory statistics. For a step pair i → i + 1 , T denotes the average number of instances that are still active and have not terminated by that comparison point, R

denotes the average number of those active instances whose next question changes after replanning, and N denotes the total number of instances in the dataset. Thus, %T /N measures how much of the dataset remains active at that step, %R/T measures how often replanning changes the next question among active instances, and %R/N measures the same change rate relative to the full dataset. The ANY row reports whether an instance has at least one such replanning-induced change across all step pairs. All entries are reported as mean std across paths. Overall, replanning frequently changes the reasoning trajectory rather than merely continuing a fixed plan. This pattern is especially clear on MuSR MM, where nearly all instances remain active across steps and %R/T is consistently high across models. MoreHopQA shows a similar trend among active instances, although %T /N decreases at later steps as more paths terminate. The iLLC results require more nuance. Because iLLC admits a natural word-by-word procedure, once the model starts processing words sequentially, the second-next question from the previous plan often matches the next question regenerated after replanning. This is most visible around steps 2–5, where many trajectories enter this stable extraction template, yielding lower %R/T despite continued replanning. However, this local reduction does not mean that replanning is absent: other steps often still show high replanning rates, and the ANY rows show that most iLLC instances experience at least one replanning-induced change. Thus, lower step-level replanning on some iLLC transitions often reflects preservation of a useful sequential decomposition rather than inactive replanning. Together, these results support the interpretation that ReHoPER’s receding-horizon mechanism repeatedly updates the intermediate-question trajectory as new information is added, while still preserving stable subplans when they remain appropriate. 31 G Hyperparameter optimization 

We perform a stage-wise hyperparameter optimization for ReHoPER. We first tune the inference temperature T ∈ { 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7} while fixing the path/step configuration to 10 − 6. After selecting the best temperature on a development split of iLLC L2-4 with 250 newly sampled instances, we fix this temperature and tune the path/step budget {n}−{ mmax } ∈ { 5−12 , 6−10 , 10 −6, 12 −5}.All configurations are budget-matched to produce at most 61 candidate answers per instance. Finally, we rerun the selected configuration on the MoreHopQA dataset to assess whether the same trends generalize across datasets. Tables 12 and 13 summarize the results. Based on Table 12, we fix the sampling temperature to T = 0 .5. On iLLC L2-4, T = 0 .5 yields the highest average accuracy across all three answer-format prefixes, improving over the best lower temperatures ( T ∈ { 0.2, 0.3}) by roughly 3pp for IO and CoT and about 1pp for PS, and still outperforming the higher temperature T = 0 .7 by around 1-2pp on IO/CoT and about 0.5pp on PS. The same trend largely holds on MoreHopQA: T = 0 .5 remains the best choice for IO and PS, and is the second best setting for CoT, indicating that this temperature transfers well across datasets. For the path/step budget in Table 13, we select the 6 − 10 configuration. On iLLC L2-4, 6 − 10 achieves the best average performance for PS, is tied for best for CoT, and is second-best for IO. On MoreHopQA, 

6 − 10 is again consistently competitive and typically within 1pp of the best configuration across IO, CoT, and PS, even though 10 − 6 occasionally attains the highest score. We therefore adopt 

T = 0 .5 and a 6 − 10 path/step budget as a single, dataset-agnostic configuration for all subsequent experiments. 32 H Robustness across repeated runs 

To assess whether ReHoPER’s gains are robust under stochastic decoding, we repeat each experiment three times and report the mean accuracy with standard deviation in Table 14. Overall, ReHoPER remains stable across runs, with standard deviations generally comparable to those of Self-Consistency (SC). This suggests that the observed improvements are not artifacts of a single favorable sample, but persist across independent decoding runs. The gains are especially pronounced on the harder iLLC L2-4 benchmark. Averaged across models, ReHoPER improves over SC under all answer-format prefixes, increasing accuracy from 8.6 to 22.3 with IO, 8.1 to 24.2 with CoT, and 11.7 to 30.8 with PS. These improvements are consistent across most models and are particularly large for Phi-4, where ReHoPER improves from roughly 10–13% accuracy to more than 54–62% across prefixes, while maintaining low run-to-run variation. On MoreHopQA, where the base SC performance is already substantially higher, the improvements are smaller but still generally positive on average. ReHoPER matches or improves the average SC result under all three prefixes, with the clearest gains under CoT and PS. While a few individual model-prefix combinations show small decreases, the overall pattern indicates that ReHoPER does not rely on a fragile prompting choice and its benefits are strongest in the more compositional iLLC setting and remain stable across repeated runs and answer-format prefixes. MuSR. MuSR evaluates multi-step reasoning over long, story-like inputs where the answer requires integrating scattered clues and applying commonsense inference rather than relying on surface matching [6]. It contains three tasks: Murder Mysteries (MM), where the model identifies the culprit from a narrative; Object Placement (OP), where the model tracks objects as they move or are relocated through a sequence of events; and Team Allocation (TA), where the model infers valid group assignments under narrative constraints. 

MoreHopQA. MoreHopQA is a multi-hop question answering benchmark designed to test whether models can connect multiple evidence pieces across a context and perform sequential reasoning hops to reach the correct answer [5]. We use the 150-example subset selected by the dataset authors, following the original evaluation setup. 

iLLC. iLLC is our controlled diagnostic benchmark for compositional reasoning. It generalizes Last Letter Concatenation (LLC) [10] by requiring the model to extract the i-th-to-last character from each word in a given list and concatenate the resulting letters. We denote subsets as L{ i}-{ w}, where 

i specifies the target character position from the end and w specifies the number of words in the input. For example, L1-4 corresponds to standard last-letter concatenation over four words, while L2-4 requires extracting the second-to-last character from each of four words.

For MoreHopQA and iLLC, we obtain ˆy by extracting the first word that appears immediately after "ANSWER:" .
