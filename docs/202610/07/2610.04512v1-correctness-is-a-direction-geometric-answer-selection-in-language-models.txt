# Correctness Is a Direction: Geometric Answer Selection in Language Models

# Abstract 

Answer correctness is encoded as a recoverable geometric direction in the hidden states of language models. We show that the mean displacement from incorrect to correct answer representations, computed at approximately 70% of model depth from fifty labeled examples with no parameter updates, yields a scoring direction that outperforms zero-shot log-probability scoring by up to +32.0 percentage points on factual benchmarks (ARC-Challenge and MMLU) and by +38.1 to +51.8 percentage points on TruthfulQA, across five models spanning 1B to 8B parameters in three architecture families (Llama, Qwen, Gemma). The method requires one forward pass and one dot product per candidate; no generation is performed at inference. Applied as a hallucination detector on individual (question, answer) pairs, the recovered direction achieves 0.693 AUROC versus 0.578 for log-probability scoring. We additionally find that correctness directions for factual reasoning, domain knowledge, and calibrated truthfulness are near-orthogonal in representation space, revealing that language models allocate geometrically independent subspaces to qualitatively distinct notions of correct answer, with architecture-dependent variation in the degree of separation. This structure explains the observed transfer pattern—the direction calibrated on factual questions transfers within task type but not across it—and suggests that LLM calibration failures may reflect a routing problem: the model’s internal representation contains more correctness signal than its output behaviour exploits. 

# 1 Introduction 

Language models internally distinguish correct from incorrect answers, yet this signal rarely influ-ences their outputs. When a model answers a factual question incorrectly, its hidden representation at the layer encoding semantic content differs from the representation on a correct answer in a consistent, recoverable direction. The standard autoregressive generation pipeline ignores this distinction entirely, selecting outputs token-by-token based on next-token probability rather than any direct measure of semantic correctness [Brown et al., 2020, Ouyang et al., 2022]. The result is well-documented: large language models (LLMs) hallucinate with high surface confidence, and users lack lightweight tools to identify unreliable outputs without expensive secondary inference [Lin et al., 2022, Ji et al., 2022]. A growing body of work demonstrates that high-level semantic properties are linearly encoded in LLM hidden representations [Alain and Bengio, 2018, Belinkov, 2022, Park et al., 2024]. Zou et al. [2025] showed that directions corresponding to abstract concepts—honesty, harm avoidance, emotional valence—can be extracted from hidden states and used to steer model behavior without fine-

tuning, suggesting that diverse semantic properties leave consistent linear traces in the representation space. Whether answer correctness specifically—the distinction between what a model should say versus what it will say—is similarly encoded as a recoverable geometric direction in single-model hidden states has not been established. We investigate this question directly. We find that it is, and that the choice of extraction method matters critically. A contrastive 

displacement—the mean difference between correct-answer representations and wrong-answer repre-sentations, averaged over just fifty question-answer triples—recovers a direction that dramatically outperforms zero-shot generation as a scoring mechanism, with no parameter updates and no gen-eration at inference time. The contrastive formulation proves essential. The standard displacement (correct-answer representations versus no-answer representations) yields high signal-to-noise ratio but low discriminative accuracy, because it encodes answer-ness rather than correct-answer-ness —a dis-sociation we characterize explicitly and which prior work on semantic directions has not distinguished. At inference, scoring a candidate answer requires a single forward pass and a dot product. Examining directions recovered across multiple task types reveals an unexpected structural property: 

task-orthogonality . Directions calibrated on ARC-Challenge [Clark et al., 2018], MMLU [Hendrycks et al., 2021], and TruthfulQA [Lin et al., 2022] are near-orthogonal in both Qwen-2.5-1.5B and Llama-3.2-1B (pairwise cosine similarities |cos θ| < 0.15 ), with partial alignment in Gemma-2-2B. Correctness is not a single geometric concept in LLM representations but a structured set of independent, task-specific directions. This accounts for an otherwise anomalous transfer pattern: a direction calibrated on ARC transfers to same-domain MMLU subjects (+6 to +20 pp above zero-shot) but fails to transfer to TruthfulQA, not because the method is brittle, but because the model allocates factual correctness and calibrated truthfulness to geometrically independent subspaces. Evaluated across five models spanning 1B to 8B parameters in three architecture families (Llama, Qwen, Gemma), with an apples-to-apples zero-shot log-probability baseline computed on identical held-out test examples, geometric scoring via our contrastive direction outperforms zero-shot by up to +32.0 percentage points on factual benchmarks and by +38.1 to +51.8 percentage points 

on TruthfulQA. On TruthfulQA—where zero-shot baselines range from 28.7% to 39.5%— task-specific geometric scoring achieves 72.1%–81.2% with fifty calibration examples and zero gradient computation. Applied as a hallucination detector in a binary classification setting, the recovered direction achieves 0.693 AUROC versus 0.578 AUROC for log-probability scoring, a substantial gain from a signal that costs one dot product per candidate at inference.

Linear representations and probing. A substantial literature establishes that semantic properties are linearly encoded in the hidden states of neural language models [Alain and Bengio, 2018, Belinkov, 2022]. Probing classifiers—linear models trained on top of frozen representations to predict some property of the input—have been used to recover syntactic structure [Hewitt and Manning, 2019], factual knowledge [Meng et al., 2022], and sentiment across a wide range of architectures [Belinkov, 2022]. The linear representation hypothesis formalises this empirically: features corresponding to semantic concepts are not merely recoverable by linear methods but are themselves approximately linearly structured [Park et al., 2024, Elhage et al., 2022]. Our work differs from the probing paradigm in a fundamental respect: we train no classifier. The correctness direction is computed as a mean displacement over fifty labeled examples and applied via a single dot product, requiring no optimisation. 

Directions in hidden states for truth and honesty. Several recent works extract directional signals from LLM representations that correlate with truth or honesty without relying on external verifiers. Burns et al. [2024] recover a latent truth direction without supervision by exploiting the logical consistency of statement-negation pairs, showing that models internally represent factual status even when they generate the incorrect answer. Marks and Tegmark [2024] extend this, demonstrating that truth exhibits emergent linear structure across model families and that the direction generalises across distinct fact domains. Zou et al. [2025] extract directions for abstract concepts including honesty and use them to steer model outputs at inference time; Li et al. [2024] similarly extract truthfulness directions from attention heads and intervene on the forward pass to elicit calibrated responses on TruthfulQA. Orgad et al. [2025] find that probing classifiers trained on hidden states are skill-specific: probes transfer within similar task types (e.g., factual retrieval) but fail across qualitatively different tasks, leading them to conclude that LLMs encode multiple distinct notions of truthfulness rather than a universal mechanism. Our work is closest to this line in motivation, but contributes three advances not present in prior work. First, we introduce a contrastive formulation that targets correct-answer-ness rather than abstract honesty or binary truth, and show this is strictly necessary: the standard displacement encodes answer-ness and is dramatically less discriminative—a distinction prior work on semantic directions has not made. Second, we provide a geometric account of skill-specificity by measuring cosine similarity between task-specific directions and showing near-orthogonality, which explains why probes fail to transfer without requiring trained classifiers; Orgad et al. [2025] observe the failure empirically but do not characterise its geometric cause. Third, unlike steering approaches [Zou et al., 2025, Li et al., 2024] that modify the forward pass during generation, we score candidates without generation, and show the mean direction matches a trained 50-sample logistic regression within 2.1 pp—confirming the mean captures the full discriminative signal available from fifty examples without any optimisation. 

Hallucination detection and confidence estimation. Detecting hallucinations in generated text has attracted considerable attention [Ji et al., 2022]. Consistency-based methods generate multiple responses and measure inter-sample agreement, either lexically [Manakul et al., 2023] or seman-tically [Kuhn et al., 2023]; both require many additional forward passes. Kadavath et al. [2022] prompt the model to evaluate its own outputs, finding that self-assessment is partially calibrated but requires additional generation. Methods that use hidden states directly for hallucination detection have emerged more recently [Chen et al., 2024, Chuang et al., 2023], but typically require training on hallucination-labelled data or specialised decoding procedures. Our hallucination detector requires neither: a direction calibrated on fifty general question-answer triples produces 0.693 AUROC on binary correct/incorrect classification, substantially above the log-probability baseline, with one dot product per candidate. 

Answer selection and scoring. For multiple-choice tasks, log-probability scoring—assigning each candidate the log-probability the model assigns to its tokens given the question [Brown et al., 2020]—is the standard zero-shot baseline and forms our primary comparison throughout. Self-consistency [Wang et al., 2023] improves factual accuracy by sampling many chain-of-thought responses and taking the majority answer, but requires tens of generation calls per question. Minimum Bayes Risk decoding [Eikema and Aziz, 2020] similarly selects the candidate with highest similarity to the candidate pool, incurring heavy generation costs. Our method scores candidates from a single 3forward pass per candidate with no generation, and outperforms log-probability scoring by up to +32.0 pp on factual benchmarks and by +38.1 to +51.8 pp on TruthfulQA.

3.2 Layer Depth and Extraction Point 

The representational content of LLM hidden states varies substantially across depth. Early layers process syntactic structure; later layers encode increasingly abstract semantic content [Tenney et al., 2019, Belinkov, 2022]. We evaluated contrastive direction extraction at seven fractional depths (0.60, 0.65, 0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90) across all model families, with layer selection performed on a 20% held-out validation split to avoid test-set contamination. Results are shown in Figure 1. 

Universal optimality at 65–70% depth. Qwen-2.5-1.5B (28 layers) and Llama-3.1-8B (32 layers) peak at fraction 0.65; Gemma-2-2B (26 layers) and Qwen-2.5-7B (32 layers) peak at fraction 0.70; Llama-3.2-1B (16 layers) peaks at fraction 0.60 (absolute layer 9), which corresponds to 56% of depth but lies within one layer of the 65% point given the coarser granularity of a 16-layer model. Across all five models the optimal extraction depth falls between absolute layers corresponding to 56–70% of total depth, despite architectures ranging from 16 to 32 layers and 1B to 8B parameters. We interpret this as evidence of a consistent representational transition in this depth range where models shift from encoding syntactic and shallow semantic features to encoding answer-quality-relevant geometry. 

Standard direction underperforms zero-shot at shallow depth. At the selected layer, the standard direction underperforms zero-shot log-probability for all three sub-3B models (Table 1). For Gemma-2-2B at fraction 0.70, the standard direction achieves only 27.6% versus 44.4% zero-shot—a −16 .8 pp degradation. This confirms that the standard direction encodes answer-ness rather than correct-answer-ness even at the semantically richest extraction depth: appending any text to a question moves hidden states in a direction that is not only non-discriminative but actively anti-correlated with correct-answer selection at these depths. The contrastive direction at the same layer achieves 66.5% (+22.1 pp above zero-shot), confirming that subtracting the wrong-answer representations isolates the quality-relevant component. 

Llama convergence at fraction 0.75. As noted in Section 3.1, at layer 12 of Llama, the standard and contrastive directions converge to cosine similarity 0.99, and both yield their highest accuracy for this model (58.0% and 53.2%). The convergence suggests that at this specific depth the model has organised its representations such that answer-ness and correct-answer-ness are nearly identical—a property that holds at layer 12 but not at layer 11 (0.70) or layer 13 (0.80, which maps to the same layer for Llama). We note that this convergence is architecture-specific: it does not occur for Qwen or Gemma at any tested depth.

# 4 Task-Specific Geometric Structure 

The experiments in Section 3 calibrate a direction on ARC-Challenge and evaluate it on ARC-Challenge. We now ask what happens when we move the calibration source, the evaluation task, or both. The answer reveals a structured geometric property of LLM representations that we call 

task-orthogonality : directions encoding correctness for different task types occupy near-independent subspaces, with partial exceptions that are themselves informative.

4.2 Transfer Within and Across Task Types 

The cosine similarity data predicts a specific transfer pattern: directions should transfer between tasks whose geometric directions are aligned, and fail—or invert—between tasks whose directions are orthogonal or anti-correlated. Table 3 tests this prediction directly by evaluating an ARC-calibrated direction on six target benchmarks spanning three semantic categories. 

Factual tasks: positive transfer. The ARC direction transfers positively to all factual multiple-choice tasks for Qwen and Gemma. MMLU-Biology receives +19 .7–+20 .4 pp above zero-shot from the ARC direction, comparable to its own-calibration result ( +7 .7 pp for Gemma, +0 .0 pp for Qwen). The ARC direction is a more effective calibration source than MMLU’s own validation split, which we attribute to calibration quality rather than task mismatch: ARC’s train split provides clean, unambiguous science questions with clearly correct and clearly wrong distractors, whereas MMLU’s small validation splits (10–30 examples per subject) contain borderline examples that add noise to the direction estimate. World History, which is semantically distant from ARC’s science focus, still benefits substantially ( +13 .5–+22 .4 pp), consistent with a direction that captures general factual correctness rather than domain-specific knowledge. 

Coherence tasks: negative transfer. HellaSwag, a sentence-completion benchmark requiring commonsense coherence rather than factual retrieval, shows strongly negative transfer for all three models ( −11 .0 to −26 .7 pp). The ARC direction actively degrades performance below zero-shot. When calibrated on HellaSwag directly, the own direction achieves +6 .3 pp (Qwen) and −4.7 pp (Gemma) relative to zero-shot, confirming that HellaSwag accuracy is not well-served by geometric scoring in general at this calibration size. We interpret this as evidence that the geometric signal for commonsense coherence is orthogonal—or slightly anti-correlated—to the geometric signal for factual correctness. Completing a sentence naturally requires fluency and world-model consistency, which may be encoded in different representational subspaces than declarative fact retrieval. 

Calibration tasks: near-zero ARC transfer, large own-direction gains. TruthfulQA tests whether models produce calibrated, honest answers rather than confidently incorrect ones. The ARC direction yields modest gains under argmax scoring ( +4 .8–+12 .9 pp), but the TruthfulQA-calibrated direction under argmax scoring yields +38 .1–+51 .8 pp—the largest single-task improvement in our experi-ments. This confirms that TruthfulQA has a distinct correctness direction that is only weakly aligned with the ARC direction in Qwen and Llama (cosine |<0.09 |) and partially aligned in Gemma (cosine 

+0 .302 ). Importantly, the TruthfulQA direction does not require argmin scoring: the contrastive formulation, computed on TruthfulQA (correct, wrong) pairs, extracts a direction that already points toward calibrated answers rather than overconfident ones. 84.3 Multi-Dimensional Correctness 

The transfer results are parsimoniously explained by a simple model: LLMs allocate geometrically independent subspaces to qualitatively distinct notions of answer quality. We identify three such dimensions in the tasks studied: • Factual correctness — whether a statement corresponds to a retrievable fact. Encoded in the ARC and MMLU directions; these are moderately correlated (cosine +0 .04 –+0 .31 ) and transfer positively within the category. • Commonsense coherence — whether a sentence completion is plausible and grammatically natural. Encoded in a direction approximately orthogonal or anti-correlated to factual correctness, as evidenced by HellaSwag’s negative transfer. • Calibrated truthfulness — whether a model’s answer reflects appropriate epistemic con-fidence. Encoded in the TruthfulQA direction, near-orthogonal to factual correctness for Qwen and Llama. This taxonomy does not preclude further subdivision: MMLU’s 57 subjects likely contain finer-grained geometric structure within the factual category. Nor does it preclude overlap: the partial Gemma alignment between ARC and TruthfulQA suggests the boundaries between categories are not crisp for all architectures. What the data establish is that the single-direction assumption—that one universal “correctness” direction covers all task types—is inconsistent with the geometric structure of the models we tested, and that the boundaries between categories can be estimated from just fifty calibration examples per task type. 

# 5 Geometric Answer Selection 

We evaluate the full geometric scoring pipeline across five benchmarks and three model families. Section 3 described the contrastive direction extraction and scoring procedure; we apply it here using task-specific calibration—50 examples drawn from each target task’s training split—except for MMLU subjects, where the ARC-Challenge calibration set transfers more effectively than MMLU’s own small validation splits (see Section 4.2). 

5.1 Experimental Setup Models. We evaluate five instruction-tuned models spanning three architecture families: Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (16 layers, d=2 ,048 ) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (32 layers, d=4 ,096 ) [Grattafiori et al., 2024]; Qwen-2.5-1.5B-Instruct (28 layers, d=1 ,536 ) and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct (32 layers, 

d=3 ,584 ) [Qwen et al., 2025]; and Gemma-2-2B-IT (26 layers, d=2 ,304 ) [Team et al., 2024]. All models are used frozen; no parameters are updated at any stage. 

Benchmarks. 5.3 Analysis Model size and performance. Larger models produce stronger geometric signals. Contrastive SNR increases monotonically with scale within each family: Llama-1B ( 8.1×) to Llama-8B ( 11 .2×); Qwen-1.5B ( 8.4×) to Qwen-7B ( 14 .9×). Accuracy follows the same trend on ARC-Challenge: Qwen-1.5B to Qwen-7B improves from 61.1% to 80.0% (+18.9 pp absolute); Llama-1B to Llama-8B improves from 44.7% to 59.0% (+14.3 pp). On TruthfulQA the pattern differs: Llama-8B’s gain (+51.7 pp) exceeds Llama-1B’s (+43.4 pp), but Qwen-7B (+41.7 pp) does not exceed Qwen-1.5B (+41.9 pp). This asymmetry is consistent with the finding that smaller models are more poorly calibrated on average [Kadavath et al., 2022]: the geometric direction recovers signal that log-probability scoring loses, and the magnitude of recovery depends on how miscalibrated the output probabilities already are, not only on model scale. 

Task type determines the magnitude of improvement. Factual tasks receive +5 to +22 pp; TruthfulQA receives +38 to +52 pp. The difference is not primarily a function of benchmark difficulty but of how well zero-shot log-probability already exploits the model’s internal knowledge. On factual multiple-choice tasks, log-probability scoring is already partially effective (baselines of 38–46%), and the geometric direction provides an additional signal. On TruthfulQA, log-probability is actively misleading (baselines near chance), and the geometric direction provides most of the discriminative signal. 

Calibration specificity. The strong TruthfulQA results depend on using a TruthfulQA-calibrated direction: when the ARC direction is used instead, TruthfulQA gains are only +4.8 to +12.9 pp (Section 4.2). This confirms that the 50-example calibration step is not a minor tuning detail but the primary mechanism by which the method adapts to the semantic structure of the target task. The practical workflow implied by these results is: calibrate a direction on 50 task-specific examples (five minutes on a consumer GPU), then score candidates with a dot product at inference time. 

# 6 Hallucination Detection 

The preceding sections evaluate geometric scoring in a selection setting: a fixed set of candidate answers is provided and the method picks one. 6.1 Setup 

We treat every (question, answer) pair in the ARC-Challenge test set as an independent classification instance. For each of the 500 test questions with four candidate answers, we assign label 1 to the correct answer and label 0 to each of the three incorrect answers, yielding 1,999 labelled pairs (500 correct, 1,499 incorrect). Each pair is scored by its GRM projection s = ˆδ⊤˜hℓ(Q, A ) using the ARC-calibrated direction from Section 3. The zero-shot log-probability score serves as the baseline classifier. Evaluation uses Area Under the ROC Curve (AUROC), which measures P (score correct > score incorrect )

across all possible correct/incorrect pairings without requiring a threshold to be chosen. AUROC is threshold-free and unaffected by the class imbalance (1:3 correct-to-incorrect ratio). 

6.2 Results 

Table 5 shows AUROC results; Figure 4 shows the score distributions and resulting ROC curves. The GRM direction achieves 0.693 AUROC, compared to 0.578 for zero-shot log-probability—an 11.5 pp gap in AUROC, representing a 2.5 × improvement in discriminative signal above the random baseline. The score distributions in Figure 4(a) show why: correct answers project to a higher mean score ( μ=+0 .0255 ) than incorrect answers ( μ=−0.0084 ), a separation of 0.034 score units across 12 standard deviations of approximately 0.048 . The distributions substantially overlap—the method is not a reliable stand-alone detector—but the systematic directionality is consistent enough to provide a meaningful signal. The score separation is modest but consistent across the full test set: correct answers score higher than all three of their incorrect alternatives on 69.3% of questions, compared to 33.3% expected at random. Full threshold-level operating point analysis (precision, recall, and false positive rate at multiple decision thresholds) is provided in Appendix D. 

6.3 Scope: Structured Candidates vs. Free-form Generation 

The hallucination detection result—and the selection results in Section 5—rely on a shared property: the candidate answer is presented to the model in the same prompt format as the calibration examples, 

"Question: {Q}\nAnswer: {A}" . When this format assumption breaks, the method degrades. We tested GRM reranking in an open-domain generation setting using TriviaQA [Joshi et al., 2017]: for each question, the model generates five candidate answers via greedy decoding plus temperature sampling ( T =0 .8), scores them with the ARC-calibrated direction, and selects the highest-scoring candidate. The result was a −0.7 pp change in exact-match accuracy relative to greedy decoding alone (35.3% vs. 36.0%), compared to an oracle ceiling of +10 .7 pp (46.7%) if the best candidate could be perfectly identified. GRM reranking recovered −6% of the oracle ceiling—a clear null result. The failure is attributable to format mismatch. ARC-Challenge calibration pairs consist of short, well-formed declarative answers (“ Photosynthesis ” or “ The mitochondria ”). TriviaQA generation produces variable-length natural-language responses (“ The answer to that question is the Battle of Hastings, which took place in... ”). The representation at the final token of a long generated response is not in the same region of the hidden-state space as the representation at the final token of a structured calibration answer. The calibrated direction ˆδ therefore loses its discriminative meaning when applied to out-of-format text. The straightforward remedy is format-matched calibration: calibrate ˆδ using examples whose answer format matches the expected generation format. If generated answers are consistently short (e.g., from constrained decoding), or if the generation prompt is identical in structure to the calibration prompt, reranking should recover effectiveness. We leave systematic evaluation of this fix to future work. The practical boundary of the method is therefore: geometric scoring is effective for structured candidate selection (multiple-choice evaluation, retrieval reranking with structured candidates) and single-answer hallucination detection when the answer format is consistent with calibration. It is not a drop-in replacement for generation-time scoring of arbitrary free-form outputs. 

# 7 Discussion 

7.1 Why Fifty Examples Suffice 

The direction estimate saturates rapidly. At N =50 , standard direction SNR is already 27–60 ×

across all five models, and contrastive SNR is 8–15 ×. Doubling the calibration set to N =100 

decreases SNR in every tested configuration and does not improve downstream accuracy. Two mechanisms likely contribute. First, the per-example displacement vectors are highly consistent: a SNR of 8–11 × at N =50 means the mean direction already dominates the within-calibration variance. Additional examples provide little new information about the direction’s orientation. Second, larger calibration sets drawn from a fixed benchmark’s training split increasingly include borderline examples—questions where the correct-vs-incorrect distinction is less geometrically clear—which marginally diffuse the mean. The saturation has a direct implication for deployment. Calibrating a direction requires approximately 250 forward passes ( 50 × 5, as detailed in Section 3) and completes in under five minutes on a consumer GPU. Recalibration for a new task or domain costs the same. This makes the method viable in settings where per-task adaptation is needed but fine-tuning is not: a practitioner with fifty labeled 13 examples can calibrate a direction, score candidates with a dot product, and discard the calibration set entirely. 

7.2 Layer Universality as a Representational Finding 

The convergence of the optimal extraction depth to approximately 65–70% of model depth across five models differing in architecture, scale, and training procedure supports a functional account of transformer depth. The validation-selected optimal layers span absolute depths of 56–70% of total depth: Llama-3.2-1B peaks at fraction 0.60 (layer 9 of 16), Qwen-2.5-1.5B and Llama-3.1-8B at fraction 0.65 (layers 18 and 21 of 28 and 32), and Gemma-2-2B and Qwen-2.5-7B at fraction 0.70 (layers 18 and 22 of 26 and 32). The finding is consistent with a functional account of transformer depth: early layers process token identity, positional, and syntactic information; middle layers integrate context into semantic representations; late layers compress representations toward output distributions [Tenney et al., 2019, Belinkov, 2022]. The correctness direction is most recoverable at the depth where semantic content is fully integrated but before the final compression to a next-token distribution discards geometric structure in favour of the output vocabulary projection. The early-layer inversion observed for Llama at fraction 0.60—the shallowest tested depth, and the validation-selected optimum for Llama-3.2-1B—where the standard direction is anti-correlated with correctness, consistent with this account: at layer nine of sixteen, representations still encode surface-level answer properties rather than answer quality. The contrastive direction is unaffected because subtracting wrong-answer representations cancels the shallow-layer confound, isolating only the quality-relevant component. 

7.3 Multi-Dimensional Correctness in LLM Representations 

The near-orthogonality of correctness directions across task types is the most theoretically significant finding in this paper. If language models encoded a single unified concept of answer quality, the directions calibrated on factual questions (ARC), domain questions (MMLU), and calibrated truthfulness questions (TruthfulQA) would be aligned. Instead, for Qwen and Llama they are nearly orthogonal ( | cos θ| < 0.16 ), implying that the model has allocated independent geometric dimensions to qualitatively distinct notions of what it means to be correct. This suggests a richer internal taxonomy than the uniform surface behaviour of LLMs implies. A model that generates a confident wrong answer on TruthfulQA is not failing to represent truthfulness internally; it may be failing to route its internal calibration signal through to the output distribution. The geometric direction captures that internal signal before the routing failure occurs. Equivalently, the gap between zero-shot log-probability performance (28.7–39.5% on TruthfulQA) and geometric scoring (72–81%) quantifies how much of the model’s internal correctness signal is lost in the generation step. Gemma-2-2B and Qwen-2.5-7B both show partial ARC ↔TQA alignment ( +0 .302 and +0 .166 

respectively), while Llama-3.1-8B shows the strongest orthogonality of any tested model (max 

| cos θ|=0 .10 ) despite being the largest. The divergence within the Qwen family—near-zero at 1.5B, partial at 7B— and between Llama-8B and Gemma-2B despite similar scale suggests that architectural and training-data factors interact in determining the granularity of geometric task separation. Disentangling these factors would require controlled ablations across a broader range of model families and training procedures, which we leave to future work. 

7.4 Relationship to Probing and Representation Engineering 

Our method shares surface similarity with probing classifiers and with Representation Engineer-ing [Zou et al., 2025] but differs in important ways. Probing trains a supervised classifier (typically a linear model) on frozen representations to predict a property [Alain and Bengio, 2018, Belinkov, 2022]. Our method trains nothing: it computes a mean displacement vector from fifty examples and uses it as a fixed scoring direction. The distinction is not merely computational. A trained probe can overfit to spurious features in the calibration set; a mean displacement cannot fit to anything beyond the mean direction. The method’s simplicity is a feature: the direction has an interpretable geometric meaning (the average displacement from wrong-answer to correct-answer representations) that a probe’s weight vector does not. 14 Representation Engineering uses contrast-pair directions to steer model behaviour by adding the direction vector to hidden states during generation [Zou et al., 2025]. We use a similar direction extraction procedure but apply the result as a scoring signal rather than a steering intervention, which means no modification to the model’s forward pass is required at inference. Two contributions are distinct from prior work in this line. First, we demonstrate that the contrastive formulation (correct vs. wrong answers, Eq. 2) is necessary for discriminative accuracy, while the standard formulation (correct vs. no answer, Eq. 1) encodes answer-ness rather than correct-answer-ness—a dissociation the Representation Engineering literature does not distinguish. Second, we characterise the task-specific geometric structure of these directions for the first time, finding task-orthogonality that explains when and why transfer across tasks succeeds or fails. Burns et al. [2024] extract a truth direction without any labels using logical consistency constraints; we use fifty labeled examples per task and achieve substantially larger accuracy improvements, suggesting the additional supervision efficiently concentrates the direction on task-relevant correctness rather than generic truth-likeness. 

Limitations. The experiments cover three architecture families, all instruction-tuned, with models ranging from 1B to 8B parameters. Whether the findings generalise to base (non-instruction-tuned) models, models above 8B, or non-English tasks is unknown. The method requires a fixed candidate set, ruling out direct application to free-form generation without format-matched calibration (Section 6.3). Fifty labeled calibration examples are required per task; while this is cheap relative to fine-tuning, it is not zero-shot in the strict sense. 

# 8 Conclusion 

We have shown that language models encode answer correctness as a structured geometric property of their hidden representations, recoverable from fifty labeled examples with no parameter updates. A contrastive displacement—the mean difference between correct-answer and incorrect-answer repre-sentations at approximately 65–70% of model depth— serves as a scoring direction that outperforms zero-shot log-probability by up to +32.0 percentage points on factual benchmarks and by +38.0 to +51.7 percentage points on TruthfulQA, across five models spanning 1B to 8B parameters in three architecture families. The extraction requires one forward pass per candidate and one dot product at inference; no generation, sampling, or secondary model is involved. A logistic regression probe trained on the identical fifty calibration examples matches this accuracy within 2.1 pp across all tested configurations, confirming that the mean direction captures the full discriminative information available without optimisation. The theoretical contribution is the characterisation of task-orthogonality: correctness directions calibrated on factual reasoning, domain knowledge, and calibrated truthfulness are near-orthogonal across four of five tested models ( | cos θ| < 0.17 for all task pairs in Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen-2.5-1.5B, and Llama-3.1-8B; Llama-3.1-8B shows the strongest separation of any tested model with max 

| cos θ|=0 .10 ). Partial alignment in Gemma-2-2B and Qwen-2.5-7B suggests the degree of geometric separation varies with architecture family and scale. LLMs do not maintain a single concept of “correct answer” but allocate geometrically independent subspaces to qualitatively distinct notions of answer quality. The practical consequence is that the 50-example calibration step is not a minor tuning detail but the mechanism by which the method adapts to the semantic structure of each task. A striking implication of the TruthfulQA results deserves emphasis. The gap between zero-shot log-probability (28.7–39.5%) and geometric scoring (72.1–81.2%) on TruthfulQA does not arise because models lack knowledge of calibrated truthfulness; it arises because the generation process fails to route that internal signal to the output distribution. Geometric scoring recovers correctness information that next-token prediction discards, and the magnitude of recovery scales with how miscalibrated the output probabilities already are—not only with model size. Several directions follow naturally from this work. he probe parity result—mean direction matches trained classifier within 2.1 pp—suggests that the discriminative structure is concentrated in a low-dimensional subspace. A natural next step is a small model ( ∼30 M parameters) trained explicitly with a contrastive geometric objective, which would eliminate the inference cost of the full model forward pass for candidate scoring. A second direction is multi-direction scoring: learning scalar coefficients to combine task-specific directions, enabling a single calibrated scorer to generalise across tasks that require different notions of correctness. Third, extending the method to format-matched free-form 15 generation would close the gap between structured candidate selection and the open-ended settings where hallucination detection is most practically valuable.

# D Hallucination Detection: Threshold Operating Points 

Table 13 reports precision, recall, and false positive rate at five operating points for the GRM hallucination detector, computed analytically from Gaussian fits to the empirical score distributions (μcorrect =+0 .0255 , σ=0 .0478 ; μwrong =−0.0084 , σ=0 .0483 ; fit validated by theoretical AUROC 0.691 vs. empirical 0.693). Precision accounts for the 1:3 correct-to-incorrect class ratio in ARC. Note: the GRM score is a ranking signal, not a calibrated probability. Precision is high (81–90%) because the 3:1 wrong-to-correct class ratio means the majority of low-scoring answers are genuinely incorrect. The practical operating trade-off is between TPR (fraction of wrong answers caught) and FPR (fraction of correct answers wrongly flagged); both rise monotonically with the threshold. At 

t=0 , 56.9% of wrong answers are flagged at a false positive rate of 29.7%, with 85.2% precision on the flagged set.
