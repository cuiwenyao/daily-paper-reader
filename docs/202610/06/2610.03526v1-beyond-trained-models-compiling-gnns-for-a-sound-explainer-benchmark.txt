ABSTRACT
Explainers for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are commonly evaluated by their plausibility, i.e.,
how well their explanations recover a predefined ground truth, such as a motif planted in the data. This
protocol implicitly assumes that a GNN trained on such data relies on the intended motif. Although
prior work has questioned this assumption, plausibility remains widespread. First, we show that
the assumption is violated on several widely used benchmarks, where, e.g., degree statistics alone
suffice to solve the task. Then, we remove this confounder by replacing training with compilation.
We achieve this by introducing Gracr, the first compiler translating graded modal logic formulas into
GNN weights, yielding models that replicate the behaviour of the corresponding formulas. Since the
behaviour of the model is now known by construction, we can define its ground truth explanation
formally and compute it exactly. Building on this, we introduce GracrBench, a benchmark of
compiled GNNs for the evaluation of explainers against this exact ground truth. Experiments on
eleven explainers across six tasks show its effectiveness for fine-grained diagnostic evaluation: notably,
we discover that most explainers are not robust to indirect influences or alternative implementations
of the same formula. These results position GracrBench as a novel, rigorous evaluation setting for
graph post-hoc explainability.

Introduction
Explaining why a Machine Learning (ML) model produces a particular output is a core challenge for deploying these
models in the real world. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) [Scarselli et al., 2008, Bronstein et al., 2021] are no exception,
and a rich literature has emerged to develop methods for explaining such models [Yuan et al., 2022, Amara et al.,
2022, Longa et al., 2025]. One of the most widely used techniques for evaluating the quality of GNN explainers is
plausibility2, i.e., how well the explanation recovers a “ground-truth” pattern. It follows this blueprint: First, identify or
create a dataset with a known discriminative pattern, e.g., graphs containing a house-like motif versus graphs containing
a cycle-like motif; then, train a GNN on this dataset; last, evaluate how well the explanations produced by the explainer
under evaluation recover the known discriminative pattern.
Previous work has already identified shortcomings of this blueprint [Faber et al., 2021, Himmelhuber et al., 2021,
Agarwal et al., 2023, Fontanesi et al., 2024]. Yet, despite these findings, our extensive literature review (see Fig. 1)
shows that plausibility remains widely used: strikingly, roughly 25% of all 155 papers we surveyed adopt plausibility
as the only criterion for evaluating explanations. The fundamental issue is that the trained GNN may learn other,

In our work, we first extend previous findings by identifying specific,
widely used datasets where the above shortcoming is likely to occur,
and then propose a new strategy for evaluating plausibility that re-
places GNN training with a compilation step. We begin by identifying
a pattern of interest, represented as a Boolean logic formula. Then,
we introduce a compiler to transform this logic formula into a GNN
that, by construction, shares the same behaviour and explanations
as the formula. This raises the question of what the “ground truth”
explanation of a formula actually is. Common practice answers with
a designated motif, but already for simple patterns – such as a node
having more red than blue neighbours – several subgraphs justify the
prediction equally well. We therefore need a notion of ground truth
that generalizes beyond simple motifs and accounts for alternative
explanations. To do so, we borrow from formal XAI the definition
of prime implicant [Darwiche and Hirth, 2020, Marques-Silva and
Ignatiev, 2022] – i.e., a minimal subgraph that alone suffices to de-
termine the prediction – and take the union of all prime implicants as
the ground truth explanation. Finally, the explainer is evaluated based
on how well its explanations recover such ground truth. Crucially,
the compilation step allows us to control exactly the behaviour of
the GNN, as it is equivalent to the logic formula from which it was
derived: effects that are usually inextricably confounded on a trained
model can be studied in controlled experiments, allowing specific
hypotheses to be tested rigorously.
Summarizing, our contributions are as follows:
• We show that popular benchmarks are not a reliable test bed for evaluating plausibility, as the GNN over which
explainers are evaluated may not behave as expected.
• We propose a compiler capable of translating a logic formula into the weights of a GNN. This yields GNNs where
the exact behaviour is known and controllable, addressing the issue above.
• To derive a principled notion of what characterizes a ground truth explanation, we build on formal XAI and adopt
prime implicants, and develop an efficient procedure to extract them.
• We exploit this compiler to propose a new, sound benchmark for evaluating plausibility. Our experiments recover
previously known findings while uncovering new ones, exposing limitations of explainers in understudied scenarios
beyond motif-based tasks.

Background
Notation. A graph is a tuple G = (V, E) with nodes V and directed edges E ⊆V × V, where an edge e is an ordered
pair of nodes e = (u, v). Throughout, a subgraph R ⊆G is a spanning subgraph obtained from G by retaining all
nodes in V and a subset of its edges. We write E(u, v) for the predicate holding iff (u, v) ∈E, and N(v) for the
neighbours of node v. We focus on binary classification tasks: a graph classifier is a function g which maps an input
graph into {0, 1}; a node classifier, instead, maps each node of a graph into {0, 1}.
Problem statement. A graph explainer is a function F mapping a classifier and an input graph to an explanation, i.e.,
a vector of edge importance scores F(g, G) = µ ∈R|E|, with µe the score of edge e. For node classification, F also
takes the node to explain. The ground truth explanation is a binary mask µ∗∈{0, 1}|E|. Finally, a plausibility score is
any scoring function – like accuracy or variants thereof – between the predicted explanation µ and the ground truth µ∗,
i.e., sim(µ, µ∗) ∈[0, 1] [Yuan et al., 2022, Agarwal et al., 2023]. The choice of µ∗is crucial but often heuristic. Next,
we describe cases in which an inappropriate choice can make plausibility misleading.

Issues with previous evaluations of plausibility
Evaluating the plausibility of an explainer typically boils down to the following blueprint [Agarwal et al., 2023]. First,
identify or create a dataset D of graphs containing a known discriminative pattern, which acts as the ground truth
explanation µ∗. For example, in the Mutagenicity dataset [Kazius et al., 2005], the presence of a NO2 group is
usually associated with the mutagenicity that distinguishes the two classes and therefore serves as their ground-truth
explanation [Ying et al., 2019, Luo et al., 2020]. Then, the plausibility score between the post-hoc explanation µ and µ∗
is computed.
As pointed out by previous work [Faber et al., 2021, Yuan et al., 2022, Agarwal et al., 2023, Monti et al., 2024], this
blueprint makes the following implicit assumption, which may not hold in practice.
Assumption 1 (informal). A GNN trained to achieve high accuracy on a dataset with a known ground-truth explanation
µ∗will learn only the discriminative pattern represented by µ∗.
In fact, ML models are known to be susceptible to learning shortcuts in the data [Geirhos et al., 2020], which invalidates
Assumption 1. Arguably, explainers are most useful exactly when they can reveal that the model is not behaving as
expected [Lapuschkin et al., 2019, Teso et al., 2023]. Nonetheless, evaluating plausibility when Assumption 1 does
not hold can be misleading, as illustrated in Fig. 2: If Assumption 1 held and the model had learned to distinguish
molecule mutagenicity based solely on the ground truth NO2 pattern, we would expect its prediction to change when
this pattern is removed, provided that the resulting molecule remains sufficiently close to the data distribution. However,
as exemplified in Fig. 2 and analysed in Appendix B.2, removing the NO2 pattern often leaves the prediction of the
model unchanged, suggesting that Assumption 1 does not hold, and that the model learned an alternative pattern. In this
case, a well-behaved explainer outputting the alternative pattern would be penalized in favour of an explainer outputting
µ∗.
Crucially, the shortcoming highlighted in Fig. 2 is likely to appear in both synthetic and real datasets. In Appendix B,
we identify several of these cases on some of the most popular graph explainability benchmarks, extending Fontanesi
et al. [2024]’s analysis to previously unexamined datasets. Specifically, we discover novel shortcuts in BaShapes
[Ying et al., 2019] and BaMultiShapes [Azzolin et al., 2023], where the task can be solved by averaging node degrees
rather than using the intended motif. Additionally, on molecular benchmarks with explicit motif rules like Benzene,
AlkaneCarbonyl, and FluorideCarbonyl [Sanchez-Lengeling et al., 2020], we observe that GNNs consistently fail
to learn the correct patterns exactly, resorting instead to spurious correlations. Notably, we argue that this issue cannot
be solved by dataset design alone. Faber et al. [2021] and Agarwal et al. [2023] propose handcrafted datasets meant to
be more reliable, e.g., by generating unambiguous ground truth and by requiring perfect model accuracy. Yet, they do
not offer guarantees that a GNN trained on them implements a uniquely identified behaviour, and verifying a posteriori

Evaluating the faithfulness of explanations. An orthogonal and widely used (c.f. Fig. 1) approach to evaluating
explanation quality is faithfulness, assessing how well an explanation reflects the model behaviour [Agarwal et al.,
2024]. Nonetheless, faithfulness comes with its own limitations: there is no universal definition, available metrics
often conflict [Azzolin et al., 2025a, Miró-Nicolau et al., 2025], and these metrics are frequently validated by their
correlation with human-defined explanations [Fang et al., 2023, Zheng et al., 2023] – the same issue that plausibility
suffers from. Despite these limitations, faithfulness remains the only viable option to assess a given explanation in
downstream applications, and the evaluation setting we will propose in Section 5 offers a reliable testbed to validate
faithfulness metrics as well, which we leave to future work.
In the remainder of the paper, we address the issue of plausibility by building GNNs whose behaviour is known by
construction. This is achieved by replacing GNN training with compilation.

From logic formulas to ground truth explanations
4.1
Gracr: Compiling a logic formula into a GNN
Rather than hoping that training has induced the intended behaviour, we construct GNNs whose behaviour can be
controlled exactly. We obtain it by compiling, in the spirit of Lindner et al. [2023], a target classifier expressed in
logic directly into the weights of a standard message-passing architecture, so that the two agree on every graph. Our
construction follows the constructive expressiveness results of Barceló et al. [2020, Prop. 4.1], which we extend to
cover also graph-level tasks.

4.2
Defining ground truth explanations formally
Having obtained a GNN reliably implementing a target formula, the next stage is to define a suitable notion of ground
truth explanations, i.e., what we consider as the desired explanation. In previous studies, this usually corresponds
to the implanted target motif of interest, e.g., the house motif in Ba2Motif [Luo et al., 2020]. Nonetheless, the case
where ground truth explanations are not unique3 is often overlooked, and a precise definition of what a ground truth
explanation looks like is often left to task-specific heuristics [Schlichtkrull et al., 2021]. In this work, we will build on
the formal notion of graph explanation introduced in Azzolin et al. [2025b], described next:
Definition 1. (Prime Implicant explanation) Let g be a classifier and G be an instance with predicted label g(G), then
R ⊆G is a Prime Implicant explanation (PI) for g(G), written as PI(g, G), if:
1. (sufficiency) For all R′, such that R ⊆R′ ⊆G, we have that g(G) = g(R′).
2. (minimality) No other R′ ⊂R satisfies (1).
PIs are not unique, and we denote the set containing the Union of all PIs (UPI) for g(G) as UPI(g, G) := ∪iPI(g, G)i.
We will shorten this to UPI when referring to a generic set of all PIs. For node-level tasks, we will write PI(g, G, u) to
indicate the PI explanation for g(G, u).
PIs are a reasonable target for the following reasons: First, they are a crisp, rigorous solution for explaining a wide range
of models, like logic classifiers and tabular models [Shih et al., 2018, Marques-Silva and Ignatiev, 2022, Marques-Silva,
2023]; Second, they were shown to encode a more informative and trustworthy notion of explanation for graph tasks
[Weinbauer et al., 2025, Azzolin et al., 2026]; Third, they naturally handle alternative explanations by collecting the set
of all possible PIs [Darwiche and Hirth, 2020, 2023]; Last, they were shown to recover the implanted target motif for
motif-based graph tasks [Azzolin et al., 2025b]. Then, our definition of ground truth explanation is:
Definition 2. (Ground truth explanation) Let g be a classifier and G be an instance with predicted label g(G), then
the ground truth explanation µ∗for g(G) is UPI(g, G), such that µ∗
e = 1{e ∈UPI}.
The definition above indicates the edges we consider to be relevant for a given task, and reconciles with existing
definitions of feature relevancy from the formal explainability literature for tabular data [Huang et al., 2023]. Having
established a precise notion of ground truth explanation, we describe in the next section how UPI can be computed in
practice.
4.3
Computing UPI for a compiled GNN
Computing PIs is a known computationally hard problem, with a few exceptions [Marques-Silva et al., 2020, Bassan
et al., 2026]. This complexity arises because for every candidate explanation R, one has to query the black-box
model with every extension R ⊆R′ ⊆G, and then iterate to find a subset-minimal candidate (cf. Def. 1). Thanks
to our compilation step, however, we can avoid the cost of invoking the black-box model by relying on the symbolic
representation from which the GNN was derived. Next, we will describe a knowledge compilation algorithm relying on
Reduced Ordered Binary Decision Diagram (ROBDD) [Lee, 1959, Bryant, 1992] that allows an efficient computation
of UPI. Although this is not a novel technique in itself [Shih et al., 2018, Darwiche and Hirth, 2023], it is to the best of
our knowledge the first time it is applied in the context of graph explainability.

Boolean variables X = {x1, . . . , xn}. This is achieved by building a directed acyclic graph where nodes represent the
Boolean variables, and edges represent Boolean assignments. This graph-based representation of a Boolean function
supports efficient operations and queries on the function, including conjoining and disjoining ROBDDs, and this

Symbolic encoding of Boolean formulas over graphs. In our setting, given a target graph G and a Boolean function f
representing a logic formula defined over G, we instantiate an ROBDD with one node for each directed edge of G, such
that X = {xe}e∈E. Variables are ordered by edge index, and node and edge features are kept constant. A subgraph
R ⊆G is therefore identified with the assignment assigning xe = 1 when e ∈R and xe = 0 otherwise, representing a
bijection between the 2|E| boolean assignments of X and the 2|E| subgraphs present in G. Thanks to this bijection, an
ROBDD compactly encodes a Boolean function over all possible subgraphs of G. Hence, we will write the ROBDD
as a function over Boolean variables X or subgraphs R interchangeably. We report an example of such a construction
in Appendix D.1.
Having established how input graphs are encoded in Boolean variables, the construction of the ROBDD follows standard
construction and reduction algorithms, for which we refer the reader to Bryant [1986] and Wegener [2000]. We refer to
this procedure as BUILD, described in Appendix D.2.
So far, f has been a generic function mapping subgraphs to {0, 1}, hence a binary classifier. In our experiments, f
is given by the modal logic sentence from which the GNN to be explained is derived, as discussed in Section 4.1.
Consequently, the procedure described next for extracting UPI from f yields a valid ground truth explanation (cf.
Def. 2) of the GNN we will explain.

5
GracrBench: Benchmarking explainers on compiled GNNs
Having obtained a GNN implementing a target behaviour, we now use compiled models as a testbed for graph explainers.
Our pipeline is as follows: we write a formula, compile it into the weights of a GNN using Gracr, generate a dataset of
graphs whose label is consistent with the formula, compute the ground truth explanation UPI with Algorithm 1, and
compute the plausibility score of explainers against the ground truth. No training is involved, so we are guaranteed the
GNN reflects the ground truth we evaluate the explainer against, thus avoiding the confounder identified in Section 3.
Formulas and datasets are chosen to highlight specific shortcomings of explainers, as will be detailed later.
Explainers. We benchmark eleven explainers spanning several methodological categories. These include four
gradient-based attributions: SALIENCY [Pope et al., 2019], deconvolution [DECONV, Zeiler and Fergus, 2014], guided
backpropagation [GUIDEDBP, Springenberg et al., 2014], and integrated gradients [IG, Sundararajan et al., 2017];
three perturbation-based methods: GNNEXPLAINER [Ying et al., 2019], GRAPHMASK [Schlichtkrull et al., 2021]
and SUBGRAPHX [Yuan et al., 2021]; two surrogate-based methods: PGMEXPLAINER [Vu and Thai, 2020] and
GRAPHSVX [Duval and Malliaros, 2021]; and two decomposition-based methods: FLOWX [Gui et al., 2024] and LRP
[Baldassarre and Azizpour, 2019, Bach et al., 2015]. We additionally include a random baseline (RND) that assigns
each edge in the computational field a score drawn uniformly at random from {0, 1}.
Computing the plausibility score. We score an explainer by separately reporting the precision and recall of the
predicted µ with respect to µ∗. Given that UPI is a union of alternative and individually valid explanations, this choice
allows us to distinguish two cases: i) an explainer may precisely highlight truly relevant edges but fail to recover
alternative explanations, resulting in high precision but low recall; and ii) an explainer may recover many relevant edges
across alternative explanations while also including irrelevant edges, resulting in high recall but low precision. We
provide additional motivation for this choice over alternatives in Appendix E. Operationally, for each target, we sweep
over all thresholds induced by the scores, keep the one maximising F1 between µ and µ∗, and report the corresponding
precision and recall. To avoid empty explanations, we only explain positive targets.
5.1
Tasks and Datasets
We consider three graph-classification and three node-classification tasks. For each task, we generate graphs with
random backgrounds and implant the target pattern in positive ones, while expressing the task as a logical formula that
Gracr converts into a GNN. Details are in Appendix F.
Graph classification. i) In positive RedBlueLeaf instances, the graph contains a chain of four red nodes, each with
exactly two blue leaves. This structure mirrors classical motif-based benchmarks in which the implanted motif constitutes
the ground truth. ii) In positive Redundancy instances, the graph contains a black node with six red neighbours, any
one of which is sufficient to satisfy the property. The task therefore admits six alternative PI explanations, while UPI
comprises all six corresponding edges. iii) In positive CompetingRedundancy instances, the graph contains a black
node satisfying at least one of three conditions: having one red, two blue, or three green neighbours. The task therefore
admits three PI explanations, while UPI again comprises six edges.
Node classification. i) RedBlueLeaf-NC is a node-level variant of RedBlueLeaf. A target node is positive if it is
red, has exactly two blue leaves, and is adjacent to another node with the same properties. Consequently, target nodes
belonging to the same motif may have different explanations. ii) In Majority1Hop, a target node is positive if it has
strictly more red neighbours than blue neighbours. iii) Majority2Hop applies this comparison recursively. A target
node is positive if it has strictly more red-ish neighbours than blue-ish neighbours, where a node is red-ish according to
the Majority1Hop rule. This corresponds to two rounds of label propagation [Nerini et al., 2025].
5.2
Results & Key takeaways
The results are reported in Table 2 and Table 3 for graph and node classification, respectively. Results highlight that we
can recover previous insights while uncovering new ones, described next.
5.2.1
Recovering existing insights
GNNEXPLAINER does not sparsify redundant edges. Among the well-performing explainers, GNNEXPLAINER is
the only one specifically designed to provide sparse explanations, due to its L1 penalty on explanation scores [Ying
et al., 2019]. Our results on Redundancy, however, reveal the opposite: GNNEXPLAINER achieves perfect recall
across a wide range of L1 strengths (see Fig. 3 in the Appendix), highlighting all alternative explanations rather than a
sparse subset, a contradiction already observed empirically [Funke et al., 2022] and explained theoretically [He et al.,
2025].

5.2.2
Uncovering new limitations of explainers
Only FLOWX, GRAPHSVX, and IG are reliable across tasks, while others fluctuate. Reading Tables 2 and 3 jointly,
FLOWX, GRAPHSVX, and IG are the only explainers that never fall to chance level; e.g., FLOWX keeps an F1 of at
least 0.92 on all six tasks, and IG never scores below 0.96 in precision. Every other explainer is either uniformly poor
or strongly task-dependent. We stress that this distinction is invisible on the motif-based tasks alone – where SALIENCY,
LRP, and GNNEXPLAINER perform on par with FLOWX and IG – and that it is only on settings departing from
well-known motif-based tasks that the difference emerges, such as the two discussed next.
Most explainers are not robust to the implementation of formulas. A desirable property of explainers is implementation
invariance [Sundararajan et al., 2017, Nauta et al., 2023], i.e., explainers should yield the same – or similar thereof –
explanations for equivalent models. Since Gracr enables us to compile GNNs with a controllable behaviour, it provides a
controlled setting in which to systematically evaluate this property. As shown in Table 2, several explainers that achieve
perfect scores on the other tasks perform at chance level on CompetingRedundancy. To investigate this discrepancy,
we evaluate an alternative formulation of the same task; see Appendix C.5. Only IG, FLOWX, and GRAPHSVX
maintain consistent performance across the two formulations, suggesting greater robustness to these implementation
changes. The discrepancy is associated with the original implementation of the “∨” operator, which introduces double
negations through De Morgan’s laws. Similar effects arise from other formula rewritings, as illustrated by RedBlueLeaf
in Appendix C.5.
Explainers struggle to recover indirect influences.
While most explainers achieve perfect UPI recovery on
Majority1Hop, none attain perfect results on Majority2Hop. We first observe that gradient-based explainers, with

consistently miss edges whose removal would increase the number of bluish neighbours, indicating that they fail to

6
Conclusion
We formalized a common failure mode when evaluating the plausibility of GNN explainers: the model being explained
may not behave as expected. First, we showed that several popular datasets used in the literature suffer from this
(Section 3). Then, we proposed Gracr, the first compiler translating graded modal logic sentences into the weights of
a GNN (Section 4). Gracr yields compiled GNNs whose behaviour is known exactly, constituting a rigorous testbed
for evaluating explainers. To define a reliable ground truth, we adopt prime implicants and define our ground truth
explanation to be the union of all prime implicants, for which we developed an exact, efficient extraction algorithm.
Finally, we proposed GracrBench, which evaluates graph explainers over compiled GNNs based on how well they
recover the respective ground truth explanation (Section 5). Results on graph and node classification recover previously
known findings while uncovering new ones, demonstrating how GracrBench enables a sound, fine-grained analysis of
the behaviour of post-hoc GNN explainers.
Limitations. Compiled GNNs produced by Gracr rely on binary embeddings and noiseless aggregations, and therefore
have limitations in terms of realism compared to GNNs trained from data. This is, however, a deliberate design
choice allowing us to reason exactly about the internal workings of both the model and the explainer, at a level
of detail previously not possible. Nonetheless, experiments conducted on compiled models should be interpreted
carefully, and evaluations based on them should be intended as a minimum bar rather than a proxy of in-production
evaluations. Furthermore, just like for trained models, our results are to be interpreted in conjunction with the model, as
different model configurations can yield different results. Nevertheless, GracrBench allows us to rigorously control
these configurations – which are otherwise inextricably confounded on trained models – and assess the robustness of
explainers across these. Last, our current implementation is restricted to binary classification and categorical node
features.
Future work. Since evaluating all available explainers was infeasible due to their large number, we exclusively tested
popular instance-level explainers. Nonetheless, our analysis can be extended to include counterfactual and model-level
explainers, especially those aiming to explain GNNs by extracting a logic formula; see Appendix A for an overview.
Also, we could make compiled GNNs more realistic, e.g., by fine-tuning after compilation. This is challenging, as
fine-tuning may invalidate their guarantees, and ensuring that the expected behaviour is preserved is left to future work.

A
Extended related work
From Logic to Neural Networks. Integrating logical rules into machine learning models has been a long-standing
line of research [e.g., Pazzani and Kibler, 1992, Towell and Shavlik, 1994, Avila Garcez and Zaverucha, 1999]. In this
domain, researchers typically investigated methods to translate input propositional logic rules into neural networks,
which could subsequently be refined through backpropagation. More recently, starting with the work of Barceló et al.
[2020], the equivalence between formal logic and GNNs has been deeply analysed to characterize the expressivity of
the latter. Specifically, Barceló et al. [2020] established a fundamental correspondence between Aggregate-Combine
GNNs and graded modal logic. Nunn et al. [2024] extended these results by proposing the logic K#, an expansion
of graded modal logic that accounts for linear combinations of counting modalities, enabling bidirectional translation
between specific GNNs and logical formulas. These results were later expanded by Grau et al. [2026] and Benedikt
et al. [2026]: the former systematically maps different classes of bounded GNNs to corresponding fragments of modal
and first-order logic, whereas the latter broadens the class of GNNs translatable into logical formulas to analyse their
expressiveness and decidability. Closest to our work are Monti et al. [2024] and Nerini et al. [2025], which likewise
replace trained black boxes with white-box GNNs of known logic. However, unlike our approach, they do not support
general graded modal logic formulas. Monti et al. [2024] evaluates only node features, leaving edge importance entirely
undefined. While Nerini et al. [2025] does target edges using a fixed label-propagation GNN (whose tasks we reuse as
Majority1Hop and Majority2Hop), their manual derivation of the ground-truth edges is flawed, as it crucially misses
edges responsible for "indirect influences" in the classification. In contrast, Gracr compiles any graded modal logic
formula and automatically extracts the exact, complete UPI (Algorithm 1) for both node and graph tasks.
From GNN to Logic. A growing line of work explains GNNs post hoc with logic formulas rather than subgraphs.
Global explainers fit Boolean formulas over learned or mined concepts to the predictions of a trained model [Azzolin
et al., 2023, Armgaan et al., 2024, Geng et al., 2026, Ragno et al., 2025a], or learn description logic class expressions
for heterogeneous graphs [Köhler and Heindorf, 2024]. Similarly, Pluska et al. [2024] distil GNNs into decision
trees, and Morris and Horrocks [2025] extract rules that are provably sound, at the price of restricting the GNN to
non-negative weights. A complementary line of work designs self-explainable GNN architectures that expose logic
rules by construction rather than post hoc [Cranmer et al., 2020, Azzolin et al., 2025b, Ragno et al., 2025b]. Rather than
extracting a logic formula out of a GNN, we go in the opposite direction: the formula is fixed first and compiled into
the GNN, so the two coincide by construction. Hence, GracrBench offers a reliable testbed for testing also logic-based
explainability methods, which we plan to investigate in future work.
B
Analyses of popular datasets for evaluating plausibility
We provide an extended analysis of Section 3. In short, for the dataset below, we investigate whether alternative
solutions exist, or whether training a GNN on the dataset may yield a model that does not reliably implement the
intended ground truth.4 Since all of these datasets require identifying the correct motifs, the alignment between models
and the intended ground truth is only possible if the models achieve nearly perfect accuracy, as discussed in Faber et al.
[2021]. For datasets already covered in Section 3, we only provide the experimental details and additional results. In
total, our analysis covers 74 out of 94 of the plausibility-related papers we considered.
B.1
Synthetic datasets
We start our analysis by looking at synthetic motif datasets. For all of them, we explicitly check the presence
of a "shortcut", i.e., if there exists a valid alternative explanation ¯µ∗to the assumed ground truth µ∗, invalidating
Assumption 1. On these datasets, we use GIN [Xu et al., 2018], as a provably powerful GNN.
We observe that some of these "shortcuts" are already known in the literature: Fontanesi et al. [2024] noticed that
the datasets Ba2Motif [Luo et al., 2020], Grid [Longa et al., 2025], and GridHouse [Longa et al., 2025] all contain
alternative ground truth explanations that can be used to solve the task. We do not further comment on these datasets,
and focus on novel shortcuts instead.

C.4
Keeping the backward pass of a compiled model informative
A compiled network computes with integers, as by Eq. (3) every coordinate is Boolean, and every weight in Table 1 is
an integer. Consequently σ is always evaluated on the boundaries of the activations (z ∈{0, 1}) or on a saturated flat
(z ≤−1 or z ≥2), where the gradient σ′(z) is 0, and this may create problems for gradient-based explainers. To avoid
issues with meaningless gradients, we therefore replace the derivative of σ with
eσ′(z) := 1{z > 0} ,
(9)
and leave the forward pass untouched. This is applied for every explainer, except for IG for the reasons described
below. Note that Eq. (9) is the derivative of a RELU rather than of a truncated one, and provides the backward gradient
that a conventional architecture would provide at that unit. Eq. (9) is also semantically meaningful, since for every
pattern in Table 1 the condition z > 0 coincides with the truth of the subformula the unit computes: z = 1 −αk for
¬φk, z = αj + αk −1 for φj ∧φk, z = ν −N + 1 for ♢≥Nφk, and z = αk for a carried-forward unit, where αk is
the incoming value of coordinate k and ν the number of neighbours satisfying φk. Gradient therefore flows along the
subformulas that are satisfied.
IG is evaluated with the exact gradient. IG is the only explainer for which we keep the unmodified derivative of σ.
This is because, otherwise, the change may violate the Completeness axiom [Sundararajan et al., 2017], as attributions
may not sum up to the difference in the output between the original samples and the baseline of IG. Our ablation study
in Table 9 confirms that IG is nonetheless robust to this choice: the two backward passes yield identical scores on five
tasks out of six, and on Majority2Hop the surrogate gradient gains 0.04 precision in exchange for 0.13 in recall, the
final F1 being 0.92 instead of the original 0.97 reported in Table 2.

C.5
Ablating how the formula is written
In standard training, we cannot control how a model internally parametrizes a learned function. Therefore, when
functionally equivalent models exist for a given task, a reliable explainer should ideally perform consistently regardless
of the underlying architecture [Sundararajan et al., 2017, Nauta et al., 2023]. Because Gracr models are compiled
rather than trained, we have full control over the underlying implementation. This allows us to turn conditions that are
usually inextricably confounded in trained models – such as vanishing gradients, layer saturation, or specific weight
configurations – into controlled experiments. In this section, we test the robustness of explainers by observing how
they react when they are evaluated on different but logically equivalent compiled GNNs. In the following, we test an
alternative choice to compile the disjunction operator for CompetingRedundancy, and an alternative logic formula for
the RedBlueLeaf task.

D.2
The BUILD algorithm for ROBDDs
In Section 4.3, we introduced the method BUILD for constructing an ROBDD for each formula and graph. In this
section, we discuss this procedure in more detail. In practice, we rely on the dd Python package [Filippidis and contrib.,
2023] for ROBDD construction and inference.
Four fundamental operations are used to construct ROBDDs [Bryant, 1986]: Reduce, Apply, Restrict, and Composition.
Any other operation on ROBDDs can be expressed as a combination of these procedures [Bryant, 1986]. Reduce
transforms an arbitrary Binary Decision Diagram (BDD) into a canonical ROBDD by eliminating duplicate subgraphs
and redundant tests. Given two Boolean functions f and h implemented as ROBDDs and a binary Boolean operator
⟨op⟩, Apply generates a BDD implementing f⟨op⟩h (and can also be used to implement logical negation and induction,
as detailed in Bryant [1986]). Restrict simplifies an input BDD into another BDD by fixing a specific variable x to
a constant value b. Finally, Composition replaces a variable x in a ROBDD f with another function implemented
by a ROBDD h. Because Apply, Restrict, and Composition yield general BDDs, Reduce must always be applied
post-operation. In our implementation, this workflow is handled automatically by the dd package [Filippidis and
contrib., 2023]. In our paper, we treat Boolean operations directly as operations on the ROBDDs themselves, using
standard logical notation (e.g., ∧, ¬,|x=1) to implicitly denote the execution of these underlying graph-construction
algorithms. We denote the constant true and false functions by 1 and 0, respectively.
There are two variations of the BUILD algorithm: one for node tasks (BUILDNODE in Algorithm 4), and one for
graph tasks (BUILDGRAPH in Algorithm 5). In the latter case, we take as input only Φ and the graph G, while in the
former we also need the target node v. Since BUILDGRAPH internally uses BUILDNODE, we start our description from
BUILDNODE.
BUILDNODE (Algorithm 4) is recursive: when a formula Φ is the conjunction of two subformulas φj and φk, the
procedure recursively constructs the ROBDDs for φj and φk separately, then conjoins them. Analogous recursive steps
handle disjunction and negation. For base cases, i.e., the constants ⊤, ⊥, and the atomic predicates φ(v) = Col(v), the
ROBDD is initialized directly as 1, 0, or the constant Col(v). The most nuanced step is handling the counting quantifier
♢≥N ψ, which requires constructing a Boolean function CN that evaluates to true if and only if at least N neighbours of
v satisfy ψ. For each incident edge e = (v, u), the algorithm computes t = xe ∧BUILDNODE(ψ, u, G), indicating edge
existence and that u satisfies ψ, and then iteratively updates the intermediate counting functions Ck, k ∈{1, . . . , N}
(line 20). After evaluating all incident edges, it then returns the counting function CN, which now indicates whether
node v has at least N neighbours satisfying ψ.
BUILDGRAPH (Algorithm 5) builds directly upon Algorithm 4. The graph-level classifier is framed as a counting
quantifier ∃[lo,hi] that verifies whether a node-level formula φ is satisfied by at least lo and at most hi nodes, i.e. it
express the formula: Φ =

D.4
Computational complexity of Algorithm 1
We proceed to analyse the time complexity of Algorithm 1 in terms of the number of symbolic operations on the
ROBDD. Given a graph G and a formula Φ, we first perform the construction of the ROBDD. Consider a node-level
formula Φ and a target node v, for which we are building the corresponding ROBDD. While ∧, ∨, and ¬ all require a
single operation on the ROBDD to be built (lines 7-12 of Algorithm 4), the existential quantifier ♢≥N can require up to
N|ET | operations, with ET the receptive field of target node v for formula Φ (lines 13-23). Calling Nmax the largest N

between counting quantifiers in Φ, the upper bound for the computational cost for building a node-level formula is then
O(|E| · |Φ| · Nmax) symbolic operations on the ROBDD, where |Φ| := |sub(Φ)| is the number of subformulas of Φ, as
defined in Section 4.1. For graph-level formulas (Section 4.1), the node counting quantifier ∃[lo,hi] additionally requires
O(|V|2) symbolic operations (lines 3-8 of Algorithm 5) to be constructed.
Then, Algorithm 2 requires O(|E|) symbolic operations to construct the function f ↑: as shown in Appendix D.3, for
each edge in E we modify f ↑iteratively by performing a symbolic operation with the ROBDD (f ↑←f ↑∧f ↑|xe=1).
Algorithm 3 similarly performs O(|E|) symbolic operations to examine if each e ∈E is also in UPI (for each edge,
it always performs a fixed number of symbolic operations on the ROBDD, and then checks its satisfiability, which is
an O(1) operation when using ROBDD). The final time complexity, for a single target of a graph classifier, is then
O(|E| · |Φ| · Nmax + |V|2) symbolic operations.

size, which is in the worst case exponential in the number of variables [Bryant, 1986] which, in our case, is the number
of edges |E| of the input graph. However, in practice, the tasks and datasets we have chosen rarely fall into this worst
case, and the dd Python package [Filippidis and contrib., 2023] provides an efficient implementation of the ROBDD.

Choosing the right evaluation metric
In this section we motivate our choice to report precision and recall separately, and scoring against the union of the PIs
rather than against each PI individually. We do this considering previous approaches:
Why not plain Accuracy [Ying et al., 2019]. On the tasks we consider, UPI covers a small minority of the edges of
a graph (c.f. Table 15). Accuracy would therefore be dominated by the negative class: the constant explanation that
declares every edge irrelevant would score very high, while carrying no information whatsoever.
30


Beyond Trained Models: Compiling GNNs for a Sound Explainer Benchmark
A PREPRINT
Why not AUROC [Luo et al., 2020] and Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PRAUC) [Nerini et al., 2025].
These two metrics measure different things, but they share one key pitfall: each compresses the precision/recall
trade-off into a single scalar, and because UPI is a union of alternative, individually valid explanations, that trade-off is
informative. Consider an explainer that returns one single PI, correctly and in full. This is alone a desirable, high-quality
explanation [Darwiche and Hirth, 2020, Marques-Silva and Ignatiev, 2022, Bassan et al., 2025, Azzolin et al., 2026], as
everything it selects is relevant and minimal. Its recall, however, is |PI|/|UPI|, which can be arbitrarily small. Hence,
the PRAUC will also be small. Since such an explainer has settled on one explanation, it has no reason to rank the edges
of the other PIs any higher than the irrelevant ones, and therefore AUROC also drops. In every case, the fact that each
and every edge the explainer selected is correct may get overwhelmed by the fact that alternative valid explanations
exist when the explainer preferred to focus on just one.
Why not a weighted intersection over union (WIoU) [Azzolin et al., 2025a]. A further alternative is to compare the
two masks with a weighted intersection over union,
WIoU(µ, µ∗) =

which has the appeal of requiring no threshold. The problem is that WIoU may charge the explanation for the total score
mass placed on irrelevant edges, which however accumulates with the size of the graph even when the ranking of the
edges is perfect. To see this, consider a task whose ground truth is a single edge per graph, and an explainer that assigns
importance 1.0 to that edge and a negligible 0.001 to all others, i.e., a perfect ordering. On a graph with |E| = 10 we
get WIoU = 1/(1 + 9 · 0.001) = 0.991, whereas on a graph with |E| = 5000 we get 1/(1 + 4999 · 0.001) = 0.167.
This shows that the same explainer, with the same ranking, is scored an order of magnitude apart, meaning scores are
not comparable across graph size.
Why the union of the PIs, and not the disjoint union [Agarwal et al., 2023]. Evaluating against every single PI
individually would require retaining one explanatory mask per PI and scoring the explanation against the best matching
one, similarly to what is proposed in Agarwal et al. [2023]. We avoid this for two reasons: First, the number of PIs can
grow exponentially in |E| so both storing the masks and matching against them may become intractable; Second, a
best-matching-PI score is easy to inflate, as the explainer returning a single PI is awarded a perfect score while telling
the user nothing about how well the explainer covers all the other alternative explanations.
In summary, our design choice of tracking precision and recall separately allows us to measure: i) how well the
explainer recovers exactly the relevant elements, via the precision score; ii) how well it can cover the range of all
possible alternative explanations, via the recall score.
Two additional caveats are of interest: First, we note that the F1 reported in Table 2 and Table 3 does not match the
precision and recall columns; this is because the F1 column is computed as the average of sample-wise precisions and
recalls, and the average of F1 scores does not necessarily match the F1 of the averages. Second, by sweeping over all
possible thresholds, we are providing an optimistic estimate of precision, recall, and F1.

We discarded works targeting non-standard explanation modalities (e.g., global decision boundaries [Wang and Shen,

Implementation details of explainers
For some explainers, we performed some modifications to their implementation in order to adapt them to our evaluation
setting. We detail each of the modifications in the following paragraphs, along with the corresponding justification.
In every case, these modifications are applied so as not to change the original scope or functionality of the explainer.
Implementations of explainers are taken from PyG [Fey et al., 2025], except when otherwise specified.
SubgraphX. [Yuan et al., 2021] We adopt the implementation provided in Agarwal et al. [2023]8. For node tasks, after
computing the explanation, this implementation applies a filter and return only the attribution on the computational
graph of the node. Since it is the only explainer to do so, we removed this filter and compared the explanation on the
full graph instead, to make the evaluation identical for all explainers. Because underlying computation of the explainer
is inherently restricted to the computation graph, edges falling outside of it are simply zero-padded. Note that this
modification does not affect execution time, and the explainer infers the depth of the computation graph directly from
the number of message-passing layers in the target model.
GNNExplainer. [Ying et al., 2019] GNNExplainer scales every message between node u and v by σ(mu,v) for a
learned logit mu,v, and the default initialisation sets mu,v ≈0, so that each message is halved. We noticed that this can
create instabilities for some datasets (like RedBlueLeaf) in the training of the logits, as halving the count of incoming
messages can greatly change the total count of the neighbours satisfying a given sub-formula, even at initialization time.
To reduce this effect, we use the bias term introduced in the original implementation9 provided by Ying et al. [2019],
such that logits are initialized at values around σ(mu,v) ∼1. In this way, the count of satisfying neighbours is roughly
preserved at initialisation time. Contrary to the original implementation, this bias term is only used for initializing the
logits, and is not injected at every forward step. Then, during training, GNNExplainer will attempt to sparsify the logits
as much as possible, following the original implementation. We verified that the value of this bias does not drive our
insight regarding the sparsification ability of GNNEXPLAINER described in Section 5.2.1 by sweeping the additional
bias term over {0, 1, 2.5, 5} on Redundancy, where 0 recovers the unbiased initialisation. Precision, recall and F1 all
remain equal to 1.00 ± 0.00 over the same 5 seeds, exactly the value reported in Table 2.
FLOWX. [Gui et al., 2024] We adopt the original FLOWX implementation10. FLOWX is a decomposition explainer,
which decomposes the contribution of each part of the graphs through flows. Besides the standard method, which we
adopt, the authors of the paper propose variations that have an additional training step to refine the original explanations.

PGMEXPLAINER. [Vu and Thai, 2020] We adapted the implementation of PGMEXPLAINER provided in PYG [Fey
et al., 2025]. Originally, PGMEXPLAINER provides as the explanation the p-value scores associated with each node for
its statistical relevance, together with a binary mask on the k nodes with lowest p-value (where k is a parameter of the
explainer). Since in this work we evaluate exclusively edge-level explanations, we assigned to each edge e = (u, v) an
importance score 1 −max(pu, pv): in this way, an edge is important only as much as its less important endpoint.
GRAPHSVX. [Duval and Malliaros, 2021] We adopt the original GRAPHSVX implementation11. GRAPHSVX is a
perturbation-based explainer that performs attribution for each node in the k-hop neighbourhood of the explained node
and for each of its features. Since in this work we evaluate exclusively edge-level explanations, we disable the feature
players so that the whole coalition budget is spent on the graph structure, and, as for PGMEXPLAINER, we assign to
each edge e = (u, v) the score min(|ϕu|, |ϕv|), where ϕ are the estimated Shapley values; the explained node, which
is not a player, receives the maximum score of its neighbourhood. Moreover, the original implementation assumes
dense adjacency matrices for graph classification tasks, incompatible with the sparse encoding of PYG; we adapted the
code to work with sparse matrices as well, keeping the original coalition sampling and regression. Because it assigns
equal importance to both edge directions, its maximum achievable precision is bounded at 50% on Majority1Hop,
Redundancy, and CompetingRedundancy, as relevant edges in these datasets contribute exclusively in one direction.
Nonetheless, across all these tasks, the explainer reaches a precision of ≈50%, implying that it successfully localizes
relevant nodes, but its inherent design limitations prevent it from distinguishing fine-grained edge directionality. Similar
reasoning applies to Majority2Hop, where the explainer reaches 56% precision: although both directions of an edge
can be important, typically only one contributes, meaning its theoretical maximum is only marginally higher. The
same theoretical cap applies to PGMEXPLAINER and SUBGRAPHX as well, though both achieve significantly lower
precision than GRAPHSVX in practice.
LRP. [Bach et al., 2015] We adopt the LRP implementation provided in CAPTUM [Kokhlikyan et al., 2020], through
the CAPTUM interface of PYG. LRP propagates the model output backwards through the network according to layer-
specific rules. We adopted the baseline epsilon rule for all the layers of our compiled models, the same rule CAPTUM
applies by default.

Theorem 1. Let Φ be a classifier and G a graph such that G |= Φ, let f = BUILD(Φ, G) be its ROBDD encoding,
and let f ↑be the output of EXTEND(f) so that f ↑maps a subgraph R to 1 iff R is sufficient. Then, for any edge e in G,
it holds that (f ↑∧xe ∧¬f ↑|xe=0̸ = 0) ⇐⇒e ∈UPI(f, G).

(←)
We aim to show that if e ∈UPI, then f ↑∧xe ∧¬f ↑|xe=0̸ = 0. Then, by assumption, e belongs to a PI, say M. We
note that M is by itself a witness of f ↑∧xe ∧¬f ↑|xe=0̸ = 0:

We now want to show that e must be in R′, meaning e ∈UPI. Let us assume to the contrary that e̸ ∈R′. Then,
