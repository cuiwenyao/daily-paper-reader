ABSTRACT 

Neuro-Symbolic (NeSy) predictors incorporate prior knowledge into the prediction process of neural networks, ensuring that outputs satisfy specified constraints, making them par-ticularly suitable for high-stakes applications where compliance with domain knowledge is essential. A key bottleneck in this paradigm is the acquisition of symbolic constraints: encoding domain knowledge into logical formulas remains a manual and expert-intensive process. In this work, we investigate the extent to which auto-formalization via LLMs can systematically translate textual knowledge into symbolic knowledge that can be plugged into NeSy predictors. To this end, we introduce auto-nesy-bench , a new benchmark for evaluating constraint formalization and its impact on downstream accuracy of NeSy pre-dictors. Through an extensive evaluation across several domains, we find that LLMs can formalize constraints to a meaningful extent, generating formulas that are often similar to those provided by human experts. Moreover, when the generated formulas are syntactically valid, they can lead to high-quality downstream predictions. The code and benchmark are available at https://unitn-sml.github.io/auto-nesy-bench/ .

1 Introduction 

Neuro-Symbolic (NeSy) AI combines the learning capabilities of neural networks with the reasoning ca-pabilities of symbolic systems, making it a promising paradigm for building reliable AI models [De Raedt et al., 2021, Garcez et al., 2022]. A prominent and well-studied class of NeSy models is that of NeSy predic-tors [Manhaeve et al., 2018, Badreddine et al., 2022, Chen et al., 2026a], which incorporate prior knowledge into the prediction process to ensure that outputs comply with domain-specific constraints [Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz, 2020, Giunchiglia et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2026b], making them suitable for high-stakes applica-tions [Giunchiglia et al., 2022, 2025, Dash et al., 2022]. Such models typically assume that the constraints are provided beforehand , requiring a domain expert to identify the relevant knowledge and formalize it into a representation that can be incorporated into the predictor. This requirement persists even in recent frameworks such as DeepLog [Derkinderen et al., 2026] and ULLER [Van Krieken et al., 2024], which aim to make NeSy predictors more accessible to practitioners, as the formalization of logical constraints still falls on the end user. This raises a natural question: what if an end user wants to deploy a NeSy predictor but has no expert available to formalize the required knowledge? 

We address this gap by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) as domain experts to formalize natural language into symbolic constraints.

Existing benchmarks target either Boolean satisfiability (SAT), such as satbench [Wei et al., 2025], or CP, such as dcpbench [Michailidis et al., 2025a], but do not evaluate how generated constraints affect learning and downstream NeSy performance. To address this gap, we introduce auto-nesy-bench , a challenging NeSy benchmark that extends the well-established rsbench [Bor-tolotti et al., 2024] with prompts, data instances, output annotations, and evaluation metrics in which the task is to formalize a logical constraint in conjunctive normal form (CNF) out of a textual description (see Fig. 1). To instantiate this evaluation, the LLM formalizes the constraints in propositional logic (PL) in one of several formalisms ranging from a textual representation of a CNF such as DIMACS [Johnson and Trick, 1996] or natural language ( NAT ) to Python programs using PySAT [Ignatiev et al., 2018], CPMpy [Guns, 2019] and SymPy [Meurer et al., 2017], each one coming with different challenges (see Section 3.1). Our results show that high-performing LLMs can formalize NeSy tasks, but performance depends on the level of detail in the constraint description. When the generated formulas are syntactically valid, formalization errors can have little impact on downstream predictions. 

Our contributions : We introduce auto-nesy-bench , a benchmark for evaluating auto-formalization in NeSy tasks. We investigate the following research questions: ( RQ1 ) Can LLMs formalize logical constraints from natural language descriptions? and ( RQ2 ) How do NeSy models perform when provided with LLM-generated constraints? To address RQ1 , we analyze the problem from multiple perspectives, examining which types of (a) LLMs, (b) input formats, and (c) output formats lead to the best results, as well as which configurations fail. For RQ2 , we evaluate two representative state-of-the-art NeSy approaches, a layer and a loss, using LLM-generated constraints. 

2 NeSy Predictors 

Among NeSy approaches [De Raedt et al., 2021, Garcez et al., 2022], here we focus on NeSy predictors ,classifiers designed for reliability [Giunchiglia et al., 2022, Chen and Zhao, 2025, Chen et al., 2026b]. Given an input x ∈ Rn, these models predict a (multi-)label y ∈ { 0, 1}m while leveraging prior knowledge K, which is usually expressed in the form of structural [Di Liello et al., 2020] or safety requirements [Hoernle et al., 2022]. The model then produces a predictive distribution pθ (y | x; K) that assigns lower [Xu et al., 2018, van Krieken et al., 2023], or even provably zero [Manhaeve et al., 2018, Ahmed et al., 2022], probability to outputs 

y that violate the constraint K.2Auto-Formalizing Neuro-Symbolic Predictors A P REPRINT 

As an example, consider Fig. 1, where a NeSy predictor determines whether a car can move forward from input observation x, such as an image of the road. Let green , clear , and forward denote Boolean variables indicating whether the traffic light is green, the road is clear, and the car moves forward, respectively. We impose the domain constraint K : ( green ∧ clear ) ⇒ forward , stating that the car can move forward when the traffic light is green and the road is clear. While the neural network may assign nonzero probability to output combinations that violate K, a NeSy predictor incorporates K into the prediction process. It assigns zero probability to invalid combinations and renormalizes the remaining probability mass. For example, green = 1 ,

clear = 1 , and forward = 0 is ruled out because it violates K, while all combinations satisfying K remain possible. Existing NeSy predictors differ along two main dimensions. First, they vary in the underlying logical formalism they support, i.e., propositional logic [Ahmed et al., 2022, Giunchiglia et al., 2024], first-order logic [Manhaeve et al., 2018, Zhou, 2019, Badreddine et al., 2022, Pojer et al., 2024, 2025], and satisfiability modulo theory over linear real arithmetic [Kurscheidt et al., 2025, 2026]. Second, they differ in how they enforce constraint satisfaction, relying on paradigms such as probabilistic logic [Lippi and Frasconi, 2009, Marra and Kuželka, 2021, Pryor et al., 2023, Skryagin et al., 2022, Maene and Raedt, 2023] or fuzzy logic [Diligenti et al., 2017, Donadello et al., 2017, Badreddine et al., 2022]. In this work, we focus primarily on semantic probabilistic layers (SPL ) [Ahmed et al., 2022], a representative state-of-the-art NeSy predictor for PL. SPL is a predictive layer that can be placed on top of a neural network to enforce logical constraints. It combines the unconstrained predictive distribution qθ (y | x) produced by the neural network with a constraint distribution cK(y), represented as a differentiable probabilistic circuit [Darwiche, 2001, Vergari et al., 2019, Choi et al., 2020] obtained through knowledge compilation [Darwiche and Marquis, 2002]. The resulting predictive distribution is given by the tractable product of experts:

3 Auto-formalizing Neuro-Symbolic Predictors 

Our goal is to use an LLM as an expert that translates natural language domain knowledge into symbolic constraints that can be later used by a NeSy predictor. The resulting pipeline, shown in Fig. 1, consists of two stages: first, the LLM formalizes the constraints; then, the generated formula is incorporated into the NeSy predictor and used during learning and inference. 

LLMs as constraint experts . Recently, reasoning models have been developed to improve performance on complex tasks by generating intermediate reasoning steps before producing a final answer [Wei et al., 2022, Guo et al., 2025]. Following Michailidis et al. [2025a], we treat an LLM as a black-box function h : Λ ∗ → Λ∗,where Λ is the model vocabulary and Λ∗ denotes the set of finite token sequences. As shown in Fig. 1, given a prompt, the LLM produces an output sequence s without requiring access to its weights, logits, hidden states, or training data. 

3.1 Constraint Formalization 

Learning symbolic constraints from data has been extensively studied as constraint acquisition [De Raedt et al., 2018, Tsouros et al., 2024, 2025]. Existing approaches typically learn constraints from labeled satisfying and violating assignments or through interaction with an oracle or solver [De Raedt et al., 2016, Bessiere et al., 2017, Freuder and Wallace, 1992]. In our setting, instead of learning from a collection of examples, the LLM receives a single natural language description of the domain knowledge and acts as the constraint-acquisition mechanism [Michailidis et al., 2024]. We focus on PL and use CNF as the target language for constraints. A CNF can be expressed through several equivalent representations, including high-level modeling languages and libraries such as CPMpy , SymPy , and 3Auto-Formalizing Neuro-Symbolic Predictors A P REPRINT 

PySAT . For example, as depicted in Fig. 1, the constraint (green ∧ clear ) ⇒ forward can be expressed in any of these representations, despite representing the same logical relation. We consider these different representations because they reflect interfaces practitioners actually use to specify constraints, from low-level solver formats to high-level modeling libraries, allowing us to test whether LLMs auto-formalization ability depends on the target syntax. Among these, Python-based libraries ( PySAT , CPMpy , SymPy ) are of particular interest, as LLMs have repeat-edly been shown to be especially capable of generating correct Python code [Chen et al., 2021, Austin et al., 2021, Li et al., 2022], making it a natural interface to test for auto-formalization. We also consider direct gen-eration of Boolean formulas in natural language ( NAT ) and DIMACS CNF, i.e., the common standard for SAT solvers [Johnson and Trick, 1996]. As shown in Fig. 1, all these representations are translated into DIMACS , to unify evaluation (see Section 5.1). 

Prompting . Each prompt frames the LLM as an expert in SAT solving and constraint modeling, specifies the syntax of the target representation ( DIMACS , NAT , PySAT , CPMpy , or SymPy ), provides the grounded variables, and requires the generated formula to be contained within a <cnf>...</cnf> block. Unlike Michailidis et al. [2025a], we also investigate how performance varies with the level of detail provided in the prompt (Section 5.1), thereby assessing the extent to which auto-formalization depends on the user’s expertise. Complete prompts are provided in Section F. 

Symbol grounding . A key challenge in translating natural language descriptions into logical constraints is identifying relevant symbols and consistently mapping them to logical variables, a problem known as symbol grounding [Harnad, 1990]. Recent work has shown that widely used open weight LLMs can struggle to correctly identify symbols in data [Wüst et al., 2025, Debole et al., 2026]. Furthermore, comparing formulas that use different symbols requires an additional alignment step [Bortolotti et al., 2025], making evaluation substantially more challenging. Since our goal is to study constraint formalization rather than symbol identification, we provide the LLM with the complete set of primary variables for each task . Each variable is specified by its name and meaning. 3 As illustrated in Fig. 1, this grounded set of variables serves as the interface between the natural language description and the target representation, allowing us to evaluate whether the LLM correctly captures the intended constraint independently of symbol identification. The LLM may still introduce auxiliary variables, provided that they are explicitly defined in terms of the primary variables. 

Self-verification . Following Michailidis et al. [2025a], we allow the LLM to iteratively correct its output over multiple stages. When a syntax error is detected, we provide a correction prompt containing the erroneous formula and corresponding error message. The LLM has to return either [[OK]] if no correction is needed, or 

[[FIXED]] followed by the corrected formula in a <cnf>...</cnf> block. Before and after each correction step, we automatically fix formatting issues that do not require LLM reasoning, such as incorrect DIMACS 

headers or missing Python imports (see Section C). 

Formalization evaluation . Existing benchmarks, such as satbench and dcpbench , primarily rely on satisfiability results to evaluate the quality of the generated formulas. In satbench , this amounts to checking whether the generated formula has the same satisfiability status as the ground-truth formula, i.e., whether both are SAT or UNSAT. In dcpbench , the evaluation instead tests whether a generated CP solution is feasible and satisfies the ground-truth constraint. These metrics are not suitable for the full auto-formalization pipeline, as we are interested not only in whether a formula is SAT, but also in which solutions it admits . This is because NeSy predictors such as SPL assign probability mass only to satisfiable assignments, preventing the final distribution from assigning mass to UNSAT assignments. To capture this, we evaluate the agreement between the valid assignments of the generated formula and those of the ground-truth formula using model counting (MC) [Chavira and Darwiche, 2008]. We obtain the entries of the confusion matrix by counting the valid assignments satisfying both the ground-truth ϕ∗ and the generated formula ˆϕ:TP = MC (ϕ∗ ∧ ˆϕ), FP = MC (¬ϕ∗ ∧ ˆϕ), FN = MC (ϕ∗ ∧ ¬ ˆϕ), TN = MC (¬ϕ∗ ∧ ¬ ˆϕ), (2) i.e., true positive, false positive, false negative and true negative, respectively. We compute these quantities exactly for tasks with at most 20 variables and use ApproxMC [Chakraborty et al., 2016] for larger instances (see Section B for details), then derive F1, precision, and recall. Recall is particularly important in our setting as SPL uses the constraints to restrict the space of possible outputs. If a generated constraint incorrectly excludes a valid assignment, the predictor cannot assign probability mass to that assignment, making the corresponding configuration impossible to predict [Grivas et al., 2022, 2024]. Finally, we report the proportion of generated formulas containing syntax errors.

3.2 From Auto-Formalization To NeSy Predictors 

Once generated, each CNF formula is embedded into an SPL [Ahmed et al., 2022] and, as an alternative, used as a SL [Xu et al., 2018]. CNFs are compiled into sentential decision diagrams (SDDs) [Darwiche, 2011] via the PySDD Python library. For SPL , the SDD is loaded as a differentiable probabilistic circuit using 

cirkit [The april Lab, 2024], while SL uses KLay [Maene et al., 2025].

At test time, we perform maximum a posteriori inference over the predictive distribution, i.e., ˆy = arg max y pθ (y | x; K) for SPL and ˆy = arg max y pθ (y | x) for SL . The quality of the LLM-generated constraints can affect the final predictor in two ways: incorrect constraints may rule out valid predictions , while incomplete or weak constraints may allow invalid predictions . These effects are hard in 

SPL , where the formula determines the feasible predictions, but act as a training bias in SL . Thus, we evaluate not only whether LLMs generate valid constraints, but also whether those constraints yield correct predictions. 

Metrics . In addition to F1, accuracy, and recall, we introduce three metrics tailored to our setting: formula relationship (R EL ), consistency (C ON ), and model-count ratio (MC-R). R EL characterizes the relationship between the generated formula ˆϕ and the ground-truth formula ϕ∗, distinguishing four cases: equal ( ˆϕ = ϕ∗), 

permissive ( ˆϕ ⇐ ϕ∗), strict ( ˆϕ ⇒ ϕ∗), and incomparable ( ˆϕ ∥ ϕ∗). A permissive formula admits all valid assignments but also some invalid ones, while a strict formula admits only valid assignments, but excludes some valid ones. Incomparable formulas exhibit both types of errors. C ON measures the proportion of assignments consistent with the ground-truth constraint, while MC-R measures the relative number of satisfying assignments admitted by the generated formula. This is useful for strict and permissive cases, as it quantifies the extent to which the set of satisfying assignments admitted by the generated formula differs from that of the ground-truth formula, e.g., if a permissive formula has MC-R = 1 .75 , then ˆϕ admits 1.75 

times as many satisfying assignments as the ground-truth. Further details are reported in Section B. 

4 Auto-NeSy-Bench 

The proposed pipeline raises two questions: (RQ1 ) can an LLM correctly formalize the intended domain knowledge? and (RQ2 ) how do LLM-generated constraints affect the behavior of the resulting NeSy predictor? 

Existing constraint-generation benchmarks address the first question in standalone CP settings, such as 

dcpbench , or SAT settings, such as satbench , while NeSy benchmarks typically assume pre-existing background knowledge. Thus, neither evaluates the full pipeline from natural language descriptions to formal constraints and their downstream impact on NeSy predictors. To address this gap, we introduce 

auto-nesy-bench , a benchmark designed to evaluate LLM-based auto-formalization and its downstream impact on NeSy predictors. We first describe the existing benchmarks and then introduce auto-nesy-bench .Additional details are in Section D. 

SATBench . satbench [Wei et al., 2025] evaluates LLMs logical reasoning through natural language descrip-tion of puzzles generated from CNF formulas. The original benchmark contains 2,100 instances, including both satisfiable and unsatisfiable problems. We repurpose its paired natural language descriptions and refer-ence CNF formulas to evaluate auto-formalization. We exclude UNSAT instances because, when equipped with SPL , they assign zero probability to every possible configuration, resulting in an uninformative down-stream predictor. After excluding these instances and applying the consistency checks described in Section D.1, we retain 1,041 SAT instances. 

DCPBench-Open . We repurpose dcpbench [Michailidis et al., 2025b, 2026] for auto-formalization, creating a new version of the original benchmark consisting of 164 instances containing natural language descriptions of combinatorial problems collected from multiple sources. These problems are designed for CP, where the goal is to construct a constraint-based model rather than enumerate the set of solutions. Consequently, the benchmark includes problems involving both discrete and continuous variables, as well as optimization problems. Optimization problems are not suitable for our NeSy predictor setting, as they seek a single optimal solution rather than a set of valid solutions. Similarly, instances involving continuous variables fall outside the scope of PL. We therefore discard such instances during preprocessing. We also remove constraints that lead to excessive circuit sizes during knowledge compilation, as well as instances whose formulas fail to convert into CNF. 5 Experimental Results 

We try to answer our RQ1 and RQ2 by evaluating several open-weight LLMs families: Qwen(-Coder) [Yang et al., 2025, Cao et al., 2026], Gemma-3/4 [Team et al., 2025, 2026], Mistral-Nemo [Jiang et al., 2023], DeepSeek-R1-Distill [Liu et al., 2024], Olmo [Groeneveld et al., 2024], Phi [Abdin et al., 2025], and GPT-OSS [OpenAI, 2025]. Sizes vary from 8.2B to 117B parameters. Every LLM has been evaluated unquantized ,except for GPT-OSS that comes natively quantized. 

5.1 RQ1: Auto-formalization of Logical Constraints 

We structure our analysis along three main axes. First, we compare different LLMs to assess their auto-formalization capability. Second, we investigate how prompt configuration affects performance. Finally, we compare different output formats to identify the most effective for formalizing constraints. 

Settings . Due to computational constraints, we evaluate a broader range of LLMs on auto-nesy-bench , our novel benchmark, while evaluating a subset of LLMs on satbench and 

dcpbench . This allows us to thoroughly evaluate our new benchmark while also providing validation across existing benchmarks. 6 Related Work 

Constraint Modeling in LLMs . Recent research has explored LLMs as a tool to translate natural language descriptions into formal constraint, logic, or CP models [Tsouros et al., 2023, Michailidis et al., 2024, 2025a]. This direction has also been explored through in-context and retrieval-augmented strategies for example selection [Michailidis et al., 2024], active-learning [Mechqrane et al., 2024, Mechqrane and Bessiere, 2026], fine-tuning [Shi et al., 2025], SAT solving [Hazra et al., 2025], and anomaly detection [Zhang and Jain, 2026]. A parallel line uses LLMs as natural-language-to-symbolic translators paired with external solvers or provers, targeting propositional and first-order logic [Liu et al., 2026, Pan et al., 2025a,b], ontologies [Strader et al., 2024], or executable programs [Austin et al., 2021]. Orthogonal approaches generate answer set programming (ASP) rules [Kareem et al., 2024], synthesize inductive logic programming benchmarks [Helff et al., 2026], and ground symbols from natural language descriptions [Stein et al., 2025]. However, these works evaluate end-task accuracy or formalization quality, and none targets auto-formalization for NeSy predictors. 

Knowledge Acquisition in NeSy . Knowledge acquisition is a well-studied problem in NeSy AI and statistical relational learning [De Raedt et al., 2021]. Existing approaches fall into two settings: (i) learning explicit knowledge from data, and (ii) learning models that acquire explicit and interpretable knowledge. The former aims to recover symbolic knowledge from training examples using a predefined vocabulary, as in inductive logic programming and its probabilistic extensions [Muggleton and De Raedt, 1994, De Raedt and Kersting, 2008]. The latter includes a growing body of NeSy models [Daniele et al., 2023, Tang and Ellis, 2023, Shindo et al., 2023, 2024, Wüst et al., 2024, Debot et al., 2024], which may be unreliable due to reasoning shortcuts [Bortolotti et al., 2025]. Both settings assume that the predicate vocabulary is specified by a human expert or that the knowledge is learned from data. Instead, we study whether LLMs can formalize it from natural language descriptions. 

7 Conclusion 

To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first benchmark and end-to-end pipeline for LLM-based auto-formalization of NeSy predictors, using constraints either as a layer or training loss. We investigate its opportunities and limitations, showing that the best-performing LLMs can generate high-quality formulas that perform comparably to manually constructed ones in downstream NeSy predictors, and that their performance depends on the quality of the constraint specification. Our results further highlight which LLMs, prompting strategies, and output formats are most suitable for auto-formalization, while showing that smaller models can struggle substantially. More broadly, we see this as a step toward promoting NeSy AI adoption: our goal is not to replace domain experts, but to support them, since generated formulas remain interpretable and can be inspected and corrected. 

Future Work . Our evaluation is restricted to propositional logic formulas with SPL and SL as representative NeSy approaches. We focus on formulas compatible with current NeSy pipelines, as handling substantially more complex constraints remains a general challenge in NeSy AI [Huang et al., 2021]. We leave the effects of automatic symbol extraction [Stein et al., 2025] and their downstream consequences [Marconato et al., 2026] for future work. Additional future directions include evaluating auto-formalization with a broader range of NeSy predictors, including architectures that support first order logic, such as DeepProbLog [Manhaeve et al., 2018], LogicTensorNetworks [Rinaldi and Serafini, 2026, Badreddine et al., 2022], and ASP-based models [Aspis et al., 2022, Yang et al., 2020, Skryagin et al., 2023, Cunnington et al., 2024]. Another direction is to study auto-formalization with more complex constraint formalisms, such as satisfiability modulo theory over linear real arithmetic [Kolb et al., 2018, De Smet et al., 2023, Kurscheidt et al., 2025, 2026], as well as uncertainty estimation [Marconato et al., 2024, Ruggieri and Pugnana, 2025, Bortolotti et al., 2026] and fine-tuning [Calanzone et al., 2025]. 9Auto-Formalizing Neuro-Symbolic Predictors A P REPRINT

Constructing the task descriptions. Each task is presented to the LLM using a fixed prompt scaffold (Section F), instantiated with two hand-written components: a constraint description and a variable list .We provide the constraint description in two variants with different levels of granularity. The detailed 

description presents each rule and explicitly lists the background facts that should not be encoded. The not detailed description details the same intended task more concisely in ordinary prose, leaving more of the rule decomposition to the model and, in some cases, leaving constraints or modeling conventions implicit (e.g., “the car may move forward iff it is allowed to proceed and the road ahead is clear”). Full prompts are shown in Section D.3.2. Both descriptions, as well as the per-variable descriptions, were independently written by two of the authors, who first impersonated an expert and then a non-expert who at least knew the task they wanted to solve. The authors then compared their versions clause by clause and reconciled all discrepancies until reaching full agreement; the released texts are the resulting consensus versions. Thus, the constraint-description explicitness is the only factor that differs between the natural and formalized conditions: the surrounding prompt template, variable numbering, and injected variable list are byte-identical across conditions.

I.1 Auto-Formalization 

In this section, we present additional results along the three axes of inputs, models, and outputs. We also provide an error analysis to better understand the errors of the generated formalizations. This investigation is divided into four components, i.e., input-level analysis, model-level analysis, output-level analysis, error analysis. 

I.1.1 Input-Level Analysis How does problem complexity influence auto-formalization performance? Beyond the prompt granularity studied in the main text (Section 5.1), auto-formalization performance may also depend on problem complexity. We consider two quantifiable proxies for complexity: the number of variables and clauses in the ground-truth DIMACS formula. Intuitively, larger formulas impose a greater formalization burden, as the model must track more variables and consistently encode more logical interactions, increasing the risk of omissions and inconsistencies. To conduct this analysis, for each benchmark, we average the F1 scores across model configurations for problems of different sizes, considering the number of variables and clauses separately. As formula sizes span several orders of magnitude, we use their base-10 logarithms and fit a separate univariate linear regression for each dataset and complexity measure. The resulting slope represents the expected change in mean F1 for a tenfold increase in the number of variables or clauses. We also report the R2 metric, which measures the fraction of between-problem variation in F1 explained by the corresponding complexity measure. Figure 10 shows the problem-level observations, fitted regression lines, and 95% confidence intervals. All six fitted slopes are negative: across all three datasets, mean F1 decreases as either the number of variables or clauses increases, suggesting that larger target formulas are generally more difficult to formalize. On 

auto-nesy-bench , a tenfold increase in the number of variables is associated with an F1 decrease of 0.1986 

(R2 = 0 .101 , p = 0 .198 ), while a tenfold increase in the number of clauses corresponds to a decrease of 

0.1391 (R2 = 0 .452 , p = 0 .00223 ). Thus, among the 18 auto-nesy-bench problems, clause count is more informative than variable count, although the small sample size warrants caution. On dcpbench , the slopes for variable and clause count are −0.0947 and −0.0845 , respectively. The corresponding fits explain roughly 28 -

30% of the problem-level variation ( R2 = 0 .303 , p = 4 .9 × 10 −8 for variables; R2 = 0 .281 , p = 1 .84 × 10 −7

for clauses). On satbench , the slopes are −0.1921 for variables ( R2 = 0 .261 , p = 2 .49 × 10 −70 ) and −0.1789 for clauses (R2 = 0 .364 , p = 2 .85 × 10 −104 ), indicating that mean F1 decreases with formula size on this benchmark as well. Overall, larger target formulas are associated with lower auto-formalization performance, but the magnitude of this effect is dataset-dependent. The results also suggest that problem complexity may not be fully captured by the formula size.

How does auto-formalization performance vary with model size? Understanding how model capabilities change with scale is a central question in the study of LLMs. Comparing models with different parameters can reveal whether a capability improves with scale and how consistent this relationship is. We therefore study how auto-formalization performance varies with model size. For each model, we average F1 across the ten configurations formed by two prompting strategies and five output formats. We then fit an ordinary least-squares regression of the mean F1 compared to the LLM parameter count on a log-10 scale. The slope measures the expected change in mean F1 associated with a tenfold increase in model size. Our comparison includes eleven models on auto-nesy-bench , ranging from 8.2B to 117B total parameters, four models on 

dcpbench , and three models on satbench .As shown in Fig. 11, the connection between auto-formalization performance and model size varies across datasets. On auto-nesy-bench , the fitted slope is +0 .3380 , corresponding to an estimated F1 increase of 

0.3380 for a tenfold increase in parameter count. The correlation is moderate ( r = 0 .506 ), with model size explaining 25 .6% of the between-model variation ( R2 = 0 .256 ); however, the association is not statistically significant ( p = 0 .113 ). With only eleven models spanning different architectures, model families, and training procedures, this result does not establish a general scaling law. On dcpbench , the fitted slope is smaller (+0 .0764 ), with R2 = 0 .055 and p = 0 .765 . This association is also not statistically significant, and the regression is based on only four models; it should therefore be interpreted as a descriptive trend rather than evidence of general scaling. On satbench , the fitted slope is weakly negative ( −0.0948 ), with r = −0.161 ,

R2 = 0 .026 , and p = 0 .897 . The 117B gpt-oss-120b achieves higher F1 than the 80B qwen3-coder-next 

(0.963 vs. 0.648 ), but the 31B gemma4-31b also substantially outperforms the latter ( 0.936 vs. 0.648 ). With only three models, this comparison cannot establish a scaling relationship. Model size also does not fully determine performance. On auto-nesy-bench , gpt-oss-20b and olmo3-32b-think achieve F1 scores of 0.505 and 0.569 , respectively, outperforming the larger 70B deepseek-r1-llama-70b (0.412 ) and 80B 

qwen3-coder-next (0.478 ). Even qwen3-8b achieves an F1 of 0.372 . Overall, larger models tend to perform better on auto-nesy-bench and dcpbench , but this trend does not extend to satbench , and parameter count explains only part of the observed variation. I.1.3 Output-Level Analysis Which output format achieves the best performance? The proposed pipeline allows an LLM to generate a CNF in one of five output formats: DIMACS , NAT , PySAT , CPMpy , or SymPy code. Each representation is then deterministically compiled into DIMACS , providing a common representation for evaluation. The choice of output format substantially affects performance. In the main paper, we report the average performance of each output format across matched runs. Here, we break down these results by model to examine how individual models perform across output formats. Figure 12 shows the F1 score for each model-format pair, averaged over the two prompting strategies (ZS and ZS-CoT). Output formats are ordered from highest to lowest according to their mean F1 across models. Models are similarly ordered according to their mean F1 across output formats. Code-based formats perform best on average on auto-nesy-bench . CPMpy achieves the highest across-model mean F1 at 0.575 , followed by SymPy at 0.549 . PySAT ranks third at 0.453 , while direct DIMACS generation reaches 0.364 and NAT performs worst at 0.331 . Thus, compared with generating the final representation directly, the two strongest higher-level formats improve mean F1 by approximately 0.19 -0.21 . This suggests that allowing models to generate a high-level symbolic program may make formalization easier than requiring them to construct the low-level DIMACS encoding directly, potentially because code is more prevalent in LLM training data. However, the best output format varies across models. The strongest model overall on auto-nesy-bench ,

gpt-oss-120b , achieves its highest F1 with CPMpy (0.954 ), compared with 0.869 for PySAT , 0.841 for SymPy ,

0.664 for DIMACS , and 0.604 for NAT . In contrast, SymPy performs best for several other competitive models, including olmo3-32b-think (0.707 ), gpt-oss-20b (0.693 ), and qwen-8b (0.582 ). qwen-coder-next 

performs similarly with PySAT (0.610 ) and CPMpy (0.608 ). Thus, while CPMpy and SymPy are the strongest formats on average, choosing the format for a specific model can yield substantial additional gains. A similar preference for code-based formats emerges on dcpbench , although the best format varies more substantially across models. Across models, CPMpy achieves the highest mean F1 ( 0.247 ), followed by 

PySAT (0.207 ) and SymPy (0.206 ). At the model level, however, deepseek-r1-llama-70b performs best with SymPy (0.109 ), gpt-oss-120b with PySAT (0.419 ), gpt-oss-20b with CPMpy (0.250 ), and 

qwen-coder-next with CPMpy (0.233 ). No single code-based format is therefore consistently best across models. The pattern is different on satbench . Here, NAT is the strongest format on average across models, achieving an F1 of 0.920 . It is also the strongest format for gpt-oss-120b (0.973 ) and qwen-coder-next (0.808 ), while gemma4-31b performs best with CPMpy (0.989 ), compared with 0.979 for NAT . Thus, the advantage of code-based representations observed on auto-nesy-bench and dcpbench does not transfer uniformly across datasets. Overall, these results show that no single output format consistently dominates across models and datasets. Higher-level code representations, particularly CPMpy and SymPy , perform well on auto-nesy-bench and 

dcpbench , whereas NAT is substantially stronger on satbench . The choice of output format should therefore be viewed as a model- and dataset-dependent factor in formalization performance.

I.2.3 What if the Constraints are Soft? Semantic Loss Downstream Results with Detailed Prompts 

In addition to the SPL results, we further analyze the downstream performance under a different NeSy approach based on the semantic loss (SL ) [Xu et al., 2018], which incorporates logical constraints as a penalty loss rather than explicitly enforcing them during prediction. Specifically, we replicate the same SPL analysis ( CPMpy 

+CoT and detailed prompt in Section 5.2) using the SL .

Semantic Loss . Given an input x and an unconstrained predictive distribution pθ (y | x), SL [Xu et al., 2018] incorporates a logical constraint K by maximizing the probability mass assigned to its satisfying assignments. Assuming conditional independence of the variables given the model outputs, it is defined as

Thus, the sum computes the total probability assigned to all satisfying assignments, and the negative logarithm penalizes the model when this probability is low. The sum in Eq. Implementation . For these experiments, we reuse the same SDDs compiled for the SPL experiments. At training time, the SDD is loaded using KLay [Maene et al., 2025], which makes the WMC, and hence of LSL ,faster. Unlike SPL , which explicitly incorporates logical constraints into the prediction mechanism, SL treats 

K as a soft constraint through an additional differentiable loss term. In our setting, it is combined with the downstream task objective as 

Ltotal (K, p θ ) = Ltask (pθ ) + λLSL (K, p θ ), (17) where λ controls the strength of the logical constraint. As all the benchmarks in auto-nesy-bench are classification tasks, Ltask corresponds to the negative log-likelihood, while for all SL experiments λ is set to 1.

Architectures . SL uses the same per-task backbones as SPL . We only replace the SPL head, which outputs the parameters of the circuit, with a classification head: an MLP with three hidden layers with ReLU activations, whose width is set to the SPL head width tuned for that task, and one output per variable of the CNF. Each output is passed through a sigmoid, so that pθ factorizes into independent Bernoulli distributions over the variables; this is the distribution on which LSL computes the probability of satisfying K, with the positive and negative literal weights given by log pθ and log(1 − pθ ). At test time, each variable is predicted by thresholding its probability at 0.5; for tasks with a single multi-class output, we predict the class whose output-bit pattern is most likely.

Table 36 propose the same SL experiments from Table 30 but using formulas generated from non-detailed prompts. equal formulas ( =) behave similarly to those generated from detailed prompts: they never reduce consistency by more than 0.01 and provide the same gains on all the tasks, e.g., sushi (+0 .89 to +0 .91 

CONS ) and sudoku (+0 .10 to +0 .31 CONS , +0 .08 to +0 .15 F1), while leaving downstream performance mostly unaffected. However, only about half of the produced formulas are exactly correct ( 9/16 , 7/12 , and 

6/13 for gpt-oss-120b , olmo3-32b-think , and qwen3-8b , respectively), and the non-equal formulas are often harmful. With detailed prompts, only a single formula reduced consistency; here, the strict and 

incomparable formulas reduce consistency on most of the tasks, often by a large margin: kand-logic-2

of the concepts. Unlike SPL , however, SL can still be trained with such a formula as it is used as a loss function. The reason is the same as for the too-strict formula in Table 30. If the formula excludes some assignments that are actually present in the training data, the two losses give conflicting signals: the classification loss encourages the model to predict the ground-truth assignments, while the semantic loss pushes it away from them because they violate the formula. The model therefore ends up in a compromise that may satisfy neither the formula nor the ground-truth labels. As a result, the average consistency gain of SL over the unconstrained model drops from about +0 .10 with detailed prompts to +0 .03 for gpt-oss-120b and qwen3-8b , and becomes negative for olmo3-32b-think (−0.02 ). The mean ∆F1 ranges from −0.04 to −0.01 . Permissive formulas ( ⇐) remain harmless, as with detailed prompts and for SPL . On cifar10 , formulas admitting about 19 × more assignments than the ground truth still slightly improve consistency ( +0 .02 ). Similarly, the permissive gpt-oss-120b formula for warcraft improves both F1 ( +0 .06 ) and consistency ( +0 .07 ), since a superset of the valid assignments can still exclude some invalid ones. Overall, SL is more robust than SPL 

to imperfect formulas because it uses the formula as a soft training signal rather than enforcing it on every prediction. As a result, incorrect formulas do not necessarily prevent training, although they can still hurt performance when they conflict with the ground truth.
