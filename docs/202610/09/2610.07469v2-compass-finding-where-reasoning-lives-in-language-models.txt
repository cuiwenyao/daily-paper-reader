## Abstract 

Explicitly eliciting reasoning substantially improves LLM performance. Existing approaches require a predefined characterization of reasoning, whether through CoT prompt design, contrastive CoT directions, or via SAE derived reasoning features. For mathematical reasoning with verifiable answers, we show that a much simpler signal suffices, which is the correctness of the model’s own direct answer attempts. This signal yields a latent direction that elicits reasoning. This direction is decodable within the activations of most attention heads, but only a small subset of them can be effectively intervened. We introduce COMPASS, an inference-time steering method that identifies these heads using a logit-space attribution score and steers their activations along the correctness direction, requiring only per-head activation statistics. Across three model families and multiple math benchmarks, COMPASS outperforms the activation-steering baselines we compare against, improves GSM8K accuracy by 16 percentage points on average, and approaches CoT accuracy with 20-70% fewer generated tokens. Interventions transfer without re-fitting to unseen benchmarks, and ablations show that both the correctness direction and the small set of heads carrying it are necessary, with the effect concentrated in remarkably few heads. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit strong reasoning capabilities across a wide range of tasks [1, 2]. A standard approach for eliciting reasoning is chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [3], which encourages the model to generate intermediate steps before producing a final answer. Recent work, however, suggests that reasoning-relevant computation also resides in the model’s hidden states and can be elicited without explicit CoT prompting [4, 5, 6], indicating that internal representations contain meaningful computational signatures that can be intervened upon. For instance, Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) have been used to elicit reasoning by identifying a reasoning-associated feature, though doing so requires a contrast condition prompt and a separate SAE per layer [4]. More broadly, linear probes, including probes trained on individual attention head activations, have been shown to decode or steer behavioral properties such as truthfulness from internal states. However only such specific properties have been steered so far, not reasoning [7, 8, 9, 10]. Each of these approaches requires some predefined characterization of reasoning, whether through a contrast prompt, a trained auxiliary model, or a property specific probe. For mathematical reasoning with verifiable answers, which is our focus, whether an intervention can be found without additional requirements and specifications remains underexplored. Surprisingly, we find that an effective reasoning intervention can be elicited from a signal as simple as a direction separating correctly and incorrectly answered samples, requiring no CoT demonstra-

The key remaining challenge is where to intervene. Correctness information is decodable across many attention heads. However, this decodability alone is not causally sufficient for effective steering [8, 7]. Unlike SAE-based approaches, COMPASS needs no trained auxiliary model, only the per-head mean activation computed separately over correct and incorrect samples, making the method as cheap to deploy as it is effective. Remarkably, COMPASS is never provided with a representation of reasoning itself, yet steering the selected heads elicits concise intermediate computations that often convert incorrect direct answers into correct solutions. Ablations show that both the correctness direction and its intervention location are necessary, with much of the effect concentrated in only a few heads . The identified interventions further transfer without re-fitting to GSM-Plus [16] and HARP [17]. Our main contributions are: • Reasoning intervention from outcome supervision alone. We show that correct/incorrect outcomes help recover an effective reasoning intervention, without CoT demonstrations, predefined reasoning directions, or identified reasoning features. • Attribution-based steering. We introduce COMPASS, which combines per-head correct-ness directions with their predicted downstream logit effect to identify effective intervention sites. • Concentrated internal control. Controlled head and direction ablations show that both, what is injected and where it is injected matter, with much of the intervention effect concentrated in remarkably few attention heads. • Accuracy, efficiency, and transfer. Across three model families and multiple reasoning benchmarks, COMPASS closes much of the gap to explicit CoT, with substantially shorter generations and without any re-fitting on unseen distributions, demonstrating that the same lightweight intervention is simultaneously accurate, efficient, and general. 

## 2 Related Work 

Eliciting Reasoning from LLMs. The reasoning capabilities of LLMs are most commonly elicited through chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [3, 1], where performance improves significantly as the model is made to think through intermediate steps before answering. However, prompting is not the only route to reasoning. Recent work shows that the internal states of the LLM carry useful computational signatures that can be extracted directly, without relying on the prompt alone. Fractional reasoning [18] takes a direct prompt and a CoT prompt, identifies the linear direction separating their latent activations, and shifts the model into a controlled reasoning regime along this direction. In a similar spirit, sparse autoencoder features extracted from direct and CoT prompts have been used to uncover a latent reasoning mode [4], and contrastive prompt pairs have been used to steer metacognitive behaviors in [19]. Each of these methods requires some prior specification of what reasoning should look like, rather than discovering it directly from the model’s own behavior. In contrast, COMPASS requires no such explicit specifications or requirements. 

Probing Hidden States of LLMs. A second line of work extracts information from hidden states via probing, using simple classifiers, typically linear, trained on activations to predict a target property. Inference-time intervention [7] trains such probes at every attention head and shifts activations along the probe direction at the heads where properties (e.g., truthfulness) are most detectable. Follow-up 2work refined how the probe guides generation [8, 10], and applied probing to new targets, including whether a model believes its own answer is correct [20], whether a math problem is solvable at all [21], and whether code is correct before it is written [22]. However, each method trains a probe on a single, curated property, such as truthfulness, honesty, or solvability, requiring its own labeled dataset, and only a subset of these properties has been shown to be steerable at all and none for reasoning. Our steering or intervention direction requires only a labeled set of single-pass generations, split by the LLM’s own correctness, with heads selected by their causal effect on the output rather than by property detectability alone. 

Activation Steering in LLMs. A third line of work steers behavior directly through activations rather than through the prompt. This traces back to in-context learning [23, 24], where prompt demonstra-tions change model behavior by shifting internal activations, an effect shown to be compressible into a single latent task vector [25]. In-context vectors made this actionable by distilling demonstrations into a vector added back during inference [26]. Subsequent methods derived directions from paired prompts [27, 28] or curated datasets [29], applied steering conditionally on the input [30], synthesized query specific vectors from a mixture of steering experts [31], or restricted intervention to an invariant low rank subspace of reasoning trajectories [32]. Each requires paired task demonstrations, a separate forward pass, or a labeling scheme. Our steering direction requires none of these, extracted instead from the model’s own outcome labels in a single pass. 

## 3 Proposed Approach 

In this section, we present COMPASS, a method that recovers a coarse correctness direction from a model’s own hidden states, identifies the attention heads that causally carry this direction to the output, and steers only those heads to elicit reasoning. Our key insight builds on the observations of [7, 8] that behavioral properties such as truthfulness or honesty, properties that at a high level resemble correctness, are decodable from the activations of several attention heads. However, only a small subset of these heads causally affect the output, which we define as a head whose intervened activation changes the model’s generated answer, as opposed to merely correlating with it. We introduce a selection procedure that distinguishes these causal heads from the many that merely encode correctness as a decodable signal. We find that the causal effect is concentrated in remarkably few heads. COMPASS answers two questions keeping the LLM frozen: (i) which direction in activation space separates the model’s correct attempts from its incorrect ones (§3.1), and (ii) which attention heads carry this direction into the output when it is intervened upon (§3.2).

Equation (5) leaves the choice of heads open. The direction θhl from Eq. (3) exists for every head, since a difference of means can be computed regardless of whether that head’s output has any effect on the model’s generation. Decodability, in this sense, only requires that correct and incorrect activations differ on average, it places no constraint on whether adding δhl to the layer input changes the final output. Selecting heads by decodability alone can therefore result in heads with no causal effect, or with an effect that opposes the intended direction. This is confirmed empirically in the ITI row of Table 1, where steering the heads that decode correctness best yields little or no improvement, and is at times detrimental. On MATH-500, it lowers Llama-3.1-8B accuracy from 40.8% to 23.6%, and Qwen3-4B from 53.8% to 30.6%. A head is useful for intervention only if its contribution δhl changes the model’s output. Decodability in activation space, as used to define θhl , only tells us that correct and incorrect activations differ, not whether that difference affects the generated token. Basic Setup. For every sample, we use a fixed, LLM specific brief answer prompt that instructs the model to produce a short answer without explanation. Full prompt details are in Appendix A.1. On the training split, we run this prompt once per sample and store the activation of every attention head at the last prompt token, prior to generation. Each generation is then graded against the ground truth answer, labeling the sample correct or incorrect. Full grading details are in Appendix A.2. For every head, we obtain a correctness direction as the difference between the mean activation over correct samples and the mean activation over incorrect samples. α is a hyperparameter controlling the intensity of the intervention, and we select α separately for every dataset through a hyperparameter search. Evaluation is a single zero shot pass on the test split of each dataset.

4.1 COMPASS Predominantly Outperforms Steering Baselines 

Table 1 reports accuracy and mean generated tokens for the direct answer baseline (standard prompt), ITI [7], Fractional Reasoning [18], and COMPASS, with the CoT and frontier model (GPT-5.4 mini) reported as reference. COMPASS improves over the direct answer baseline on every model and dataset, and over both ITI and Fractional Reasoning on every model and dataset except Gemma4-12B on SVAMP, where Fractional Reasoning performs best. On GSM8K, COMPASS closes most of the gap to the CoT reference on Llama and Qwen3, reaching 83.7% against an 83.4% ceiling and 89.8% against 92.3% respectively, and recovers a substantial part of the gap on Gemma4-12B, reaching 88.0% against a 96.1% ceiling. It does so while generating 20 to 70% fewer tokens than CoT across datasets, for instance 68 versus 224 tokens on Qwen3 SVAMP. COMPASS thus achieves accuracy comparable to or approaching explicit reasoning at a fraction of its token cost. 

4.2 COMPASS Transfers Without Refitting to Unseen Datasets 

Table 2 reports accuracy on GSM-Plus and HARP using the direction, heads, and α obtained from the training splits of GSM8K and MATH-500 respectively, applied without modification. On GSM-Plus, accuracy rises from 60.4% to 71.1% on Llama and from 65.6% to 80.4% on Qwen3. On HARP, accuracy increases from 21.5% to 22.7% on Llama and from 35.4% to 49.1% on Qwen3. GSM-Plus consists of adversarial modifications of GSM8K test problems, and HARP is a harder competition level dataset than MATH-500. Since the directions and heads were never exposed to either dataset, this transfer indicates that they capture a property of the model rather than a fit to the source dataset.

## 5 Conclusions and Future Work 

We presented COMPASS, an inference-time steering framework that recovers a reasoning intervention from outcome supervision alone, without chain of thought demonstrations or a predefined notion of reasoning. A single forward pass over a pool of graded problems yields a per-head correctness direction and a logit-space attribution score, together identifying the few heads whose contribution shifts the first generated token toward a correct answer. Across three model families and multiple math benchmarks, COMPASS outperforms existing activation-steering baselines, closes much of the gap to explicit chain of thought at a fraction of the token cost, and transfers to unseen datasets without re-fitting. While effective in this setting, COMPASS opens several directions for future work. Our attribution score is defined for domains with a binary notion of correctness, such as short-answer math questions. Extending it to open-ended tasks such as proof generation, where outcomes are not directly gradable, is a natural next step. Similarly, while our ablations identify which heads causally drive correctness, a mechanistic account of why these specific heads emerge, and why they promote tokens that initiate correct derivations, is left to future work. We view COMPASS as a step toward using a model’s own outcomes, rather than externally imposed notions of reasoning, to identify and steer the internal computation that produces them.

A.2 Grading 

Free form generations do not reliably carry a marker such as \boxed{} . A correct answer that ends in “ . . . = 109 33 ” is extracted as “33” by a naive last number rule. Grading therefore proceeds in two passes over every generation. The first pass is deterministic. An answer is extracted from the raw generation and compared with the gold answer through a fixed cascade. The cascade checks normalized string equality, then numeric equality at relative tolerance 10 −6 with percent signs and unit tails stripped, then reduction of a derivation chain to its final value, and finally bounded symbolic equivalence through SymPy. We call the resulting label the single pass grade. The second pass follows Fractional Reasoning [18] and uses an LLM formatter. A second call to the same model, never steered, restates the generation as a single line of the form Answer: \[\boxed{...}\] , and the same deterministic cascade then grades the boxed answer. The system message of the formatting call tells the model that its job is to extract the final short answer from the more detailed answer. The user prompt depends on the model and the dataset as follows. 12 Every model uses the prompt below on every dataset, The placeholder {reasoning} carries the generation being graded.     

> Formatting prompt
> Here is a worked solution: {reasoning} Copy its final answer, exactly as the solution states it, into this line and output nothing else: Answer: \[ \boxed{your_answer_here} \] Do not solve the problem. Do not explain. Do not recompute anything. If the solution states no final answer, box its last expression.

The final label of a row is the OR of the single pass grade and the formatted answer grade. Only rows the single pass grade marks incorrect are sent to the formatting call, so the formatter can promote a row whose answer the extraction missed but can never demote a row the deterministic cascade already accepts. This is due to the fact that the formatting call is itself a generation and can fail as one. Hence, a regrade that can only add keeps such failures from destroying correct labels. Truncated generations are sent to the formatter as well, since a capped generation can still contain its final answer, and their label follows the same rule. The same instrument grades everything, so every accuracy score comes from a single universal grader. Accuracy is always reported over all rows of the split.

A.5 Baselines and Oracles 

ITI and Fractional Reasoning are re-implemented and run on our models, prompts, splits, token caps, and grader.
