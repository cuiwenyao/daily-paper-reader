Generative Interpretability via Scalable Neuro-Symbolic Models
Xiaocong Yang
AI Interpretability @ Illinois, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
Urbana, IL, USA
xy51@illinois.edu
Abstract
As the use of Large Language Models moves from chatbots into
agentic systems, where outputs become actions with irreversible
consequences on reality, the existing paradigm on AI Interpretabil-
ity research, post-hoc interpretability, is structurally inadequate for
safe and trustworthy model deployment: it explains behavior after
the fact but cannot audit or intervene in an inference computation
before it commits to an output. We therefore argue for a shift toward
generative interpretability, an architectural property under which
a model’s inference pass natively exposes semantically meaning-
ful checkpoints that are human-understandable and amenable to
causal intervention. We show the merits of generative interpretabil-
ity as comparison to other interpretability research paradigms, and
propose Neuro-Symbolic Models as a concrete instantiation.

Introduction
Recent years witnessed the rapid development of connectionist AI
models, especially Large Language Models (LLMs) with unprece-
dented real-world impacts. Although powerful, they are generally
believed to lack interpretability on their internal working mecha-
nisms and are therefore described as "black-box" models [22, 34].
A direct consequence is that it is usually infeasible to understand,
audit and intervene in undesirable model behaviors such as halluci-
nation, sycophancy and harmful content generation in a scalable
and systematic way. The negative consequences of such undesired
model behaviors are dramatically amplified as the trend of deploy-
ing LLMs in agentic systems, such as healthcare systems that help
with diagnoses [44] and autonomous driving systems that control

irreversible consequences that are much more severe than in a

agentic settings for auditable autonomy and operational runtime

In this work, we argue that a paradigm transition is necessary
for interpretability research to meet this requirement. Most current
work on explaining LLMs falls into the category of post-hoc inter-
pretability [23], which relies on reverse-engineering tools external
to the LLM. Such tools are confined to observation: their interpreta-
tions are confounded by the tools’ own approximation errors, and
the tools themselves are usually discarded at deployment rather
than used to make the deployed model safer. As a result, post-hoc
interpretability cannot reliably translate into safer behaviors from
deployed LLMs. Instead, we propose the concept of generative inter-
pretability as an alternative. The key claim from this concept is that,
interpretability should be a property inherent to the model
computations that allows effective audit and intervention at
deployment, directly targeting the research thrusts and challenges
proposed in the DARPA-NSF-CAISI AI Forge report.
2
Post-hoc Interpretability and Its Limitations
Post-hoc interpretability refers to the property where the computa-
tions and behaviors of an already-trained model can be attributed
and grounded into human understandable terms [23]. Based on
the explanandum, they can be summarized into two categories:
anatomical interpretability and physiological interpretability.
2.1
Anatomical Interpretability
Anatomical interpretability studies local structures in a model that
function stably and consistently over different inputs, usually ac-
companied by modularity and functional specialization. For exam-
ple, the anatomy of multi-head self-attention layers (MHAs) and
feed-forward networks (FFNs) in a Transformer [42] have been
thoroughly studied before the emergence of LLMs. For example,
the MHAs in a BERT model [11] are discovered to specialize in pro-
cessing certain semantic or syntactic information in input [9]. The
FFNs in a Transformer are usually interpreted as factual knowledge
banks [10, 15], which can be edited to influence model outputs on
certain facts [26].

2.2
Physiological Interpretability
Physiological interpretability recovers the end-to-end information
flow of a specific inference pass executed by the model. The theoreti-
cal basis can be traced back to causal mediation analysis [31], where
the computation flow on a given input sample is characterized as
a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) and interventions are applied to
intermediate nodes to study the causal effect on model output. Early
efforts in this category include probing [4] and naive saliency trac-
ing [20, 37, 38]. With the development of LLMs, the Transformer
Circuit Theory [13] and its follow-up work [8, 12, 21] are proposed
and become one of the most cutting-edge techniques in post-hoc
interpretability. Instead of performing naive, metric-based tracing,
Transformer Circuit Theory targets reducing an inference pass by
LLMs to combinations of simplified operations on semantically
meaningful directions called features in model activation spaces.
These work systematically study and answer three key questions
centered around features: (1) how are features distributed in activa-
tion spaces (quasi-orthogonally); (2) how to identify features and
their meanings (via dictionary learning); and (3) how does a model
use these meaningful features to generate its output (via circuit
tracing with attribution graphs).
2.3
The Shared Fundamental Limitation
The work from post-hoc interpretability gives us insights to better
understand LLMs. However, all of them have a fundamental limita-
tion: they can explain what a model does, but cannot change
what it does, in a systematic and scalable way. On the one hand,
post-hoc interpretability tools are far from as effective in inference-
time intervention as they are in representation explaining. For
example, Basu et al. [3] documented a large gap between a model’s
internal representations and its outputs that steering interventions
based on different post-hoc interpretability tools failed to reliably
close. On the other hand, post-hoc interpretability tools suffer from
severe scalability and/or efficiency problems that block them from
being deployed outside of laboratory environment. Those powerful
interpretability tools in Transformer Circuit Theory, such as Sparse
Autoencoders and Cross-layer Transcoders, cannot be efficiently
integrated into LLMs to be deployed, due to the extremely large
feature dimensions used to encourage sparse and clean feature de-
composition. Other lightweight tools, such as model editing [43]
and activation patching [52], are limited to modifying a specific
type of model behavior with the dependency on labeled data related
to that behavior, and hence far from scalable and systematic.
3
Generative Interpretability
The empirical failure of intervention with post-hoc interpretabil-
ity tools directly motivates the necessity of a new interpretability
paradigm that is tailored for effective audit. In this section, we
articulate generative interpretability as the appropriate require-
ment on models to procure inference-time audit and controllable
intervention.
3.1
Definition
We define generative interpretability as follows.
Definition 1 (Generative Interpretability). An archi-
tectural property of a model under which its inference pass1
exposes intermediate checkpoints that are (i) semantically mean-
ingful to humans natively and (ii) causally intervenable by
construction.
Human understandability. Interpretability in AI is typically
framed in terms of human social attribution and cognition [7, 27].
By semantically meaningful to humans, we require that a checkpoint
to be mapped to a human-readable concept. Audit and intervention
ultimately reflect human decisions and values, and they can only
identify undesired model behaviors when the intermediate states
are expressed in terms they understand. This excludes interpreta-
tions that are only transparent in terms of mathematical function-
alities. For instance, an optimization view yields a mathematically
interpretable Transformer [49], whose layers have well-defined
roles such as token set compression and sparsification, yet it does
not reveal any semantic meanings the model assigns during pro-
cessing inputs. By native, we require the acquisition of meanings
not to rely on external tools to recover. For example, the framework
by Mueller et al. [28] separates the choice of checkpoints from the
discovery of their meanings; whether the checkpoints are Atten-
tion heads, neurons or activation subspaces, their meanings rely on
post-hoc interpretability tools to search and assign, and therefore,
it cannot avoid the key defects of post-hoc interpretability.
Causal intervention. An interpretability framework is consid-
ered faithful when reflecting the real reasoning process of a model
processing inputs [14, 17]. In particular, the framework should be
able to be tested with “do-intervention” [30] and the counterfactual
output should align with the interpretation consistently, as argued
in [29]. This is important to achieve predictable model behaviors
when imposing intervention, although this is usually not the case
with post-hoc interpretability tools. The requirement on causal in-
tervention directly implies that the checkpoints need to be variables
the inference pass functionally depends on, where altering their
values necessarily propagates through the downstream computa-
tion and so do-interventions have well-defined counterfactuals by
construction, instead of contingent and approximate causal effects
as with post-hoc interpretability tools.
3.2
Two Axes of Interpretability
The definition above commits to two requirements but leaves open
how generative interpretability relates to existing interpretability
paradigms. We organize this comparison along two axes capturing
distinct dimensions of commitment: when interpretation is avail-
able relative to inference (the temporal axis), and where along the
inference path human-readable structures reside (the spatial axis).
The temporal axis. We divide interpretability approaches into
three categories based on when human-understandable states are
available relative to model inference. Most traditional machine
learning models, such as linear regression models, decision trees,

a naive application of already-readable patterns. Rudin [34] advo-

performance by a fully interpretable, domain-specific model [16].
Those models are usually described as white-box models. The afore-
mentioned post-hoc interpretability methods recover interpretable
states only after inference: the human-understandable states are
not natively exposed by the model but reconstructed by external
tools from completed inference passes. The third category, where
interpretable states emerge during inference passes, has drawn less
attention. A representative work is Concept Bottleneck Models
(CBMs) [19] and its extension to LLMs [36], where the computa-
tions from neural networks are grounded in semantic concepts
from a pre-defined, human-understandable concept pool as the
final component in inference paths. However, these models are
usually limited to simple similarity matching in a specific domain.
The spatial axis. Interpretability approaches can also be catego-
rized by the spatial position of interpretable states in an inference
path. As a baseline, the vanilla LLM and interpretations based on
it (such as Chain-of-thought [46]) offer human-readable represen-
tations in form of text tokens only at input/output boundaries. All
post-hoc interpretability methods rely on external tools to construct
interpretations that lie externally to the model. The CBM family
provide an interpretable interior checkpoint layer that allows simple
concept matching, and most white-box models are interpretable
throughout the model by definition.
The position of generative interpretability. Along both axes,
generative interpretability occupies an intermediate position. Tem-
porally, it resides during inference. A checkpoint’s schema (con-
cept space) is usually pre-defined, while the values filling it are pro-
duced by an opaque sub-computation (such as a neural network) as
the input is processed to that checkpoint. The interpretable states,
therefore, can neither be derived from the static model alone, nor
relying on the full inference pass to be completed. Spatially, it re-
quires multiple interior checkpoints for the purpose of effective
causal intervention. Boundary readability exposes only inputs and
outputs, and a single checkpoint captures only one cross-section of
a multi-step computation within a model, which is insufficient to
attribute undesired output (this will be discussed more in Section
3.3).
3.3
Generative Interpretability as the Minimal
Sufficient Commitment for Auditable LLMs
We argue that, for LLMs, generative interpretability is the minimal
architectural commitment sufficient to make audit and intervention
over the inference pass that is scalable in development and compu-
tationally tractable at deployment. The commitment is prescriptive:
a property of the models we should build on existing LLMs, not as
they are.
A partial order by auditable content. The axes induce partial
orders on commitment strength. Spatially, a throughout-interpretable
model is legible at any interior point, subsuming multiple-checkpoint
readability, which subsumes a single checkpoint and then the input-
output boundary. Temporally, a model legible before inference re-
mains legible during it when given an input. Post-hoc interpretabil-
ity lies outside this chain, as its readable structure is reconstructed
externally rather than residing in the model, and therefore implies
no commitment on the model architectures.
Why not weaker. Auditing content generation before it goes
to output rules out after-inference interpretability immediately,
whose readable state is recovered only once the output—and any
unsafe content—already exists. During inference is thus the weakest
temporal position that still permits pre-generation audit. Spatially,
two weaker commitments fall short. The input-output boundary
(vanilla LLMs, CoT) exposes no intermediate checkpoints on the
inference path. Note that any audit on CoT’s intermediate steps is
based on tokens that are already generated via inference passes, so
it can only reject an undesired generation rather than attribute to
the computation that produces it and correct accordingly. More-
over, the intermediate steps in a CoT are not faithfully reflecting
the internal computation [2, 35, 40], making intervention on them
unreliable [41]. A single interior checkpoint as in CBM audits one
cross-section, and in the setting of LLMs, it cannot attribute an
undesired output to the computation either, as empirically verified
in [3]. From a theoretical perspective, as proposed by Poggio [32],
any efficiently computable function, characterized by efficient Tur-
ing computability, is provably compositionally sparse with a DAG
structure. Therefore, to meet the requirement of causal intervention
as in Definition 1, we need at least multiple checkpoints to model
such structures.
Why not stronger. One might demand a stronger, white-box-
style commitment. However, such semantic throughout-readability
harms the representational capacity of the model. A representative
is the effort to achieve monosemanticity of neurons or directions
in activation spaces, supporting clean mapping between semantic
concepts and model structures. However, this is infeasible for LLMs,
which must represent far more meaningful concepts than they have
dimensions, packing them into near-orthogonal directions through
superposition [12]. A 𝑑-dimensional space admits exponentially
many such near-orthogonal directions [18], and forcing every direc-
tion to be monosemantic would abandon this packing and collapse
the number of meaningful concepts from exponential to linear in
the dimension. Generative interpretability, however, only commits
interpretability at limited checkpoints, leaving most of the model
unconstrained to represent knowledge efficiently.

Neuro-Symbolic Model as an Instance with
Generative Interpretability
In the development of AI history, Neuro-Symbolic Model [6] is a
successful instance that exploits the benefits from both Connec-
tionism and Symbolism AI paradigms. From the perspective of
information compression, neural and symbolic models process raw
input data in fundamentally different ways. Symbolic systems com-
press by projecting onto a pre-defined schema vocabulary, and the
representations are discrete and hard-bounded that generalize in an
algebraic manner [24], naturally providing human-understandable
states. Neural networks, however, compress by projecting onto

4.1
Symbolic Components as Checkpoints
Unlike the vague patterns in neural networks, the representations
in a symbolic system are human-understandable by construction.
The causality requirement immediately selects among different
neuro-symbolic architectures that meets Definition 1. Using the
categorization criteria proposed in [6], the Type-3 Neuro-Symbolic
Models, where the neural and symbolic components work in a
cooperative way in a feedback loop, best satisfy this requirement. A
representative Type-3 model is NLProlog [45], where an inference
pass consists of composable proof steps represented by predicate
symbols, and similarity scoring produced by text embeddings inside
each step stays opaque. The proof steps thus form multiple interior
checkpoints within a single inference pass, each auditable against
the expected intermediate representation.
4.2
Challenges of Building Neuro-Symbolic
Models with LLMs
Neuro-Symbolic paradigm is a promising candidate to mitigate
the black-box nature of LLMs given by the intrinsic generative
interpretability. However, most existing Neuro-Symbolic Models
are built on a small-sized neural network component and confined
to a specific task domain, as there are several key challenges that
block scaling up.
Challenge 1: Construction of a universal, general-purpose
symbolic system. In existing Neuro-Symbolic Models, it is feasible
to build the symbolic components from scratch by human experts
for a narrow, closed use case and domain, such as logic rules for
math theorem proving. However, this is intractable for building a
universal symbolic system covering as many domains as an LLM
does for general purpose, such as the Cyc project [50] starting
from 1980s but paused in recent years of “information explosion”. A
promising alternative approach is to build upon enormous existing
symbolic systems from all different domains and use data-driven
approaches to align, merge and extend into a colossal one to match
the scope of LLMs, as is being studied by us concurrently.
Challenge 2: Integration of neural and symbolic compo-
nents. Although the Type-3 Neuro-Symbolic Model mentioned
earlier gives us a sketchy prototype of the fusion of neural and
symbolic components, the existing instances from this type usu-
ally simplify into a rule-based, fixed workflow template that only
works on a specific task. It seems difficult to characterize a universal
workflow pattern that guides the design of general Neuro-Symbolic
Models. A compromising approach is to abstract the shared work-
flow template for a task category and build Neuro-Symbolic Models
for it, such as the Matching Neural Network for Information Re-
trieval proposed by Zhai [51], while the shared symbolic structure
of the workflow is usually oversimplified.
Challenge 3: The boundary between neural and symbolic
components for appropriate generalization. The aforemen-
tioned difference of information compression echoes a classical
topic of knowledge itself in philosophy. Some knowledge is gov-
erned by a continuous structure of similarity. For example, whether
two unseen words are near-synonyms is judged by graded similar-
ity instead of by any finite rule. Wittgenstein [47] coined a term
“family resemblance” to refer to such knowledge, and neural net-
works are naturally preferred to be used for their representation.
Other knowledge is governed by discrete, exact rules. For instance,
the correctness of a hash or of formal syntax is rule-defined, and
any minor departure is simply wrong rather than approximately
right, whose properties can be well captured by symbolic mod-
els. Characterizing knowledge precisely, therefore, is meaningful
for determining the proper boundary of the neural and symbolic
components in a Neuro-Symbolic Model.
5
Conclusion
We argued for a paradigm shift on AI interpretability research from
explaining trained models (observation) to building auditable mod-
els (intervention). Generative interpretability formalizes this: an ar-
chitecture exposes semantically meaningful, human-understandable,
and causally intervenable checkpoints along its inference pass, and
Neuro-Symbolic Models instantiate this paradigm. We want to
emphasize that this shift is not optional in agentic AI settings, es-
pecially in high-stake scenarios such as healthcare, legal system
and autonomous driving where the attribution of obligations and
responsibility is also valued besides average performance on large
samples. The audits at checkpoints on the inference path, ideally,
mark a distinct locus of responsibility, so an undesired outcome
traces to the step that bears it rather than to the system as a whole.
Additionally, this mission requires efforts beyond technical work,
including designing mechanisms of checks and balances on the
power of AI audit, and therefore requires sustained collaborations
among universities, frontier AI labs and governments.
