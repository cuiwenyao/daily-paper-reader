## Abstract 

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting can fail severely on constraint-dense logical reasoning tasks, where unverified errors accumulate silently across steps. We introduce SymStep : an LLM makes one atomic claim at a time ( DEDUCE: Alice, pet, Cat ), then a lightweight con-straint propagator checks the claim for consis-tency with prior accepted deductions, rejects con-tradictions, and cascades implied facts automati-cally. SymStep+G additionally provides an MRV guidance after each accepted step, directing the LLM toward the most constrained unresolved variable. On a 35-puzzle retained subset of Ze-braLogicBench —benchmark of 1,000 Einstein-style logic puzzles—Direct and CoT both achieve 

0% , while SymStep+G reaches 97% . On AR-LSAT analytical reasoning problems, SymStep achieves 100% vs. CoT’s 87%. On LGP-14 ,SymStep+G achieves 100% vs. 0% for CoT and Logic-LM, the strongest prior symbolic +LLM baseline we compare against. Ablation studies re-veal that MRV guidance is a key mechanism for reducing directionless cycling, while consistency checking provides a safety net against explicit contradictions. Across six benchmarks spanning five task domains, SymStep variants match or ex-ceed every baseline on constraint-dense and arith-metic tasks. Experiments on AQUA-RAT alge-bra confirm the advantage is constraint-density-specific. 1

## 2 Problem Formulation 

2.1 Logical Grid Puzzles 

Logical grid puzzles are the clearest way to test step-level deductive reasoning because every fact must follow explicit constraints; there is no world knowledge to fall back on, and any undetected error propagates through all subsequent deductions. In a scenario such as “Alice, Bob, and Carol each have a different pet (cat, dog, fish) and live in differ-ently colored houses (red, blue, green). Alice does not have a dog. Bob lives in the blue house. . . . ” , the goal is to de-termine who has what, using only the given clues. Every attribute assignment must be derived step by step from the constraints. Formally, a problem has n entities (e.g., people) and k

attributes (e.g., pet, job), each with a finite domain of val-ues. Every attribute is bijective : each value is assigned to exactly one entity. The task is to uniquely determine all entity–attribute–value assignments from the given clues. 

Solution uniqueness. We require each puzzle to have 

exactly one valid assignment. This uniqueness is verified by running the constraint propagator on the stored solution and confirming that it reaches a fully determined state with no alternatives, a check that also caught two originally in-cluded puzzles (E3-Sport and M2-Nationals) that each ad-mitted two valid solutions; automated uniqueness checks are mandatory in deductive benchmarks. (See §4.1.) 

2.2 Generalization to Other Reasoning Domains 

While LGPs are our primary benchmark, SymStep’s archi-tecture is domain-agnostic: it applies to any task that can be decomposed into a sequence of atomic constraint asser-tions with checkable consistency . We demonstrate this on three additional domains: 

Scheduling puzzles (SP). Constraint satisfaction prob-lems in a scheduling context, e.g., assigning presenters to conference time slots given ordering and compatibility con-straints. SP share the bijective structure of LGPs but use different domain vocabulary (time slots, rooms, roles) from the LGP distribution, enabling cross-domain transfer tests. 

Math word problems (MWP) and financial reason-ing (FIN). Multi-step numeric reasoning (e.g., deriving em-ployee salaries, compound interest, or tax liabilities from arithmetic relationships). Each D EDUCE step asserts a vari-able’s numeric value. Verifier checks arithmetic consistency and cascades any directly implied values. The MRV hint identifies the next computable unknown: variable whose constraint becomes evaluable given current knowledge. All extensions reuse the same D EDUCE protocol and outer SymStep loop, only the symbolic verifier is adapted per domain. 2.3 Why Step-Level Verification? 

Consider a small puzzle from E2-Jobs (Adam/Beth/Chris, drinks: Tea/Coffee/Water, jobs: Doctor/Teacher/Engineer):                                              

> Adam drinks tea (clue 1). The doctor drinks coffee (clue 2), so Adam is not the doctor. Beth is not the engineer (clue 3). Chris is not the doctor (clue 4). So the doctor must be Beth. Adam is not the teacher (clue 5), so Adam is the engineer. Chris is the teacher. Beth drinks coffee. Therefore: Adam=Tea+ Teacher ,Beth=Coffee+Doctor, Chris=Water+ Engineer .

Although the reasoning chain correctly establishes “Adam is the engineer” in step 4, it contradicts that con-clusion in the final answer (“Adam=Teacher”). The parser receives “Adam=Teacher” as the final answer and marks the puzzle incorrect. This is unverified step accumulation :each new sentence updates the narrative without checking it against what has already been established. The longer the chain, the greater the chance that a contradiction will go unnoticed. SymStep prevents this by modeling reasoning as a se-quence of atomic constraint assertions dt = ( σ(p, a ) = v)

or dt = ( σ(p, a )̸ = v). A deduction is consistent if it does not contradict any previously accepted deduction. The propagator enforces this property at every step, ensuring that no inconsistent fact enters the context. 

## 3 SymStep 

3.1 The D EDUCE Protocol 

SymStep structures LLM–verifier interaction through a minimal DEDUCE protocol : the LLM produces exactly one constraint assertion per step: 

DEDUCE: Entity , attribute , Value 

DEDUCE: Entity , attribute , NOT Value 

This format is parseable by regex, requires no formal logic training, and is far less demanding than generating a com-plete Prolog program or FOL formula. The LLM signals task completion with CONCLUDE: done .

3.2 Constraint Propagator 

The symbolic backbone of SymStep is a constraint prop-agator that maintains a possibility matrix: Π( p, a ) ⊆ Va:a set of values still consistent with all accepted deductions for entity p, attribute a. Initially, Π( p, a ) = Va for all p, a .

Intuition. Π is similar to a grid where each cell lists which values a person might have for a given attribute. When the LLM deduces “Alice has Red,” the propagator (i) crosses Red off the grid for every other person (arc-consistency), and (ii) checks whether any cell has now shrunk to a single option. If so, that forced assignment is propagated automatically, potentially triggering further eliminations without requiring an LLM call. 

Positive update. On σ(p, a ) = v: if v / ∈ Π( p, a ), return 

> CONTRADICTION

. Otherwise, set Π( p, a ) ← { v} and re-move v from Π( p′, a ) for all p′̸ = p; if any such set becomes empty, return CONTRADICTION .

Negative update. On σ(p, a )̸ = v: discard v from 

Π( p, a ); if Π( p, a ) = ∅, return CONTRADICTION .

Unit propagation (arc-consistency cascade). After each update, if |Π( p, a )| = 1 for any undetermined (p, a ), that singleton value is propagated as a positive deduction, poten-tially triggering further eliminations. This arc-consistency step may derive eliminations not derivable from any single clue in isolation—the propagator “does the bookkeeping” that the LLM could miss. The propagator returns one of: OK (msg ), CONTRADIC -

> TION

(msg ), or SOLVED . Contradiction messages identify the specific inconsistency, e.g., “assigning Cat to Alice leaves Bob.pet with no valid values.” The propagator runs in 

O(n2k) time per call, which is faster than LLM inference. Note that the propagator guarantees consistency (no contra-diction with prior accepted claims) not entailment : a deduc-tion consistent with the current state but not implied by the clues can still be accepted; the LLM bears responsibility for clue-grounded claims (formal analysis in Appendix F). The propagator guarantees consistency with prior accepted de-ductions, not logical entailment from the clues; a fabricated but locally consistent claim can still be accepted. 

3.3 SymStep: Verified Step-by-Step Reasoning 

Figure 1 illustrates the full pipeline, and Appendix Algo-rithm 1 gives the pseudocode. On each turn, the LLM re-ceives the full interaction history h and produces a single DEDUCE assertion. The propagator checks it, and feedback (confirmation or contradiction) is appended to h before the next LLM call. The LLM never sees an unverified fact ac-cepted into its context, every piece of confirmed informa-tion is consistent.

3.5 Why Per-Step Granularity Matters 

Appendix Table 16 positions SymStep against existing work. Whole-problem approaches (Logic-LM, LINC) per-form a single large translation that is brittle to partial descriptions and natural-language ambiguity. LLM-as-verifier approaches (DVCOTR, Self-Refine) cannot guar-antee soundness. Only SymStep combines deterministic, per-step verification with proactive guidance. 

## 4 Experiments 

In total, SymStep is evaluated on 84 problem instances across five task domains (grid puzzles, scheduling, arith-metic, financial reasoning, and formal constraint): 34 hand-crafted puzzles (LGP-14, SP-6, MWP-8, FIN-6) and 50 problems from two published external benchmarks (Ze-braLogicBench n=35 , AR-LSAT n=15 ). A domain-boundary analysis on 35 AQUA-RAT algebra problems is in Appendix E. All main comparisons are method-vs-method on the same model (Claude Haiku), isolating archi-tectural contribution from model scale; the Sonnet cross-model comparison is in §4.4. Single-run results on each benchmark are cross-validated by the N=3 multi-run ab-lation (§5.2), which confirms zero-variance reproducibility for guided configurations. 

4.1 Benchmark: LGP-14 

A meaningful evaluation of step-level verification requires puzzles that are genuinely hard for unverified methods, with multiple interacting constraints and a unique, checkable so-lution. We construct LGP-14 , a benchmark of fourteen log-ical grid puzzles across three difficulty levels (Appendix Ta-ble 12). All puzzles have a unique solution verified by the constraint propagator; this check caught two originally in-cluded puzzles (E3-Sport and M2-Nationals) that each ad-mitted two valid solutions. Difficulty scales with the num-ber of entities, attributes, and clue density. We additionally provide LGP-20 , which includes six further verified puz-zles (E5–E6, M6–M7, H6–H7) released with this paper for future scale-up evaluation. 

4.2 Methods and Baselines 

SymStep constitutes multiple design paradigms: pure LLM generation, iterative self-improvement, one-shot symbolic program synthesis, and our own step-level verification. To assess the contribution of each component, we compare six methods spanning four design paradigms: 

(1) Zero-shot methods. Direct : a single LLM call that outputs the full assignment directly. CoT [Wei et al. , 2022]: add “think step by step” before answering. Both make ex-actly one LLM call per puzzle. 

(2) Self-improvement without symbolic grounding. Self-Refine [Madaan et al. , 2023]: generate an initial so-lution, then ask the LLM to re-check every clue and revise. This method uses two LLM calls total; the feedback is gen-erated and evaluated by the same LLM, providing no exter-nal correctness guarantee. 

(3) Symbolic-program generation (SOTA). Logic-LM [Pan et al. , 2023]: ask the LLM to write complete Z3 Python code for the puzzle, execute it, and read the solver’s output. ## 6 Analysis and Discussion 

CoT vs. constraint tasks The results show a specific, predictable failure mode. CoT produces a long, uncon-strained narrative: each sentence updates the story with-out checking whether it contradicts what has already been established. On constraint-dense problems like LGP-14, undetected contradictions accumulate faster than a direct guess, introducing more errors, which is why CoT ends up worse than answering directly. This failure is constraint-density-specific : CoT achieves 100% on MWP-8, where arithmetic chain dependencies are simpler and a single wrong number does not produce a tangled web of inconsis-tencies. Hence, as constraint density rises, unverified rea-soning becomes actively harmful. 

Guidance as primary driver, verification as safety net. 

There are two main reasons to include verification, even with guidance. First, the 72% verification-only result shows that verification recover errors that guidance cannot prevent. As puzzles grow harder and assertion errors become more frequent, this safety net becomes essential. Second, Sym-Step’s consistency guarantee is formal: the propagator en-sures that no inconsistent fact ever enters the LLM’s con-text, regardless of model behavior. MRV prevents dead-locks, symbolic verification provides correctness. Neither suffices alone as problems scale. 

SymStep as propagation-guided search. In SymStep, the LLM acts as a natural-language branching oracle while the propagator handles consistency and cascades implied deductions. Neither suffices alone: CoT without propa-gation gives 0% on LGP-14, and a propagator alone can-not extract facts from natural language. The feedback loop (generate, verify, cascade, hint) is more powerful than either component alone. 

Cost and efficiency. SymStep+G uses ≈ 7 LLM calls per problem versus 1 for Direct/CoT, but ≈ 60% of assignments are derived by cascade automatically. At ≈ $0 .0013 /puzzle (Haiku), the full LGP-14 benchmark costs under two cents. The meaningful comparison is cost per correct so-lution : Logic-LM’s single call costs ≈ $0 .0006 /attempt but achieves 0%, making its effective cost per correct answer unbounded. 

## 7 Conclusion 

LLMs mainly fail on constraint-dense reasoning because unverified errors accumulate unchecked. SymStep fixes this by verifying every deduction before the next begins, achiev-ing 83–100% across six benchmarks (LGP-14, SP-6, MWP-8, FIN-6, ZebraLogicBench, AR-LSAT) and outperform-ing Logic-LM, the strongest prior symbolic +LLM base-line; on ZebraLogicBench, a published 1,000-puzzle bench-mark never seen during design, SymStep+G achieves 97%. Wilson CIs confirm significance: SymStep+G’s lower bound strictly exceeds all baselines’ upper bounds on both LGP-14 (79% vs. 22%, n=14 ) and ZebraLogicBench (85% vs. 10%, n=35 ). 

## 8 Limitations and Future Work 

Scaling and model coverage. SymStep has been validated on 119 instances spanning six independent domains and two model families; extending to FOLIO [Han et al. , 2024] and ProofWriter [Tafjord et al. , 2021] is a natural next step, and LGP-20 (20 verified puzzles) is released for this pur-pose. Initial Sonnet results (90% on LGP-10, §B) are en-couraging; a clean cross-model study on GPT-4 [OpenAI 

et al. , 2024], open-weight models [Touvron et al. , 2023], and large reasoning models (o1, DeepSeek-R1 [Guo et al. ,2025]) will clarify whether the gains are model-agnostic. 

Extending the symbolic backbone. The propagator handles bijective constraints—a broad class covering grid puzzles, scheduling, and selection problems—with elegant arc-consistency propagation. Temporal ordering, count-ing, and conditionals are well-studied in constraint pro-gramming; adding SAT/ASP [Gelfond and Lifschitz, 1988] backends would extend SymStep to these domains, and the outer loop and D EDUCE protocol are already backend-agnostic. The SymStep+G regression on SP-6 ( n=6 , not statistically significant) raises an interesting open ques-tion: learned or adaptive guidance [Lightman et al. , 2024] that adjusts hint aggressiveness based on constraint density could close this gap and clarify when MRV direction is ben-eficial versus disruptive.

A Related Work 

Chain-of-thought and multi-step reasoning. The most popular approach for multi-step reasoning with LLMs is chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [Wei et al. , 2022], with extensions ranging from zero-shot variants [Kojima et al. ,2022] and Least-to-Most decomposition [Zhou et al. , 2023] to tree- and graph-structured search [Yao et al. , 2023a; Besta et al. , 2024]. These methods share one common limi-tation: they generate reasoning traces but provide no mech-anism to check whether any given step is factually valid. PAL [Gao et al. , 2023] and Program-of-Thought [Chen et al. , 2023] address arithmetic by offloading computation to Python, but logical deduction at the step level remains un-verified. Other iterative strategies such as ReAct [Yao et al. , 2023b], Self-Ask [Press et al. , 2023], RAP [Hao et al. ,2023], Thought Cloning [Hu and Clune, 2023], and MCTS-guided search [Zhao et al. , 2023] add reasoning loops, yet none introduce step-level symbolic verification. The rich evaluation literature on math reasoning [Cobbe et al. , 2021; Hendrycks et al. , 2021], commonsense tasks [Talmor et al. , 2019; Bhakthavatsalam et al. , 2021; Geva et al. , 2021; Sakaguchi et al. , 2021; Zellers et al. , 2019; Yang et al. ,2018], and multi-task suites [Suzgun et al. , 2022; Srivastava 

et al. , 2022] has revealed that constraint-dense problems are a common bottleneck for these approaches. 

Step-level verification. Several recent works confirm that verifying reasoning as it unfolds is more powerful than checking a completed trace, yet none achieves the determin-istic soundness SymStep introduces. DVCOTR [Ling et al. ,2023] decomposes CoT into “Natural Program” steps that the LLM itself verifies, but the verifier is the same model that made the claim, so errors can go undetected and still accumulate. Faithful CoT [Lyu et al. , 2023] translates a completed CoT trace to a symbolic solver post hoc, but by the time checking occurs, errors have already propa-gated through subsequent steps. Self-Refine [Madaan et al. ,2023] and Reflexion [Shinn et al. , 2023] iterate on LLM self-critique, which produces useful surface-level correc-tions but remains logically ungrounded. 

Neuro-symbolic integration. The neuro-symbolic tradi-tion, spanning tensor product representations [Smolensky, 1990], neural-symbolic cognitive architectures [d’Avila Garcez et al. , 2009], and recent surveys [Wang et al. , 2024; Garcez and Lamb, 2023; Yan et al. , 2025; Sakhovskiy et al. , 2024], aims to combine the flexibility of learned mod-els with the reliability of formal reasoning. Our prop-agator draws directly on classical formal methods: arc-consistency [Mackworth, 1977], description logics [Baader 

et al. , 2003], and first-order resolution [Robinson, 1965]. The two most closely related systems, Logic-LM [Pan et al. , 2023] and LINC [Olausson et al. , 2023], represent the whole-problem end of this spectrum: both ask the LLM to produce a complete formal program (Z3 or FOL) from a natural-language description in one shot, then hand it off to a solver. This brittle translation achieves 0% on LGP-14 and SP-6. Language-to-logic translation has also been ex-plored for temporal logic [Fuggitti and Chakraborti, 2023] and structured knowledge bases [Bollacker et al. , 2008; Berant et al. , 2013]. LLM-Modulo [Kambhampati et al. ,2024] makes the conceptual case for pairing LLMs with external critics; SymStep instantiates this principle at the finest possible granularity—one claim, one check—with a deterministic symbolic propagator. 

Large reasoning models and test-time compute. Recent LRMs (o1 [Valmeekam et al. , 2024], DeepSeek-R1 [Guo 

et al. , 2025]) and test-time compute scaling [Snell et al. ,2024] extend CoT by allocating more inference-time com-putation, yet their scratchpads remain unverified. SymStep is complementary: it provides a sound symbolic oracle that deterministically checks every intermediate claim, indepen-dent of model scale. SymStep is evaluated on Claude Haiku and Sonnet; the broader landscape includes GPT-4 [OpenAI 

et al. , 2024], Gemini [Anil et al. , 2023], and LLaMA [Tou-vron et al. , 2023]. 

Classical planning and constraint satisfaction. Sequen-tial search under constraints underpins classical AI plan-ning: STRIPS [Fikes and Nilsson, 1971], PDDL [Mc-Dermott et al. , 1998], Graphplan [Blum and Furst, 1995], and LAMA [Richter and Westphal, 2010]. Compilabil-ity results [Nebel, 2000] and the situation calculus [Mc-Carthy and Hayes, 1987] formalize constraint semantics. Applied to LLMs, planning benchmarks [Valmeekam et al. , 2023b; Valmeekam et al. , 2023a; Valmeekam et al. ,2024] show consistent failure without symbolic support; LLM +PDDL [Liu et al. , 2023] and generalized plan-ning [Silver et al. , 2024] partially address this. LLMs also power embodied agents [Huang et al. , 2024; Ichter 

et al. , 2023; Wang et al. , 2023a; Wang et al. , 2023b; Song et al. , 2023] on benchmarks such as ALFRED [Shrid-har et al. , 2020], ALFWorld [Shridhar et al. , 2021], and VirtualHome [Puig et al. , 2018]. 

LLM agents, tools, and code. Tool-augmented LLMs [Schick et al. , 2023; Shen et al. , 2023; Cai et al. , 2024] and agent architectures [Sumers et al. , 2024; Liu et al. , 2024; Yang et al. , 2023] extend LLMs with external execution. Code-as-policy [Liang et al. , 2023; Singh et al. , 2023] and code generation [Le et al. , 2022; Li et al. , 2022] show that structured outputs with execution feedback improve reliability. Inner Monologue [Huang 

et al. , 2023] provides environment feedback to the LLM analogously to our propagator; SymStep specializes this feedback loop for logical consistency with a sound verifier.

## F Formal Properties of the Constraint Propagator 

Theorem 1 (Consistency) . Every assignment σ(p, a ) = v

accepted by the propagator is consistent with all previously accepted deductions. The possibility matrix Π satisfies: 

v ∈ Π( p, a ) iff v has not been ruled out by any accepted deduction at any prior step. Proof. By induction on the deduction sequence. Base : Ini-tially, Π( p, a ) = Va; the invariant holds trivially. Step : A P-

PLY returns OK only if v ∈ Π( p, a ), ensuring consistency. Arc-consistency propagation discards v from Π( p′, a ) for all p′̸ = p, maintaining bijectivity. Any empty domain trig-gers CONTRADICTION , so no inconsistent state is ever ac-cepted. 

Theorem 2 (Propagation completeness) . If |Π( p, a )| = 1 

for some (p, a ) after accepting a deduction, the propagator derives this assignment automatically without an additional LLM call. The cascade reaches an arc-consistency fixed point in O(n2k) steps. Proof. Each cascade step strictly reduces the total do-main size P

p,a |Π( p, a )|, which is bounded below by nk ;thus, the cascade terminates. The O(n2k) bound follows from standard arc-consistency analysis [Mackworth, 1977; Hoffmann and Nebel, 2001]. 

Soundness caveat. The propagator guarantees consistency ,not soundness . A consistent-but-incorrect deduction (valid against the prior state but not entailed by the clues) can still be accepted. The residual failure rate on hard puzzles re-flects exactly this gap.
